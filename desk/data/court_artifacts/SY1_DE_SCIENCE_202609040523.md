## SCIENCE BRIEF — SY1.DE (Symrise AG)

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**Established science.** Ethanol in a fermented beverage is not a passive passenger. It is (i) a solvent that holds hydrophobic esters and terpenes in solution, (ii) a carrier of retronasal aroma release, (iii) a trigeminal stimulus (warmth/burn), and (iv) a body/viscosity contributor. Removing it therefore causes four distinct, physically separable losses, and the industry's confusion comes from treating them as one problem.

**What each route actually does, and what it leaves for a flavour house.**

*Route 1 — thermal (vacuum distillation, spinning cone).* Ethanol boils at 78.4 °C but the esters that matter co-boil with it. Under vacuum at ~35 °C you get the alcohol out and take the aroma with it: 96–98% of total esters, 85–95% of higher alcohols, 92–100% of terpenes/C13-norisoprenoids (C1, C2, wine matrix). The industrial answer is **aroma recovery**: condense the stripped volatiles, separate the ethanol-rich condensate from an "aroma-water" fraction, re-dose the aroma-water. TU München measured how well that works in beer (C3): at optimal reflux, 67% of 3-methylbutyl acetate lands in the recoverable aroma-water, but only 3.5% of 3-methylbutanol does. So recovery is **partial and compound-selective** — it returns the fruity acetate esters better than the fusel alcohols that give body. That residual gap is the genuine, physically-grounded addressable space for purchased flavour. It is a gap, not the whole loss.

*Route 2 — membrane (reverse osmosis / nanofiltration).* Runs cold (GEA: <10 °C), so no thermal degradation, and the membrane's selectivity means ester loss is materially lower than thermal (81–92% vs 96–98% in the wine comparison; RO preserved fruity/floral aroma with 4–62% reduction vs 94–99% for VD). GEA sells this as "most of the aromas are retained" (C5 — vendor claim, and it is the vendor's own product page, so a bench should treat it as marketing, but the peer-reviewed direction agrees). **The better the membrane, the less flavour needs buying.** This is the structurally important point: the equipment leg and the chemistry leg are, at the margin, *substitutes*, not complements.

*Route 3 — biological (maltose-negative yeast, cold-contact/arrested fermentation).* No ethanol is made, so nothing is stripped and no dealcoholization equipment is bought at all. The characteristic defect is different in kind: **worty** off-flavour from Strecker aldehydes — 2-methylpropanal, 2-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanal (C6). The 2025 literature's answer is a *microbiological* one: a newly isolated maltose-negative brewer's yeast (M-I) produced more esters and higher alcohols and **fewer** Strecker aldehydes than the *Saccharomycodes ludwigii* reference. A yeast-strain solution displaces both the equipment sale and the flavour sale.

**Where the physical ceiling sits.** Ethanol's trigeminal burn and its solvency cannot be reproduced by any recovered fraction of the beer itself, because ethanol *is* the missing molecule. This is the one loss that is irreducibly a chemistry problem, and it is exactly what Symrise's own materials claim to address — symlife™ "mimicking authentic alcohol burn notes… tingling or cooling effects" (C12). Symrise's cooling/sensate chemistry (Frescolat family) and its bitter-masking flavanones (C9, C10) are real, granted assets that plausibly bear on this. But note the ceiling on the *claim*, not the science: no page found asserts a patent over the NA application (C12, C13).

**Historical failure mode of this class, by name.** The class failure is not scientific, it is commercial: **flavour add-back is the step customers insource.** The recovered aroma-water in Route 1 belongs to the brewer, is produced by the brewer's own plant, and is free at the margin. AB InBev's EP2566944B1 (C7, active to 2031) and Heineken's yeast work (C8) show the largest customers building the NA answer *upstream of the flavour purchase* — in the mash bill (β-amylase inactivation, ≤25% fermentability wort) and in the yeast genome (PAD1/FDC1). A picks-and-shovels thesis requires the shovel-maker to be non-substitutable by the miner. Here the two largest miners have granted patents on not needing the shovel.

**The honest counter-case, stated at equal strength.** NA *spirits* invert the argument completely. There is no parent liquid to recover aroma from — an NA gin is water plus botanical distillates plus body/heat agents, built from zero. Symrise's Vinotec™, Spirit Signatures, Giniper and Fermantarom lines address exactly this (C12), and here the flavour house is not an additive, it is the product. NA spirits is also the smallest of the three no-alcohol sub-categories by volume (IWSR frames no-alcohol *beer* as the absolute-volume leader, C17). So the sub-segment where Symrise's content-per-litre is highest is the sub-segment with the least litres.

## 3. THE FIELD (as of 2026-09-04)

**Equipment layer (bears directly on the G1A.DE sibling, and on whether SY1's need exists).**
- **GEA (G1A.DE)** — AromaPlus / AromaPlus PRO RO membrane dealcoholization, launched 2016, modular <50 hl/batch to ≥100 hl/hr continuous, to 0.5% or 0.05% ABV, <10 °C. Named installations: Laitilan (FI, first in Scandinavia), Adnams Ghost Ship 0.5% (UK), Schönbuch Braumanufaktur (DE, running since May 2018). GEA also sells the *biological*-route hardware (self-cleaning separators) — i.e. GEA is paid on Route 1, 2 **and** 3, which Symrise is not. [gea.com](https://www.gea.com/en/products/membrane-filtration/membrane-plants-solutions/gea-aromaplus-membrane-dealcoholization-unit/)
- **Flavourtech** (private, est. 1987, Australia) — Spinning Cone Column, steam-under-vacuum thin-film; two-column dealcoholization with integrated aroma recovery; Resin Adsorbing Column (RAC) developed specifically for NA beer. [flavourtech.com](https://flavourtech.com/industries/alcoholic-beverages/) — a private OEM that is **not** among the five expressions and that owns the aroma-recovery step.

**Chemistry layer — capability is not scarce.** Every one of the five flavour-house expressions markets a functionally equivalent toolbox, and the naming is near-interchangeable:
- Symrise: symlife™ taste balancing (masking, mouthfeel, sensates, alcohol-burn mimicry), Vinotec™, Fermantarom.
- Kerry (KRZ.ID): **Tastesense™ Sensations** and an explicit "**Spirit absolutes**" technology that "recreates the authentic taste of spirits in low & no alcohol, delivering the same warmth and punch" — the closest published like-for-like to symlife™'s alcohol-burn claim. [kerry.com alcohol taste](https://www.kerry.com/products/taste-ingredients/sweet-flavours-and-extracts/alcohol-taste.html), [Tastesense Sensations](https://www.kerry.com/products/taste-ingredients/taste-modulation/taste-balance-sensation)
- IFF: Tastepoint flavour-trend programme covering 2026 NA/F&B directions. [foodingredientsfirst coverage](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/)

The cross-comparison caveat that matters: **none of these is a matched comparison.** They are marketing taxonomies, not endpoints. There is no published head-to-head sensory trial between symlife™ and Tastesense™ on a common NA base beer with a common panel. Any bench claiming one house's NA chemistry is technically superior to another's would be resting on trademark copy.

**Where a real technical differentiator does exist for Symrise:** SymTrap® (C11) is a granted, expiry-2028 adsorption process for concentrating aroma out of *aqueous* phases with demonstrated 46× linalool enrichment and complete diacetyl removal. That is mechanistically adjacent to dealcoholization aroma-water — the same physical problem (dilute volatiles in a water phase). No Symrise document found applies SymTrap® to beer aroma-water; the marketed applications are fruit/vegetable side streams (Apple, Strawberry, Banana, Maracuja, Cranberry SymTrap®) and perfumery. This is the single most interesting unexploited technical link in the thesis and it is currently **unevidenced** (see §6).

**Demand side — who lacks the capability and has paid for adjacent ones.** Mid-market and craft brewers cannot run aroma-recovery R&D or maintain a strain-development programme; they are the natural buyers of both a GEA skid and a flavour house's finished NA system. The top-4 global brewers can and do build in-house (C7, C8). NA-spirits startups have no brewing plant at all and buy 100% of their flavour. So the flavour-house-addressable customer set is structurally the **mid-market brewer + NA-spirits brand**, not the volume-weighted centre of the NA market.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

**T1 — Wine numbers are not beer numbers.** The headline 81–98% ester-loss figures (C1) are from *wine* dealcoholized to 0.7% v/v. The beer figures (C3) are from a different matrix, different target (0.02% abv), and are reported as *recovery into aroma-water*, not as loss. A bench that quotes "98% of esters are destroyed, therefore flavour must be bought" is importing a wine estimand into a beer argument. Do not transfer.

**T2 — RO-vs-VD comparisons are not at matched conditions.** The published RO/VD comparison runs VD at 35 °C; GEA's commercial RO runs <10 °C; the TUM thermal work varies condenser temperature and reflux rate as the independent variables. "RO preserves aroma better" is directionally supported but the magnitude is condition-specific.

**T3 — Vendor claim vs measured value.** GEA's "most of the aromas are retained" (C5) is a product page. The peer-reviewed measurement (C3) is a lab column. Neither is the other's control. Flag any kill built on GEA's marketing as vendor-sourced.

**T4 — Regulatory thresholds make NA market sizes non-additive across geographies.** US: "non-alcoholic" = <0.5% ABV with a mandatory adjacent numeric statement, **no tolerance** (C19). Other jurisdictions use different cut-offs and different words ("alcohol-free", "de-alcoholized", "0.0"). I verified only the US primary in this pass. Any global NA volume series silently pools products that are not the same regulatory object.

**T5 — IWSR "key markets" ≠ global.** The "~4% by 2027" figure (C17) is a share of total beverage alcohol volume **across key markets**, not a global share and not a share of beverage volume overall. Reading it as "NA is 4% of drinks" overstates by a large factor.

**T6 — Three vendors give three flavour-application shares** (32.7% / 33.88% / 35.92%, C18) on different base-year and different market definitions ("food flavors" vs "flavors & fragrances" vs "beverage flavoring systems", the last of which ranges from US$9.1bn to US$26bn across vendors). Shown, not averaged.

**T7 — Symrise's reported vs organic growth carries a 2–3pp FX wedge.** Derived from company figures (C15): H1 2026 group reported sales 1,530 + 1,009 = €2,539m against 1,527 + 1,026 = €2,553m prior — **−0.5% reported** — while TNH organic was +3.2% and Scent & Care ~0%. Comparing SY1.DE reported growth against GIVN.SW (CHF), IFF (USD) or KRZ.ID (EUR) reported growth mixes four currency bases. Each company also defines "organic" differently with respect to portfolio and price/mix.

**T8 — Segment ≠ division ≠ business unit.** TNH (€3,028m) contains both Food & Beverage and Pet Food. Pet Food's growth profile diverged sharply from F&B in 2025–26 ("slight organic sales decline" in Q2 2026 vs F&B "market-leading mid-single-digit"). Any inference about beverage momentum from the TNH line is contaminated by pet food. Segment-level flooring hides this mix.

**T9 — The Beverages BU growth quotes are qualitative and moving.** FY2025: "high single-digit"; H1 2025: "double-digit"; H1 2026: not mentioned at all, and an analyst had to ask (C16). A deceleration is *consistent with* the record but is not stated by the company. Do not read the silence as a number in either direction.

**T10 — "NA-exposed revenue" has no company definition.** There is no line item, no estimand, and no company statement of what would count. Any figure a bench uses is an analyst construct; §5's arithmetic below is labelled as such.
