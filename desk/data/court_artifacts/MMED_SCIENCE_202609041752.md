## SCIENCE BRIEF — MMED

**The question the case turns on:** MiniMed's two physically critical consumables — the subcutaneous *insulin delivery interface* (infusion set) and the *glucose/ketone sensing interface* — are both manufactured by third parties (Convatec/Unomedical and Abbott respectively), and both are under active regulatory events. Everything technical reduces to: how much of MiniMed's clinical performance and supply continuity is under its own engineering control, and how much sits inside two suppliers it does not own.

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**Established science — the delivery interface.** CSII delivers rapid-acting analog insulin through a soft (Teflon) or steel cannula into subcutaneous tissue. Three physics failure modes dominate, all of them class-inherent, none of them proprietary to MiniMed:

1. **Occlusion / silent occlusion.** Insulin aggregates and fibrin/tissue reaction at the cannula tip progressively restrict flow. Basal rates in AID are low enough (often <1 U/hr) that pressure-based occlusion detectors need hours to trip — hence "silent." The user's first signal is hyperglycemia, not an alarm.
2. **Leak at the connector/tubing interface.** Insulin escapes before reaching tissue. This produces *under-delivery without an alarm at all* — pressure downstream is normal because the fluid path is open, just open to the wrong place. This is precisely the mode the FDA letter documents (>5,000 leak complaints; 210 Varisoft tubing-detachment complaints producing >80 hyperglycemia reports).
3. **Site inflammation limiting wear time.** The historical 2–3 day set-change interval exists because of subcutaneous inflammatory response and insulin-stability degradation at body temperature, not because of the hardware.

The clinical consequence chain is short and well characterized: under-delivery → no circulating insulin → unrestrained lipolysis → ketogenesis → DKA. Because pump users have *no* subcutaneous depot of long-acting insulin, the time from delivery failure to ketosis is on the order of 4–6 hours — far shorter than for MDI patients. This is why an infusion-set leak is a materially different hazard from a CGM inaccuracy, and why continuous ketone sensing is mechanistically the correct compensating control.

**The novel-engineering claim — Extended Wear.** MiniMed's Extended Wear Infusion Set attacks failure mode (1) and (3) via a tubing connector designed to improve physical and chemical insulin stability, extending labeled wear to 7 days. The pivotal data bound the claim honestly: **83.8% survival at day 6, 77.8% at day 7** ([PMID 35263188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35263188/)) — i.e., roughly one in five sets does not reach labeled wear. Failure specifically attributable to unexplained hyperglycemia was low (0.1–0.4%). The physical ceiling here is tissue biology, not materials: no connector chemistry removes the inflammatory limit at the cannula tip.

Note the interaction the FDA letter creates: the *Extended Wear* set is the one for which the letter says Unomedical's own risk assessment rated potential harm "life threatening" and for which design verification was found lacking. The product carrying MiniMed's differentiated wear-time claim is the product with the most specific validation finding against it.

**Established science — the sensing interface.** Both Abbott (Instinct, Libre Duo) and Medtronic (Simplera) use subcutaneous amperometric glucose-oxidase electrodes; interstitial fluid lags plasma glucose by roughly 5–15 minutes, and accuracy degrades during rapid rate-of-change. Libre Duo adds a second working electrode using β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase chemistry to read ketones on the same filament. That is an incremental extension of an established platform, not a new physical principle — which is consistent with FDA granting it via De Novo (novel *device type*, new special controls) rather than treating it as a novel risk class.

**The closed-loop layer.** SmartGuard now holds an **iAGC** clearance, which is the structural point: FDA has decoupled the controller from the sensor. This is what lets Abbott sensors drive MiniMed pumps — and it is also what lets *any* compliant sensor drive them, and MiniMed's controller drive nothing that Abbott does not supply.

**Class failure modes, by name.** The historical failures in this class are informative and mostly implementation-specific: MiniMed's own **670G** hybrid closed loop was abandoned by users at high rates because of frequent auto-mode exits driven by sensor calibration demands — an algorithm/sensor-trust failure, not a control-theory failure. **Unomedical's October 2023 Varisoft recall** was tagged Class I and, per the FDA letter, came ~11 months after the firm knew of the defect — a complaint-handling failure, not a design-physics failure. Vivera's design directly targets the 670G lesson: elimination of SmartGuard exits, optional meal announcement, no 48-hour warm-up, targets from 90–140 mg/dL.

Abbott has monetized this by making Libre Duo available *both* direct-to-consumer and to at least five pump partners; MiniMed's contracted position is an exclusive *MiniMed-integrated variant*, not exclusivity over the analyte.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

Enumerated before the benches build kills on them.

1. **MARD is comparator-defined, not device-defined.** The same three sensors rank identically vs YSI and very differently vs capillary Contour Next (Simplera 16.6% vs Libre 11.6%→9.7%). Any bench comparing a Medtronic-cited MARD to an Abbott- or Dexcom-cited MARD from separate studies is comparing comparator methods, not sensors. Only the Eichenlaub simultaneous-wear design is method-matched.
2. **Vendor MARD figures (~8.5% / ~9%) have no primary I could locate.** Do not place them alongside the peer-reviewed 11.6–16.6% range as if commensurable.
3. **Single-arm vs randomized.** IMPACT2D (780G in T2D) is **single-arm** against a run-in baseline; Tandem's CIRCUIT and Insulet's STRIVE are controlled designs. A "+8.6% TIR" from a run-in comparison is not the same estimand as a between-arm difference.
4. **AID TIR is target-setting dependent.** 780G supports a 100 mg/dL target (Vivera extends to 90); Control-IQ's standard target band is higher. TIR (70–180) comparisons across systems partly measure how aggressively each system was configured, not algorithm quality. Time-in-tight-range (70–140) is the less confounded metric and is reported inconsistently.
5. **Sensor "wear time" has two definitions.** Labeled wear (15 d Instinct, 10 d Libre Duo, 7 d Simplera) is not achieved wear. Abbott printed the honest version for Libre Duo: 84.1% adult / 68.8% pediatric sensors reached day 10. Infusion sets have the same split (labeled 7 d vs 77.8% survival). Comparing one product's *label* to another's *survival* is the error to avoid.
6. **MAUDE counts measure reporting behavior at least as much as device behavior.** The Unomedical MDR series (219 → 8,764 → 8,159 → 30,627 in seven months of 2026) rises immediately after a warning letter citing **21 CFR 803.50 late-reporting violations and 41,000+ complaints closed without investigation**. The mechanically expected consequence of that letter is a retrospective reporting catch-up. Two further caveats: openFDA date-histogram counts are bucket-limited and early-year totals may be incomplete; and MDR counts are un-normalized by installed base. Additionally, the brand mix inside the Unomedical slice (Inset Guard 26.6k, AutoSoft XC 18.2k, Inset 16.5k, **Quick Set 15.9k**, Inset II 13.9k) mixes Tandem- and MiniMed-branded sets with different installed bases, and reports for the same product may be filed by either the manufacturer or the specification developer — the mix is not a failure-rate comparison.
7. **MiniMed's Q1 FY2027 contained an extra week.** The company itself quantifies it at ~1.0–1.5% of full-year growth. The +16.6% headline and the thesis's "TTM $3,102M +14.3%" are 14-week-inflated; peers report 13-week calendar quarters.
8. **Fiscal-calendar mismatch.** MiniMed's fiscal year ends in late April and Q1 FY2027 ended **July 31, 2026**; Insulet, Tandem, Dexcom and Abbott report calendar quarters. "Q1 2027" for MMED overlaps peers' Q2 2026. Any sequential or YoY cross-read must be date-aligned first.
9. **"Exclusive" is doing two different jobs.** Abbott's exclusivity to MiniMed covers *a MiniMed-integrated sensor variant*. Abbott simultaneously sells Libre Duo direct and is integrating it with Beta Bionics, Sequel, Insulet and Tandem. Reading "exclusive" as "MiniMed is Abbott's only ketone partner" inverts the fact pattern.
10. **Supplier revenue and customer revenue are on different planes.** Convatec Infusion Care H1 2026 revenue was $246M across *all* customers including non-diabetes (AbbVie, Supernus, Tanabe, >15% of IC); MiniMed consumables were $261M in a single quarter at reimbursed end-user prices. These are manufacturer transfer price vs downstream realized price and cannot be netted or ratioed.
11. **Ketone alarm thresholds are not blood-BHB equivalents.** Libre Duo's Urgent High Ketones alarm fires >3.0 mmol/L on interstitial fluid; clinical DKA thresholds are defined on blood BHB. FDA's own language — "ketone information should always be interpreted in the context of glucose readings and symptoms" — is the caveat, and FDA published no MARD-equivalent ketone agreement statistic.
12. **The warning letter date has two circulating values.** The letter is dated **2026-01-08** (FDA); Convatec's disclosure is dated **2026-02-03**, and several outlets report the later date as the letter date. The thesis's "1/8/26" matches the primary.
