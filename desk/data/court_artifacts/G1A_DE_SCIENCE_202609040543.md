## SCIENCE BRIEF — G1A.DE (GEA Group AG)

**The technical question the case turns on:** whether removing ethanol from beer is a *proprietary chemistry* GEA owns and gets paid for on every NA launch — or a **commodity unit operation on expired 1990s membrane IP that a large and growing share of NA launches bypasses entirely** by never making the ethanol in the first place. Everything downstream (materiality, moat, fillability) rests on that fork, plus a second one: whether reverse osmosis — GEA's chosen route — can physically and economically reach the 0.0% (≤0.05% ABV) label that the premium end of the category is being built on.

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**Established science — why ethanol removal is intrinsically destructive.** Ethanol (MW 46, bp 78 °C) is not chemically exotic relative to the congeners that make beer taste like beer. Every separation route exploits either size/solubility (membrane) or volatility (thermal), and on both axes the ethanol overlaps heavily with the esters (ethyl acetate, isoamyl/3-methylbutyl acetate, ethyl octanoate), higher alcohols and terpenics that carry fruity/floral character. That overlap is **class-inherent thermodynamics, not an implementation defect**: C1 and C2 show 87–92% ester loss even on the *gentler* route (RO), 96–98% on vacuum distillation. There is no membrane and no column that removes ethanol and leaves isoamyl acetate behind at scale. The consequence is structural and matters for the thesis's own framing: **every physical dealcoholization route creates a downstream flavour-reconstruction problem**, which is why Heineken states plainly that it "brings back these characteristics with our premium quality flavor" (D1). The value that dealcoholization destroys is re-created by the *flavour* layer, not by the equipment layer.

**Where the physical ceiling sits for GEA's chosen route.** RO removes ethanol by permeating it (with water) through a thin-film composite spiral-wound membrane at ~10–15 °C and 10–30 bar while rejecting the larger aroma/colour/turbidity fraction. As the retentate's ethanol falls, the driving force falls with it and osmotic pressure rises — ethanol flux "extremely low and continuously decreasing" (C3). The only fix is **diafiltration: adding water back to keep the gradient alive**. That is the whole game. It is why the last decade of the product's development has been about water (A3: "80% or more" less diafiltration draw), why competitors claim RO "cannot produce 0.0 beers cost-effectively" (C4), and why the honest characterisation of AromaPlus PRO is not "a better membrane" but **"a two-stage recycle loop that makes an old membrane process cheaper on water."** Whether that is enough to be cost-competitive at 0.05% ABV against a stripping column is the single most decisive unresolved technical fact in this brief (see §6).

**GEA's novel claim vs. the commodity base.** The commodity base is complete: the two- and three-step RO dealcoholization process was patented by **Dow Deutschland in 1990–92 and is long expired** (C5). The membranes are described by GEA itself as thin-film composite spiral-wound polymer — the industry-standard construction; the bench literature runs the same experiment on an **Alfa Laval** RO99 membrane (C3). No GEA-assigned NA-specific patent surfaced (C6, tagged as a negative search, not a proof). What GEA appears to own is **integration and skid engineering** — frame-mounted, prewired, CIP-integrated, touchscreen, retrofittable — plus brand and service reach. That is a real but *replicable* asset; it is the kind of moat that shows up in service attach rates and lead times, not in exclusion.

**Historical failure modes, named, and whether they are class-inherent:**
- **Worty off-flavour** (2-/3-methylbutanal, unfermented maltose) — the defining defect of the *biological* route (arrested fermentation, maltose-negative yeast). **Class-inherent to biological**, and the reason physical dealcoholization existed at all (C8).
- **Ester/higher-alcohol stripping and body loss** — the defining defect of the *physical* route. **Class-inherent** (C1, C2), mitigated but never eliminated by aroma recovery (Alfa Laval's own numbers: ~50% aroma recovery at <0.5%, collapsing to ~25% at 0.0 — C4).
- **Microbiological fragility** — removing ethanol removes a hurdle; NA beer needs ~4× the pasteurization units and shows 2–5× spoilage incidence (C9). **Class-inherent to the product category, not to any route** — and it is the mechanism by which an NA line pulls through separators, sterile filtration and aseptic filling regardless of which dealcoholization technology (if any) is chosen.
- **Aroma-recovery recursion at 0.0** — recovered aroma condensate is itself alcoholic and cannot be added back below 0.05% ABV without a resin adsorption step (C11). **Class-inherent to thermal routes**; it is a genuine RO advantage that RO's retained aroma never leaves the retentate.

**The mechanism that most threatens the "picks and shovels" premise.** The Crabtree-negative yeast route (C7) does not arrest fermentation and does not remove alcohol — it *never makes ethanol*, metabolising only monosaccharides under maintained aerobic conditions. Steinecker/Novonesis report 0.0% in two days in a **recirculation unit**, not a dealcoholization plant. If that route industrialises, the NA launch still buys equipment — but it buys a **fermentation/recirculation and oxygen-control** package (and, per C9, more pasteurization and aseptic filling), and it buys **zero** dealcoholization units. The thesis's load-bearing sentence — "every retrofit pays regardless of which brand wins" — is technically conditional on the *route*, not on the brand. Note also A9: GEA sells the biological route's hardware too, so route substitution is not equivalent to GEA losing the customer — but it does move the sale out of the dealcoholization product line and (per B5) between divisions with 11.4% and 26.7% EBITDA margins.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

**T1 — Wine ≠ beer in the RO-vs-VD comparison.** C2's 87–92% vs 96–98% ester numbers are **wine**, dealcoholized to **0.7% v/v**, with an **8-panellist** sensory panel, on Chardonnay/Pinot Noir/Merlot; the authors themselves caveat that high-terpene varieties may behave differently. Do not carry that ratio into a beer kill without saying it is a cross-matrix transfer.

**T2 — "0.0" is three different numbers.** ≤0.05% ABV (the 0.0 label, and Alfa Laval's stated definition), ≤0.5% ABV ("alcohol-free" in the EU and Germany), and 0.01–0.03% ABV (what stripping columns are actually asked to hit). C3's "RO failed to reach 0.5%" is a **no-diafiltration bench result**; A1's "0.05%" is a **with-diafiltration commercial claim**. These are not the same estimand and neither refutes the other.

**T3 — Aroma recovery percentages are not comparable across routes.** Alfa Laval's "50% / 25%" (C4) is *recovered-and-added-back* aroma on a thermal route. GEA's "retains just about all of the flavours" (A3) is *never-removed* aroma on a membrane route. One is a recovery yield, the other a rejection rate. They cannot be differenced.

**T4 — Opex per hl excludes the diafiltration water.** Alfa Laval publishes 2.8–3.8 EUR/hl for stripping (C4). GEA publishes **no** EUR/hl at all — only *relative* water and energy savings versus its own prior generation (A3). A bench that compares "80% less water" to "2.8 EUR/hl" is comparing a delta to a level.

**T5 — Division margins are pro forma and the product mapping is inferred.** B4's divisional numbers are explicitly labelled pro forma on GEA's own slide, restated onto a structure that only took effect 1 Jan 2026. B5's placement of brewery systems in Nutrition Plant Engineering is read off GEA's own portfolio text, **not** off a segment note that names AromaPlus. Do not build a margin kill on the assumption that the dealcoholization revenue definitely sits in the 11.4% division without checking the FY2026 segment note.

**T6 — "Beverage 12%" is a customer-industry cut, not a segment.** It spans beer, soft drinks, coffee, juice, wine and spirits; it is disclosed as a pie chart in an IR factsheet, not as an audited segment; and it is unchanged 2023→2025 (B2/B3), which means it carries no information about NA specifically. There is **no NA-revenue line, no brewing line, and no order-intake split by application anywhere in GEA's public disclosure.**

**T7 — GEA's own market claims are 7 years stale.** A8: GEA's NA page still cites Euromonitor 2013–2018. A bench quoting GEA's growth figures would be quoting pre-pandemic data as current.

**T8 — A6 is unfalsifiable.** "Every second liter of beer brewed with the aid of GEA systems" has no stated denominator, no methodology, and an elastic verb ("with the aid of"). Treat it as marketing reach, not installed-base share, and do not convert it into an NA attach rate.

**T9 — The used-equipment price (E3) is a broker listing.** It is not GEA's list price, the spec is not verified, and used capital-equipment listings are notoriously unrepresentative of new-order ASPs. Any per-unit revenue arithmetic built on $450k inherits that.
