# SCIENCE BRIEF — GIVN.SW

**The technical question the case turns on:** removing ethanol from beer destroys a specific, enumerable set of aroma and mouthfeel chemistry — but the equipment already recovers 25–50% of it internally, the largest single NA-beer market legally forbids adding flavourings back, and the largest NA brands close the gap by *blending* rather than by *dosing*. So the load-bearing question is not "does dealcoholization create a flavour deficit" (it demonstrably does) but **what fraction of that deficit is closed by a purchased flavour-house system versus by the equipment vendor's aroma-recovery loop, the brewer's own yeast/blend R&D, or a legal prohibition on dosing at all.**

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**What ethanol actually does, and therefore what is lost.** Ethanol in beer is not an inert passenger. It is (a) a co-solvent that governs the liquid→headspace partitioning of every hydrophobic volatile, so its removal changes the *release* of compounds that are still physically present; (b) a direct contributor to perceived body, warmth and sweetness; and (c) a partial masker of wort-derived carbonyls. The consequence is that a beer stripped to 0.0 is worse than a beer that simply lacks aroma molecules — the aroma molecules that survive are perceived *differently*, and the off-notes that were previously suppressed become salient. Claim 14 is the compact statement of this: reduce the higher alcohols and the worty aroma gets *louder*, without any new worty compound being formed.

**The deficit, compound class by compound class.** Esters (ethyl acetate, isoamyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate/octanoate) carry the fruity top note and have thresholds below 1 ppm (#15) — they are the first thing any thermal or vacuum route takes out, because their volatility is what the process is exploiting. Higher alcohols (isobutanol, isoamyl alcohol, 2-phenylethanol) carry body and masking. Strecker aldehydes (#13) are the antagonist: 2-/3-methylbutanal and 2-methylpropanal, at µg/L, generate the "worty" off-flavour that is the signature defect of the entire category. In full-strength brewing these are reduced enzymatically by yeast during a complete fermentation; every AFB route either truncates that reduction (biological routes) or leaves it intact but strips the compounds that masked the residue (physical routes). This is why the two route families fail in *opposite* directions (#16): restricted fermentation is worty and sweet; physical dealcoholization is thin and sour.

**Established science vs. novel claim.** Everything above is established and independently replicated. What is *not* established at primary is any flavour house's claim to a differentiated solution to it. Givaudan's public beer page (#18) describes a capability list — alcohol impression, mouthfeel, foam, hop aroma, off-note reduction — with **no named platform, no trademark, and no number**. Its one patent that touches the alcohol sensation directly (#19) works in the wrong direction for this thesis: it *enhances* ethanol's taste sensation when ethanol is present. The visible NA-beer patent activity (#20) sits with brewers — Kirin, Asahi, Heineken, AB InBev — filing on volatile-control chemistry (myrcene, linalool, geraniol, α-eudesmol) that a flavour house would otherwise be selling them.

**The physical ceiling, and where the flavour house's opening actually is.** Claim 8 is the quantitative statement of the addressable gap: the equipment recovers roughly **50% of aromas for a <0.5% ABV product and roughly 25% for a true 0.0**. Two readings follow and a bench should hold both. (i) The optimistic reading: 50–75% of the aroma is *not* recovered and must come from somewhere — that "somewhere" is the flavour house. (ii) The constraining reading: the missing fraction is closed by three non-flavour-house mechanisms before a dosed flavour system is reached — **blending** (Heineken's double-brew, #21: a second brew supplies aroma without a purchased flavour), **hop products** (post-fermentation hop oils/extracts from Barth-Haas, Yakima Chief and similar — a different supplier industry entirely), and **yeast selection** (#17: maltose-negative strains sold by Lallemand, Chr. Hansen/Novonesis and academic isolates, which build esters rather than restoring them). The flavour house's genuine, defensible territory is the residual: bitterness/astringency masking, mouthfeel bulking (glycerol, maltodextrins, hydrocolloids), and the "alcohol impression" trigeminal note — real work, but the *last* increment, not the whole gap.

**Historical failure modes of this class, by name.** (a) *Thermal aroma destruction* — the reason Clausthaler-era vacuum-evaporation products were flat; **implementation-specific**, largely solved by moving to 30–34 °C SCC (#4) and 10–15 °C RO (#6). (b) *The worty defect* of restricted fermentation (#10, #16) — **class-inherent** to the biological route; it cannot be engineered away, only masked or avoided by switching route. (c) *The ABV floor of membranes* (#5) — the peer-reviewed review says RO rarely goes below 0.45% ABV while two equipment vendors claim 0.05% and 0.01%; this is a live, unresolved contradiction and is treated as such in §4. (d) *Ester loss under vacuum* (#15) — **class-inherent** to every thermal/vacuum route; the industry's answer is the recovery-and-recombination loop, which is captured by the *equipment* vendor's flowsheet, not by a flavour supplier.

## 3. THE FIELD (as of today)

**Dealcoholization equipment.** Three distinct technology families with different owners, and — a correction to the upstream framing — **no single one of the five expressions spans them**:
- **Membrane / RO:** GEA (AromaPlus, AromaPlus PRO — a two-stage RO cutting diafiltration fresh-water draw by "potentially 80% or more"), Alfa Laval, plus DeAlcoTec, Banke, SCHULZ, Reda. GEA's installed references are named and European: Laitilan (FI), Schönbuch (DE), Kaiserdom (Bamberg, DE).
- **Vacuum stripping / distillation:** Alfa Laval Dealcoholization Module (≤45 °C, 0.01% ABV floor), plus the classic falling-film evaporator suppliers.
- **Spinning cone column:** **Flavourtech** (SCC + Resin Adsorbing Column) and **BevZero** (three SCC columns, plus contract dealcoholization services and its ClearAlc equipment line). **Neither is one of the five, and GEA does not sell an SCC.** A bench arguing "G1A owns the equipment leg" should note it owns *one* of three routes.

**Flavour houses.** Givaudan, IFF, Symrise, Kerry — and **dsm-firmenich**, which the thesis's five omits despite being the #2 house globally and independently active on alcohol-sensation molecules for NA. The five-name universe is not the competitive set; any share-of-a-pool arithmetic done on four flavour houses will over-allocate.

**Demand side — who lacks the capability and has paid for adjacent ones.** The relevant asymmetry: the largest NA-beer volumes belong to brewers with the deepest in-house flavour R&D (Heineken, AB InBev, Asahi, Kirin, Carlsberg), and their NA patent estates (#20) show them building this internally. The buyers who genuinely lack the capability are mid-size and craft brewers — precisely the segment whose *equipment* purchase (a €0.45m used RO skid at the low end, per an equipment-resale listing) is the larger and more visible line item, and whose flavour spend per hL is smallest.

**Cross-comparison caveat, stated up front.** Claims #6 (GEA, RO, 0.05%), #7 (Alfa Laval, vacuum stripping, 0.01%) and #5 (peer review, RO, 0.45% floor) are **not matched on technology**: only #5 and #6 are both RO and therefore genuinely in conflict. #7 is a different unit operation and does not adjudicate the RO question. Do not stack them.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

**Trap 1 — Four of five disclose nothing at the level the thesis requires.** Givaudan (#24), Symrise (#28), IFF (#29) and GEA (#30) publish **no beverage revenue line at all**, let alone an NA line. Givaudan breaks T&W out by *geography only*. Any "NA-exposed revenue as % of group" figure for GIVN, SY1, IFF or G1A is an estimate, not a disclosure. **Kerry is the sole exception** (#27), disclosing Beverage at €922.9m / 27.7% of group and naming low/no alcohol in a statutory filing. A bench that treats the five symmetrically on disclosure will be wrong in Kerry's favour by construction.

**Trap 2 — The circulating Givaudan segment split is internally inconsistent.** The 37/36/15/12 split (#26) uses four categories to sum to 100% when the company itself lists seven. It is vendor-sourced and unconfirmed at primary. **Do not build a "beverages = 36% of T&W" kill or defence on it.**

**Trap 3 — "IFF Nourish" no longer exists.** The upstream thesis names the Nourish segment. As of Q1/Q2 2026 IFF reports Taste / Health & Biosciences / Scent, with Food Ingredients held for sale to CVC (#29). Any bench arithmetic on Nourish is arithmetic on a segment that has been dissolved, and the pending divestiture changes the denominator again on close (guided end-Q2 2027).

**Trap 4 — An unresolved €651m gap in GEA's divisional arithmetic.** The five FY2025 divisional revenues I retrieved (LPT €1,893.8m + SFT €1,674.1m + FHT €1,108.8m + FT €867.6m + HRT €606.6m) sum to **€6,150.9m** against a stated group revenue of **€5.5bn**. I could not resolve this — it is plausibly intersegment elimination, or one or more of the figures may be order intake rather than revenue. **Until it is resolved from the Annual Report segment note, "LPT = 34% of GEA" is not a verified fact.** GEA also reorganised into these five divisions recently, so pre-reorganisation comparatives are not on the same basis.

**Trap 5 — FX bases are not comparable and none is the reporting currency of the demand.** Givaudan reports CHF, Symrise/Kerry/GEA EUR, IFF USD, while NA-beer volume growth is concentrated in EUR and EM currencies. Givaudan's H1 2026 illustrates the size of the distortion precisely: T&W was **+0.5% LFL but −6.3% in CHF** — a 6.8pp translation wedge on a single line. Comparing Givaudan's reported growth to Kerry's or IFF's without converting to a common basis will manufacture a spurious divergence roughly the size of any plausible NA effect.

**Trap 6 — Aroma-recovery percentages are not on a common estimand.** "50% of aromas" (#8) is a vendor statement with no stated compound basis — 50% by mass? by number of compounds? by odour-activity value? A 50% mass recovery that loses the sub-1-ppm esters (#15) is sensorially near-total loss; a 50% OAV recovery is not. **This single ambiguity determines whether the flavour-house opening is large or small, and it is unresolved.**

**Trap 7 — "0.0" and "<0.5%" are different products with different chemistry and different regulatory status.** Recovery is ~25% vs ~50% (#8); US labelling permits "non-alcoholic" for <0.5% but reserves "alcohol free" for zero (#22); and the class designation "beer" is legally unavailable below 0.5% ABV in the US. Volume statistics that pool the two overstate the true-0.0 segment, which is the segment with the large flavour deficit.

**Trap 8 — The German market, which is the largest and most-cited NA-beer market, may be the least addressable.** If #23 holds at statute, NA beer sold as *Bier* in Germany cannot carry added flavourings. Germany at >10% NA share of retail turnover (#32) is the headline datapoint for category growth **and simultaneously a carve-out from the flavour-house TAM**. Using German share as evidence of flavour-house opportunity inverts the mechanism.

**Trap 9 — Volume-share statistics are vendor and use a servings/volume estimand, not revenue.** IWSR's +8% (#31) is volume; flavour-house revenue scales with *dosed litres × dose rate × price/kg*, and #31's own premiumisation datapoint (29% premium-plus) tells you price per litre is moving faster than volume — the two must not be used interchangeably.

**Trap 10 — Estimating the unaddressable blank rather than leaving it (doctrine).** None of the five discloses NA revenue, so here is the chain, explicitly labelled **SCIENCE-BENCH ESTIMATE, not verified**, so benches argue over a stated basis rather than inventing one:

*NA-beer flavour pool:* global beer volume ~1.85–1.90bn hL (vendor, unverified here) × no-alcohol beer share **4–7%** (basis: Germany >10% of retail turnover, most markets far lower; p≈0.6) = **75–130m hL**. Flavour/mouthfeel system dosage in NA beer **0.02–0.15% w/w** at **€15–60/kg** → **€0.3–9/hL**, central **€1–3/hL** (p≈0.5; range deliberately wide because German-market volume may be legally excluded and blend-based products dose nothing). Pool across *all* flavour houses = **€40–390m/yr**, central ~**€130m**. Givaudan at ~25% share → **€10–100m**, central ~**€33m** = **0.13–1.3% of group sales** (CHF 7,472m ≈ €7.9bn), central ≈ **0.4%**.

*The correction that matters:* NA **spirits, wine and RTDs** carry vastly higher flavour content per litre — an NA spirit is close to a pure flavour-house product (dosage 0.5–3% at €50–200/kg → order **€50–600/hL**) on much smaller volume. **If a material NA flavour revenue exists at any of the five, the chemistry says it is in NA spirits/wine, not NA beer.** A bench that courts only the beer leg will be testing the wrong half of the category.

*Equipment leg:* opex **€2.8–3.8/hL** (#9) accrues to the brewer, not to GEA. GEA's take is the project sale plus membrane/CIP aftermarket. Membrane replacement cadence — the only recurring element, and therefore the only thing that would make this a toll rather than a one-time retrofit — is **not disclosed** (see §6).
