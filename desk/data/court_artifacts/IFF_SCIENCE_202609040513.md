## SCIENCE BRIEF — IFF

**The technical question the case turns on:** removing ethanol from beer breaks three things — ethyl-ester formation, aldehyde masking, and body/trigeminal "warmth" — and each break can be repaired either with a *purchased molecule/enzyme* (flavour-house revenue), a *capital process* (equipment revenue), or a *brewer-owned strain or process patent* (neither). The brief below establishes which repair route each break actually takes today, and who holds the primary-verifiable IP on each.

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**Established science.** Ethanol at 3–12 % v/v is not a passive passenger in beer; it is a functional component with three separable roles, and removing it breaks each one by a different mechanism.

**(i) The ester deficit is stoichiometric, not a process defect.** Beer's principal aroma class — esters — is formed by biosynthesis through acetyl-CoA synthetase and alcohol acyltransferase acting on **acetyl-CoA plus alcohols** (C3, Muller Table 1). Ethyl esters therefore require ethanol as a substrate. Restrict alcohol formation and you restrict ester formation by the same arithmetic. Muller states it directly: under restricted alcohol formation "there is insufficient formation of esters which impairs the final aroma of the beer." This is **class-inherent**, not implementation-specific, for any biological route that suppresses ethanol. It is the reason the biological route has *always* needed something added back, and the reason Chr. Hansen's patented workaround (C16) targets **isoamyl acetate** — an *acetate* ester requiring isoamyl alcohol and acetyl-CoA rather than ethanol. That is a chemistry-aware escape from the constraint, and it is patented by a culture supplier, not a flavour house.

**(ii) The worty defect is a partition-coefficient effect that switches on at the legal line.** Strecker aldehydes — methylpropanal, 2- and 3-methylbutanal, methional, phenylacetaldehyde — are formed by Maillard chemistry during malt kilning, mashing and wort boiling and are present in *all* beer. They are not perceived in full-strength beer because ethanol retains them in the liquid phase. Perpète & Collin's finding, as reported in the review, is that **ethanol promotes retention of these compounds at considerable levels only above 0.5 % v/v** (C2). Below that, they partition into the headspace and are perceived at thresholds under 1 µg/L (C5). This is why the worty problem appears abruptly at, not gradually toward, the 0.5 % ABV boundary — and why a regulatory change in that boundary (C31) is a *technical* variable, not merely a labelling one: moving the UK ceiling from 0.05 % to 0.5 % moves products from the side of the line where masking fails to the side where it works.

**(iii) The trigeminal deficit has no ingredient in ordinary beer to replace.** Ethanol's warmth is TRPV1 agonism plus sensitisation — the heat-activation threshold drops from 42 °C to 34 °C, i.e. mouth temperature (C4). Nothing else in the beer matrix does this. This is the one break that *cannot* be repaired by process engineering at all; it can only be repaired by adding a molecule that hits the same channel. That is precisely what the two 2026 patent filings do — dihydrochalcones on TRPA1/TRPV1/TRPM8 (C17) and piperine + nicotinic acid (C18).

**Where the physical ceiling sits.** On the physical-removal side, the ceiling is selectivity. Membranes "are not selective enough to prevent other small compounds from permeating along with ethanol"; thermal routes co-distil anything with volatility at or below ethanol's. Ethanol (MW 46) sits inside the aroma molecular-weight band, so no separation that removes ethanol cleanly leaves the low-MW aroma fraction intact — the RO/nanofiltration cut-off diagram in the review places "molecules (alcohol)" and "ions" in the same rejection window. The two engineering answers are (a) capture the aroma fraction *first* and recombine it (vacuum distillation with a flavour stripper and recombination tank; the SCC's two-stage capture-then-strip; pervaporation aroma recovery) or (b) run cold enough that the aroma fraction is not mobilised (RO at 7–15 °C). Vendors on route (b) claim no restoration is needed (C9); the peer-reviewed position is that membranes still cause "significant loss of aroma compounds of low molecular weight." **These are in direct conflict and are not reconciled by any source located.** Show both.

**Named historical failure modes.** US Prohibition-era "near beer" (1920–1933, ≤0.5 % ABV, malt-extract based) is the named ancestor of the category and is described as a defect-driven product (C1 §1.2). Interrupted fermentation, "widely used" through the late 20th century, is judged in the same review to be **no longer competitive organoleptically** without severe technology change (C7) — and its historical fix was explicitly the addition of bought flavourings to paper over specific defects. That is a class-inherent failure of the *cheapest* route, and it is the single strongest technical support in the primary literature for a purchased-chemistry thesis. Cold contact and immobilised-yeast bioreactors are described as having incomplete or very short fermentations, hence "lower organoleptic qualities," with the bioreactor route additionally "difficult to control" with "high cost and risk of microbiological contamination" — implementation-specific problems layered on the class-inherent ester deficit.

- **GEA:** equipment only; no chemistry.

**The sixth party.** **dsm-firmenich is not among the five expressions and holds the most specific, most recent, primary-verifiable NA IP located in this exercise:** WO2026064099A1, dihydrochalcones acting on TRPA1/TRPV1/TRPM8 at 1–1,000 ppm, claimed to permit ethanol reduction up to 100 % (C17), on top of a named commercial stack (Novasense® Alcohol Sensation, Brewers TasteZyme™ G, BeeR'Evolution — C28). It is filed against exactly the one break that cannot be engineered away (§2(iii)).

Academic IP is also entering the pool independently (KU Leuven/VIB, C18), which is a supply-side fact about the durability of any single house's position.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

Enumerated before the benches build kills on them.

1. **"Beverages 36 %" is not alcohol exposure.** Givaudan's own slide defines Beverages as *soft drinks, fruit juices, instant beverages* (C24). Using 36 % as an NA-beer proxy overstates it by an unknown but large factor. Any bench that reaches for the single most available beverage number in the peer set will reach for this one.
2. **IFF discloses no beverage or alcohol end-market at all** (C21). An IFF "NA % of group" figure cannot be derived from disclosure; it can only be estimated. Blanks are not permitted by doctrine, but neither is presenting an estimate as disclosure.
3. **IFF's denominator has moved twice in 18 months.** Pharma Solutions sold 2025-05-01; Food Ingredients moved to discontinued ops in the Q2 2026 filing (C19). A ratio computed against FY2024 group revenue and one computed against $3,860m H1 2026 continuing-ops revenue are not the same ratio, and the direction of the difference is to *raise* any exposure percentage mechanically, with no change in the business.
4. **Segment misattribution.** The one identified IFF NA asset is an enzyme (H&B, $601m Q2), not a flavour (Taste, $688m Q2). A materiality test run against the wrong segment is wrong in both numerator and denominator.
5. **The perimeter changes again before Q2 2027.** Texturants and emulsifiers — the ingredient class most adjacent to body/mouthfeel rebuild — leave with the Food Ingredients disposal group (C22). Whether they carry NA revenue is unverifiable (C23), but any exposure estimate must state which side of that close it is computed on.
6. **0.05 % ABV and 0.5 % ABV are different products, not different precisions.** GEA quotes 0.05 %; Alfa Laval's Lowal literature quotes <0.5 %. These serve different legal regimes (UK 0.05 % vs EU/US 0.5 %, C32) and impose different aroma-loss burdens, because the worty threshold sits between them (C2). Vendor specs quoted side by side are mismatched estimands.
7. **Capacity units differ.** GEA publishes hL/day *and* hL/h; Alfa Laval's page publishes neither. Comparing "1,000 hL/day" to "50 hL/h" without conversion (50 hL/h ≈ 1,200 hL/day at full utilisation) is a common error, and utilisation is not disclosed by anyone.
8. **Temperature specs conflict within the same technology.** Flavourtech says 30–45 °C for the SCC; the peer-reviewed description says 30–34 °C with ~11 repeated passes (C11 vs C12). Do not average; a kill resting on "SCC is a hot process" must say which source it uses.
9. **Cross-jurisdiction NA volume series mix product definitions.** A country reporting NA volume at ≤0.5 % (Germany, US, Brazil), one at 0.05 % (UK), one at <1.2 % (France) and one at <2.8 % (Finland) are not counting the same beverage (C32).
10. **IWSR is vendor data and its category boundaries shift.** "No-alcohol +9 % in 2025" spans beer, wine, spirits and RTD; "2 % → 4 % of global beer volume" is beer-only. These two numbers appear in the same press material and are routinely conflated (C33).
11. **Patent counts are not commensurable capability.** Brewers hold *product-composition* claims (C15), culture houses hold *strain* claims (C16), flavour houses hold *molecule/use* claims (C17, C18). Counting families across these classes measures filing style, not moat.
12. **Currency and period bases differ across all five.** Givaudan T&W CHF 3,642m (FY2025), Symrise TNH €3,028m (FY2025), IFF Taste $688m (Q2 2026 — a *quarter*), GEA H1 2026 €2,715.6m (a *half*), Kerry in EUR. Four currencies and three period lengths. Annualising a single IFF quarter also embeds beverage seasonality that is not disclosed.
13. **Kerry's history is a denominator trap in the same family as the segment changes above** — the thesis flags the consumer-foods disposal; I did not verify Kerry's current segment perimeter at primary and it should not be assumed stable (§6).
