## SCIENCE BRIEF — KRZ.ID (Kerry Group plc)

**The question the case turns on:** whether removing ethanol from a fermented beverage creates a *thermodynamic* defect that only a proprietary flavour system can repair — or a *compositional* defect repairable with commodity add-backs (glycerol, mineral salts, malt extract, hop products) and a yeast choice, in which case the paid layer sits with yeast, hop and equipment suppliers rather than with a flavour house.

## 2. THE MECHANISM

**Established science.** Ethanol in beer is simultaneously (i) a flavour-active molecule, (ii) a *solvent* that sets the polarity of the matrix, and (iii) a bulk contributor to viscosity/body and to warming trigeminal response. (i) is replaceable; (ii) is the hard one. Aroma release into headspace is governed by air/liquid partition coefficients, which depend on matrix polarity. Strip ethanol and the matrix becomes more polar: hydrophobic esters partition out more readily (perceived loss of fruity/estery top notes) while hydrophilic Strecker aldehydes — 2-methylpropanal, 2-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanal, methional, phenylacetaldehyde — are *retained and released differently*, which is the physical origin of the "worty" defect that defines the category (C3, tagged unverifiable at primary; the direction of the effect is uncontested in the literature, the magnitudes are what I could not confirm at source).

The operational consequence, and it is the crux: **you cannot fix an NA beer by re-dosing the original aroma at the original concentration**, because the concentration→perception mapping has moved. That is a genuine, non-trivial formulation problem, and it is the strongest technical support available for the thesis's "chemistry layer gets paid" claim.

**The three physical routes and what each destroys.**
- *Vacuum/thermal distillation.* Ethanol's boiling point drops under reduced pressure (Müller et al. operated at 40 mbar / 36 °C). But the volatile aroma fraction is stripped *before* and *with* the ethanol: their condensate ran 38.74% abv while the recovered "aromawater" carried the esters. The paper's own conclusion is that a downstream aroma column is **essential**, and that recovery is selective — esters enrich preferentially over higher alcohols (3-methylbutanol only 3.54% transfer). So thermal NAB is not "beer minus ethanol"; it is beer minus ethanol minus a *biased subset* of its aroma, with a biased subset returned.
- *Reverse osmosis / nanofiltration.* No heat, but ethanol leaves with water and the retentate must be diafiltered back to volume. Bóna et al. measured 15–18% real-extract loss and ~50% passage of Na⁺/K⁺ giving ~80% depletion in product. Their panel needed **mineral salts and glycerol added back** before the product was acceptable.
- *Spinning cone column.* Two passes — volatiles stripped ~30 °C, ethanol ~40 °C, aroma returned. Dominant in wine, less so in beer.

**The biological route bypasses all of it.** Maltose-negative / maltotriose-negative yeasts (SmartBev NEER, WLP618, *S. ludwigii*, *Pichia kluyveri*) ferment only monosaccharides, so ethanol never forms above ~0.5%. Capex-light — a strain purchase, not a €450k skid. Its defect profile is the *opposite* of the physical route: highest worty aroma **and** highest residual sweetness, because maltose is left in the beer (C5).

**Where the ceiling sits.** The accepted repairs are, in order of tonnage: glycerol (bulk commodity), maltodextrins/dextrins for body (commodity), mineral salts (commodity), malt/cereal extract (Döhler, Ireks — commodity-to-specialty), hop products including late/dry-hop aroma and downstream hop oils (BarthHaas, Yakima Chief — specialty), yeast strain choice (Novonesis, Lallemand — proprietary, patent-protected), and only then bitter-receptor maskers and encapsulated aroma systems (the flavour-house layer). The thesis's picks-and-shovels logic is sound *at the level of the layer*; the open technical question is **what fraction of the per-hL repair spend lands in the flavour-house sub-layer** versus the yeast, hop, malt-extract and equipment sub-layers. No primary I found quantifies that split.

**Named historical failure modes, and whether they are class-inherent.**
1. *Worty aldehyde defect* — class-inherent to the biological route (incomplete yeast reduction of Strecker aldehydes to their alcohols); implementation-specific to the physical route (aroma-recovery design).
2. *Loss of body / "empty" mouthfeel* — class-inherent to all ethanol-removal routes; ethanol contributes body directly.
3. *Microbiological instability* — class-inherent and under-appreciated: ethanol is a preservation hurdle, and NA beer with residual fermentable sugar has neither ethanol nor low pH working for it, forcing pasteurisation, sterile filtration or preservative systems (see *J. Am. Soc. Brew. Chem.*, "Microbiological Quality Control in Non- and Low-Alcohol…", 2025, https://doi.org/10.1080/03610470.2025.2508612).
4. *Mineral stripping* — implementation-specific to membranes (Bóna et al.).

## 3. THE FIELD (as of 2026-09-03)

**Brewers hold the NA-beer IP.** The patent register since 2020 is dominated by Heineken Supply Chain B.V. (concentrate/capsule/dispense systems, gluconate low-alcohol beer, WO2025061705A1 and WO2026032794A1 on dispensing NA draught), Suntory Holdings and Tsingtao (C11). The peer-reviewed aldehyde-control work is itself co-authored by Heineken R&D with TU Delft (Gernat, Brouwer, Ottens). This is the demand side *already holding* the capability in-house at the top of the market.

**Yeast/enzyme suppliers hold the capex-light route.** Novonesis (SmartBev NEER, NEER Punch), Lallemand, White Labs WLP618. This is the route a brewer without a dealcoholisation plant takes — and it routes spend to a biotech supplier, not a flavour house.

**Hop and malt-extract houses hold the aroma add-back.** BarthHaas ran a dedicated 2026 NA campaign, publishing Part 1 (flavour optimisation) with Part 2 on dealcoholisation technologies guided for summer 2026 (https://www.barthhaas.com/company/news/news-article/bh/know-how-and-expertise-for-outstanding-flavor). Döhler — private, unlisted, therefore not investable through the five — sells **ethanol-free beer extracts**, malt/cereal extracts and beer distillates specifically to "enhance texture and mouthfeel while boosting the alcohol-like profile of non-alcoholic beer" (https://www.doehler.com/en/applications-solutions/beer-malt-beverages/beer.html). Döhler is the closest pure competitor to the thesis's own picks-and-shovels description and it is not one of the five.

**Equipment.** GEA AromaPlus (RO, to 0.05% abv), Alfa Laval LOWAL de-alcoholizer, Krones/Steinecker dynamic fermentation (biological route). Note that Krones/Steinecker is a *listed* equipment leg the thesis's five omit, and it sells the route that competes with GEA's, not a complement to it.

**The five, on NA-specific technical evidence:**
- **GIVN.SW** — hop-oil/polymeric-terpene emulsion filing (WO2026068507A1, Apr 2026) and a 2024 taste-modifying ingredient. Direct NA-beer-relevant chemistry on the register.
- **IFF** — ES2987952T3, explicitly for "improving the alcohol sensation as well as the carbonation effect". The single most on-thesis patent found across the five.
- **SY1.DE** — zero hits on "non-alcoholic beer" in the assignee query run.
- **KRZ.ID** — zero hits. Public NA platform is **spirits-facing botanicals** (2020) plus general-purpose Tastesense masking/mouthfeel/sensations. Its own alcohol and brewing pages do not name dealcoholisation or NA beer.
- **G1A.DE** — equipment (AromaPlus), verified product, but competing against Alfa Laval, Krones/Steinecker *and* against the biological route which needs no equipment at all.

Cross-comparison caveat: the patent counts above come from a single register (Google Patents) with assignee-string matching. Different corporate assignee vehicles, jurisdictional filing strategies and trade-secret practice make these counts **non-comparable as a ranking**. They are evidence about *what is on the public register*, nothing more.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

**T1 — Beverage ≠ alcohol ≠ NA.** Kerry's €922.9m beverage line bundles coffee, nutritional beverages, refreshing beverages, dairy beverages and alcohol. The thesis's materiality bar is on *NA-exposed* revenue; the disclosed number is three levels of aggregation above it. Any bench building a kill or a save on 27.66% is using the wrong denominator's numerator.

**T2 — The share is flat, and that is arithmetic, not interpretation.** Beverage was 27.65% of group in H1 2025 and 27.66% in H1 2026. Beverage revenue fell 3.63% y/y against group −3.66%. Whatever low/no alcohol contributed (C15), it did not move beverage's share of Kerry by a basis point at the reported-revenue level. This is the Asahi arithmetic template applied at the only granularity Kerry discloses.

**T3 — Volume vs revenue vs FX.** Kerry grew volume +3.3% while reported revenue fell −3.7%. Pricing, mix and translation FX drive the gap. Any NA growth claim stated in revenue terms is confounded; any stated in volume terms is not reconcilable to a revenue contribution because Kerry publishes no volume-by-end-use.

**T4 — Segment axis mismatch.** Kerry's reportable segments are geographic (Americas/Europe/APMEA) with EBITDA margins disclosed *by region only*. End-use market is a supplementary revenue-only cut. **Relative margin of the NA-exposed business — the third leg of the thesis's own materiality test — is not derivable from any Kerry disclosure.** It is not a number that exists to be found.

**T5 — Portfolio discontinuity.** Kerry Dairy Ireland phase 1 completed 31 Dec 2024 (C18), Consumer Foods earlier. The H1 2025 comparative already excludes Dairy, so the −3.7% is *not* a disposal artifact — but any multi-year series crossing 2022 or 2024 is not like-for-like, and the 30% Co-Op option remains outstanding.

**T6 — Category CAGR ≠ ingredient spend.** IWSR's +7–9% no-alcohol volume CAGR and the +US$4bn-by-2028 figure are **retail/consumption value in 10 key markets** (vendor data). Converting that to flavour-house revenue requires a per-hL ingredient spend and a capture rate, neither of which any primary in this corpus supplies. Compounding a consumption CAGR onto a supplier's revenue is the trap.

**T7 — Patent absence is a weak instrument.** Flavour houses protect compositions by trade secret precisely because publication teaches the formula. Kerry's zero NA-beer hits (C10) is evidence about the register, and it is *symmetric* — it also means no bench can verify Kerry's moat by the same instrument. Do not build a kill on C10 alone; do not build a save on Kerry's marketing pages alone (C6/C9 have no numbers in them at all).

**T8 — Route conditioning.** The bill of materials for a biologically-produced NA beer (yeast strain, unchanged wort) and a physically dealcoholised one (equipment, aroma recovery, glycerol, mineral salts, hop add-back) are **different products bought from different suppliers**. Any model of "the chemistry layer gets paid on every NA launch" must condition on route mix, which is undisclosed (see §6).

**T9 — Commodity add-backs.** The add-backs a peer-reviewed panel actually required were **glycerol and mineral salts** (C2) — bulk chemicals with no flavour-house margin. That is the single most direct threat to the picks-and-shovels premise, and it is on the record at primary.

**T10 — "NA" is not one legal object.** EU: ≤0.5% "alcohol-free", ≤0.05% "0.0%", in force 18 Mar 2026. US TTB: <0.5% falls outside alcohol-beverage jurisdiction entirely. Japan's market convention is 0.00%. Volume series, category values and per-country growth rates are threshold-dependent and not additive across jurisdictions without restatement.

**T11 — FX bases across the five.** KRZ.ID and SY1.DE report EUR, GIVN.SW CHF, IFF USD; Kerry earns 54% of revenue in the Americas while reporting in EUR. Cross-name margin and growth comparisons need a stated FX convention.

**T12 — Spirits vs beer.** Kerry's identified NA asset (Botanicals Collection Zero) serves gin/rum/whiskey analogues. NA *spirits* is a small fraction of the no/low category; IWSR puts no-alcohol **beer/cider** at ~70% of category growth. Kerry's demonstrated NA capability and the category's growth engine are not pointed at the same product.
