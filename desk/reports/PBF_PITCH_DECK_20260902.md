# PITCH — PBF — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — no position and no purchase at ~$77; a 0.4–0.5% starter only on a fill at ~$68 or lower, cancelled outright on a Russian fuel-export-ban lift or a ceasefire.

## What we believe

PBF Energy refines crude oil into gasoline, diesel and jet fuel at six US plants. Refiners don't sell oil — they sell the *gap* between what crude costs and what fuel sells for. That gap is enormous right now: the standard three-barrel blended margin is around $61 versus roughly $20 a year ago, and the diesel margin at about $105 is an all-time record.

We believe the gap is wide for a reason that will not close in a quarter. Ukrainian drones have hit at least 25 Russian refineries since August 2025. Russian refinery runs fell to 3.6 million barrels a day in July 2026 — a 24-year low against a 5.3–5.6 million normal. Critically, the damage is not only to simple distillation towers, which are repaired in weeks. About 250,000 barrels a day of *hydrocracking* capacity is offline versus 50–60 thousand a year ago — a four-to-five-fold increase. Hydrocrackers are the units that make diesel, they are built from specialised components, Western sanctions have lengthened the lead times to source those components, and the same plants have been struck repeatedly before repairs finished (Tuapse three times in April, twice in May). The world is short diesel-making capacity, not short crude.

We also believe PBF's own calendar is unusually favourable into the peak. It deferred both its Chalmette and Toledo autumn maintenance shutdowns to 2027, and finishes the Martinez hydrocracker work at the end of September. That leaves all five systems available through the fourth quarter — its cleanest quarter in two years — arriving exactly as margins peak.

And we believe the balance sheet can carry a bad outcome. Cash of $894.1M against $1,800.0M of debt principal leaves roughly $906M net debt on $6.5B of equity. That is not a company that gets forced into anything.

What we do **not** believe is that this is cheap. It is not. It is a fairly-to-richly priced cyclical whose only defence is that the market has already written down next year.

## What the market believes

The market believes almost exactly what we believe about the margin — and has already marked down what comes after.

Analysts model $17.97 of earnings per share this year and **$9.70 next year, a 46% decline**. They are not extrapolating the boom. At $76.97 the stock trades at 7.9 times the street's own post-normalisation number. The average price target is $67.62 — below the current price — though that average is dragged by a $38 outlier; the median is $73, and the three most recent large-bank targets are all *above* spot (UBS $84 on Aug 4, JPMorgan $83 on Aug 5, Goldman $81 on Aug 18).

The market also believes this is a trade, not an investment. The stock is up 181% in twelve months and sits at its 52-week high, 3.04 times its low. It is 89% institutionally held with 9.26% of shares sold short and 3.28 days of average volume to cover — meaning a meaningful part of the move up has been short sellers being forced to buy, not new investors deciding the business is worth more. Insiders filed fifteen sale-related forms between August 14 and 19, selling into that strength.

The spread of analyst estimates is the real tell: this year's earnings forecasts range from $11.44 to $26.71, a 2.3-fold spread. Nobody knows. The price is a bet, and it is being priced as one.

## Why we might have edge

Three places, and only three.

**One: the mechanism read.** The widely-reported version of the Russian story is "refineries got hit, they'll fix them." Our own red-team analyst reached exactly that conclusion and recommended rejecting the idea on it. It's wrong on the specifics — the published Kpler and RFE/RL reporting names hydrocrackers, catalytic reformers and hydrotreaters, not just distillation columns, and quotes the sanctions-lengthened parts problem directly. The distinction between "distillation tower damaged, back in a month" and "hydrocracker damaged, specialised parts, sanctioned supply chain, hit again before it restarted" is the entire difference between a two-quarter margin and a two-year one. We caught that the second time we looked. But — honestly — this is published reporting, not proprietary work. Our edge is diligence, not information.

**Two: the accounting.** PBF's headline cash generation looks either much better or much worse than it is depending on how carefully you read the cash-flow statement, and both our own benches got pieces of it wrong (detailed below). The clean number: first-half operating cash of $1,265.1M, less $476.5M of ordinary capital spending, less $242.4M of deferred maintenance spending that is easy to miss, equals **$546.2M** of real free cash. Strip the genuinely one-off insurance cash and it is about **$435M** for the half — roughly a 9–10% yield on a $9.2B company, from a half-year that averaged a $30–35 margin with two plants partly down. Spot margins are nearly double that. This is the calculation the court asked for and neither bench did properly on filed numbers.

**Three: the calendar asymmetry.** Deferring Chalmette and Toledo is being read as a negative (it pushes cost into 2027). It is also, mechanically, the reason PBF has five plants running into the highest margins on record. Both readings are true; we think the near-term one is larger and less noticed.

## Why it might be priced in

Bluntly: we have no disagreement with the market about the thing that matters.

We think margins stay elevated. So does the street — that's why this year's estimate is $17.97. We think next year is worse. So does the street — that's why next year's is $9.70. There is no gap between our view and consensus that we can point to and say "this is what they've missed." Our own red-team analyst named this as the killing argument and we do not have a good answer: paying a normal multiple for peak earnings is the classic way to lose money in refining, and at $76.97 we would be paying 13.8% above the average analyst target and 92% of the single most bullish target on the street.

Everything else compounds that. The stock is at its high after tripling. Part of that tripling is short covering, which is fuel, not valuation. Reported profit includes a $313.0M non-cash inventory writeback that reverses if crude falls — and crude falling is precisely the scenario in which we'd be wrong. The share count has gone the wrong way (114.6M diluted in early 2025 to 121.07M now; basic shares 113.75M to 117.78M), the buyback is absent from the second-quarter release, and the dividend yields 1.43% on a quarter that earned $6.22. Management raised $800M at a 9.875% coupon in March 2025 — at the bottom, when it was expensive.

If the war ends, or Moscow lifts the export ban it has said it will lift "when the market recovers," this stock does not go down 10%.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — the switch, not the wound.** Roughly a quarter or more of the missing fuel isn't destroyed capacity at all; it's withheld by decree. Russia's gasoline-and-diesel export ban runs to end-January 2027 with a publicly stated condition for lifting it. A ceasefire, a negotiated settlement, or simply Moscow deciding the domestic market has "recovered" restores product to the export market overnight, with no repair time required. The record diesel margin is partly a war artifact, and peace is the bear case. *Monitorable:* yes — a published decree, ceasefire headlines, Russian export volumes. This is the risk we can actually watch, and it is the one that cancels the position outright.

**Failure narrative 2 — the margin we can see is not the margin PBF earns. (NO TRIPWIRE.)** Everything above quotes *benchmark* refining margins — public, published prices for a standard barrel. PBF's realised margin is a different, private number, and the gap between them is made of things nobody publishes: which crude grades it actually bought and at what discount, what its renewable-fuel credit obligation costs (disclosed in the filings only as risk-factor language — we found no source that quantifies the 2026 dollar amount), how much low-value byproduct it made, what unplanned downtime it ate. Our own bull-case sensitivity — about +$925M of pre-tax profit per quarter at a $61 benchmark margin — rests on an **assumed 40% capture rate** that the company does not disclose. Benchmarks could stay at record highs all quarter and PBF's number could still disappoint, and we would not learn it until the print. There is no sensor that can be armed for this, because the input is not published anywhere between quarters. **Unknown-unknown class: unpublished basis risk — the distance between a public price we can watch and a private realised number we cannot, where the gap is composed of inputs the issuer never discloses at observable frequency.** We are one instance away from a whole family of these; the same structure applies to any commodity processor.

**Failure narrative 3 — we get filled only when we're wrong, and our own rules pull the order anyway. (The reflexive/structural leg.)** This is the uncomfortable one. We refuse to buy at $77. Our first band is $68. So we only own this stock after an 11% decline — and the most likely single cause of an 11% decline in PBF is *the exact news that invalidates the thesis*: a ceasefire, an export-ban lift, a crude collapse. We have written an order that fills preferentially in the world where we are wrong. That is adverse selection against ourselves, and it is a direct instance of the house rule about never bidding into flow you yourself predict.

Two things compound it. First, our own standing into-print rule automatically cancels resting buy orders within about three days of a scheduled earnings date — so a $68 order sitting unfilled through late October gets pulled around **October 26** and has to be deliberately re-entered after the print. Nobody should be surprised by that. Second, if we're *right*, the stock runs from $77 and we never own it at all; the honest expected outcome of a bands-below-only order on a name at its 52-week high is "no position," and we should say so rather than pretend we have exposure.

What is *not* a risk here is our own footprint. PBF trades hundreds of millions of dollars a day; a 0.4–0.5% starter for this book is a small fraction of one percent of a single day's volume. We are a price-taker in both directions. The reflexivity risk isn't our size — it's that we would be joining a crowd (89% institutional, 9.26% short with three days to cover) whose forced buying *is* the recent price, at the moment that crowd is most fully committed.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that the war ended — or Moscow simply switched exports back on — and we discovered that the record diesel margin was a policy artifact we had mistaken for a physical shortage, in a stock we bought at its 52-week high after it had already tripled.
