# PITCH — LEU — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — no position, no orders; both benches land on reject, but on different grounds, and the surviving reason is narrow enough that it should be adjudicated rather than assumed.

---

## What we believe

Centrus enriches uranium. It is the only US-owned company running an enrichment cascade on US soil, at Piketon, Ohio, and it is the only domestic source of the high-assay fuel that the next generation of small reactors needs. The government is paying it to build that capability, and reactor developers are signing up for the output years before it exists.

The original case was that the market had marked this down as a story stock while the story was quietly becoming a contract book — a $4.5 billion backlog against a market value of roughly $3.7 billion, growing $600 million in a quarter, stretching to 2040.

**That belief did not survive the courts, and we should say so first.** The audited filing does not contain that backlog number. What it contains is $0.8 billion of remaining performance obligations, up from $0.6 billion at year-end, running to 2030. The gap is explained — about $3.0 billion of the marketed figure is contingent commitments, and the filing states plainly that it excludes unexercised options and indefinite-quantity government work — but explained is not the same as owned. Firm, contracted demand is $0.8 billion, or about 22% of the market value, not 123%.

What we still believe, stripped down:

- Enrichment demand is real and rising, and firm obligations grew 33% in six months.
- The Piketon build is genuinely accelerating. Job postings went from 1 a month in March to 23 a month in August, 43 open roles, against a company guide of at least 175 net new hires. Companies that are quietly stalling do not hire like that.
- The company has $1.87 billion of cash. Whatever else is true, it is not going to run out of money.

What we no longer believe: that the demand side is 5x larger than the audited page says, and that this is an undiscovered name.

## What the market believes

The market believes roughly what we believe, and has already written it down.

Seventeen analysts cover the stock. Price targets run $223 to $279, against a $184 tape — so the sell side is bullish by 21% to 72%. At the same time, 21.6% to 23.3% of the float is sold short. That is a stock with a loud argument on both sides and no quiet corner.

Critically: the street's revenue estimate for 2026 is $459.3 million, sitting at the *bottom* of the company's own $450–500 million guidance range, and 2026 earnings estimates were cut from $2.90 to $2.59. Our thesis named one decisive question — is the second half worse than the first? The street has already answered yes and priced it.

The stock is down 60% from its 52-week high of $464.25, but it is also up 29.5% from its 52-week low of $142.13. This is a name that already bounced a third off the bottom. It is not a fresh dislocation.

## Why we might have edge

Three places where the work found something the tape may not hold:

**1. The hiring signal is a real, forward, non-financial read.** Piketon postings went 1 → 4 → 14 → 23 per month between March and August. This is the single measurement in the entire record that runs *against* the bear case, and both benches conceded it. If the cascade were quietly slipping, the staffing curve would flatten. It has not. Nobody on the street is counting job postings at a single Ohio parcel.

**2. The cost side is where the story actually lives, and almost nobody is looking there.** The company buys enriched product cheaply under a legacy Russian contract priced in 2018 and running to 2028. A Russian decree from October 2025 has suspended the Russian supplier's US export licence through the end of 2027. Replacement supply from Orano runs to 2030 at a market-linked price, which is higher. So the volume decline this quarter is a *supply* problem, not a demand problem — and it is locked in by policy through 2027. Everyone is arguing about demand. The binding constraint is inputs.

**3. The short interest is a mechanism, not just a fact.** Twenty-three percent short against $1.87 billion of cash and a convertible bond that is deep in the money is a squeeze setup. That is a timing instrument if we ever want to own this. It is not a reason to buy, and it is not a reason to reject either.

## Why it might be priced in

**The honest answer is: it probably is.**

The decisive test we set ourselves was whether we saw something the street did not on the second-half slowdown. We ran that test and we failed it. Consensus revenue is already at the low end of guidance and earnings estimates have already been cut. We have no number that differs from the street's number on the question we ourselves called decisive. That is the cleanest reason to pass in this entire record, and it is the reason we are passing.

Supporting that:

- **Valuation is not cheap on anything audited.** Enterprise value is roughly $3.0–3.4 billion depending on how you treat the convertible bonds, against roughly $200 million of annualised gross profit — call it 15x to 17x. And that $200 million is generous, because the quarter contained a one-time $53.4 million uranium sale whose profit margin the company did not disclose. Strip a plausible contribution from it and the multiple goes to the low 20s.
- **The dilution is bigger than the thesis assumed.** The convertible principals are $402.5 million and $805.0 million, not the $350/$700 million in the original brief — those were pre-greenshoe. Full conversion is 7.63 million shares against 19.95 million outstanding: **38% dilution**, with no capped call or hedge disclosed anywhere in the filing.
- **The government prize is capped and slow.** The funded piece is a $900 million fixed-price task order plus $170 million of options, to deliver one metric ton of high-assay fuel by March 2032. Against a $3+ billion enterprise value, the funded contract is smaller than the valuation it is supposed to justify. Real upside has to come from commercial contracts the audited $0.8 billion does not yet contain.
- **The segment doing the government work is losing money.** Technical Solutions ran a $1.7 million gross loss this quarter against a $3.2 million profit a year ago — on a fixed-price contract, while hiring accelerates. (Fairly: over six months that segment is still $1.9 million profitable on 8% revenue growth. The quarterly loss is a base effect, not a trend. See below — we got this wrong twice.)

## What remains unverified

- **The live price.** Everything here is priced off a secondary tape at $184.12 (one bench used $186.72). Four denied broker requests.
- **The quarterly filing itself, at deck level.** Both benches read it through a proxy service after the SEC blocked direct fetches. I did not independently re-open it while writing this. Every audited figure quoted here is a bench read of the primary document, uncontested by the opposing bench — but not re-verified a third time.
- **The uranium sale's profit margin.** Not disclosed. This is the number that would resolve whether the quarter was good or merely dressed. The company chose not to publish it.
- **Whether the $900 million is actually obligated.** Federal spending records show nothing. Could be timing, could be a failed name match. Unresolved.
- **Whether any import arrivals occurred.** Our customs check returned an empty list, which is not evidence of anything.
- **What the cascade earns per unit at full rate.** Centrus discloses no steady-state unit economics for the Ohio plant, so the actual prize — recurring enrichment output, not the capped government order — cannot be valued from public information. Stating this as a gap rather than filling it with an assumption.
- **Whether convertible holders are hedged.** No capped call or bond hedge appears in the filing. Absence of disclosure is not proof of absence.
- **The consensus figures themselves** come from aggregator sites, not from a primary estimate service. Our reject ground leans entirely on them.
- **Two competing detector proposals came out of this court and neither is settled.** One bench wants a rule that flags marketed backlog running far ahead of audited obligations; the other showed that same rule would fire falsely on nearly every company with government or contingent contracts, and proposed a blocking guard. The rule should only be adopted with the guard attached — otherwise we will manufacture this exact false alarm on the next name.
