**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION: Do not buy at $12.28. ## What we believe

Phreesia sells check-in software to medical practices. It has three revenue lines: subscription software fees from clinics, payment processing on patient bills, and "Network Solutions" — health messaging (mostly drug-company advertising) shown to patients during check-in.

We believe the stock, at $12.28, is not the bargain it appears to be, for three reasons the court confirmed from the company's own filings:

1. **The core subscription business shrank.** Subscription revenue fell 3.0% year over year ($52.7M vs $54.4M) on a like-for-like basis — the 10-Q itself states prior periods were *not* restated, so this is a real decline, not an accounting reshuffle ([10-Q, quarter ended April 30, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1412408/000141240826000172/phr-20260430.htm)). Client count grew 7% over the same period, so revenue *per client* in the core product fell roughly 9%.
2. **The "cheap at 6x cash earnings" case depends on ignoring stock compensation.** Rebuild the cash flow honestly — operating cash flow of $23.9M in the quarter, minus $7.55M of capital spending, minus roughly $54M/year of stock handed to employees, plus interest costs that jumped from $0.4M to $2.3M per quarter after the debt-funded AccessOne acquisition — and true annual free cash flow to owners is roughly $5–15M against a $759M market value. That is not cheap.
3. **The share count grows 5.3% per year** (62.0M diluted shares now vs 58.9M a year ago), so with roughly zero organic growth, revenue per share is falling.

The advertising business faces a September 2 verdict: about 100 FDA enforcement letters to drug advertisers in September 2025 froze marketing budgets, drug-company clients are "committing lower spend levels for the second half," and the quarter being reported faces the toughest year-ago comparison of the year (+24.7% growth to beat). We believe the right move is to let that print resolve before paying anything.

## What the market believes

The market has already punished this stock — down 62.5% from its 52-week high of $32.76 — but has also already begun rewarding a recovery: the price is up 58% from the April low of $7.77. The buy case as the market sees it:

- Management **maintained full-year revenue guidance** of $510–520M despite the FDA-letter disruption.
- First-quarter operating cash flow was a **record** ($23.9M) against a fiscal first quarter that was negative two years ago — the cash trajectory is genuinely, steeply improving.
- An activist investor, **Pale Fire Capital, owns 14.7% of the company** ($103.5M position), which reads as smart-money endorsement.
- Stock compensation is falling ($17.2M/quarter a year ago to $13.6M now), so dilution is decelerating.
- On the popular adjusted-EBITDA framing, the stock trades near 6x — cheap for healthcare software.

This is a covered, followed stock. Our no-entry stance is about timing and price, not about the market having missed the story.

## Why we might have edge

- **The unnarrated line.** The bull discussion is entirely about the advertising freeze (temporary, external, guided). Nobody is narrating that core subscription revenue fell while the client count rose — a per-client decline of ~9% that management has not explained. If it is churn or repricing rather than a deliberate mix shift toward smaller practices, the "stable software base plus temporary ad shock" story is wrong at its foundation.
- **The activist stopped buying four months ago — below $8.55.** The 14.7% stake reads as endorsement at today's price, but the filing record says otherwise: Pale Fire's last purchases were March 31–April 2 at $7.78–$8.53, and no amendment has been filed since (a purchase of 1%+ would legally compel one — [filing history](https://www.secform4.com/13dg-history/1412408.html)). At $12.28 you pay 47% more than the informed buyer's last trade and 6% more than their blended average cost (~$11.60). The endorsement expired at a much lower price.
- **The corrected accounting frame.** The gap between "6x EBITDA" and "$5–15M of true free cash flow" is unusually wide here because the add-backs (stock comp ~$54M/year) are unusually large relative to the company's size. Investors anchoring on the adjusted multiple are valuing a number that mostly isn't cash.
- **Sharper print math than consensus framing.** The quarter being reported on September 2 laps +24.7% advertising growth — the year's hardest comparison — so a weak headline there is partly mechanical, and the court pre-committed the correct thresholds (below) rather than reacting to the optics.

## Why it might be priced in

Stated honestly, much of our case is visible:

- The subscription erosion **is not new information** — it began mid-fiscal-2026, two quarters before the FDA letters, and the market has had four quarters to watch it. A 62% drawdown plausibly already reflects it.
- The FDA advertising shock is fully public, quantified in guidance, and management has already re-affirmed the full-year outlook once with the disruption in hand.
- The cash-flow improvement is real and steep, which bounds the downside case: this is a company crossing into cash generation, not one burning toward a financing.
- Stock-comp and dilution, our sizing complaints, are both decelerating.
- There is also a scenario where the FDA's proposed broadcast-advertising rule *redirects* drug-marketing dollars toward point-of-care channels like Phreesia's — the enforcement letters and the eventual rule can cut in opposite directions.
- Our own defense bench's ruling: the rejection here is **timing-based, not a claim of mispricing**. Two defensible valuation frames diverge; we are choosing not to pay to find out which is right 13 sessions before the answer.
