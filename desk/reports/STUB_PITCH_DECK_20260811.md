# PITCH — STUB — PROPOSED VERDICT (one line)

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION: NO POSITION in either direction into the August 12 earnings report; reconvene the court within 48 hours after the report, where the live question is whether the market's fear of ticket-price-cap laws spreading nationwide is overdone.**

## What we believe

StubHub at $8.78 is down 68.5% from its 52-week high of $27.89 (and 53% above its $5.74 low), and the stock now embeds three fears: a weak summer driven by the World Cup resale market, a thin underlying business once accounting noise is stripped out, and a state-by-state regulatory campaign to cap resale prices and fees. After a full adversarial review, we believe:

1. **The reported cash flow badly overstates the real business.** Of the $298.4 million of operating cash flow in Q1, $273.6 million was simply customer money passing through the platform (cash collected from buyers not yet paid out to sellers grew from $845.9M to $1,111.7M). The underlying cash generation was roughly $17 million for the quarter. This is confirmed from the company's own Q1 release.
2. **The common stockholder's claim is thinner than headline profit suggests, but improving fast.** There is a preferred stock layer senior to the common — $454 million carrying value, $706 million liquidation preference — so profit attributable to common holders was $32.5M in Q1, not the $48.0M headline. However, that preferred layer shrank from $758M to $454M in a single quarter, the company repaid $100M of debt in May, and interest expense has fallen from $42.4M to $17.3M year over year. The balance sheet is healing quickly.
3. **The direction of Wednesday's earnings report is genuinely unresolvable in advance.** The World Cup ticket-price collapse happened June 22–29 — inside Q2, not Q3 as the original thesis assumed — and revenue books when the ticket transacts, not when the match is played. Both Q2 and Q3 are contaminated by World Cup effects in both directions, and we found zero StubHub-specific World Cup sales data anywhere. Anyone claiming to know how this quarter looks is guessing.
4. **The regulatory fear has a free, real-world test running — and so far the business passed.** Maine's 10% cap on marketplace service fees has been in force since September 2025. Through two reported quarters with that cap live, StubHub's overall fee rate *rose* to 20.5% of ticket value. Vermont's cap (effective July 1, 2026) applies only to small venues under 3,000 seats. The feared laws exist, but where enacted they are so far too small to dent the blended economics. This is the seed of the post-earnings long case.

## What the market believes

The market is pricing StubHub as a broken IPO with a binary event ahead: down two-thirds from the high, with short sellers holding 11.8% of the freely tradable shares (28.9M shares) — a crowded bearish bet into the report. The bear story the price implies: World Cup resale prices collapsed publicly in late June, price-cap laws are advancing in five states (Massachusetts is next, with more behind it), and the company's impressive-looking cash flow is mostly customer float rather than real earnings. Management, meanwhile, reaffirmed full-year guidance in May ($9.9–10.1 billion in gross ticket sales, $400–420M EBITDA), which the market evidently does not fully believe. Wall Street coverage exists (Citi and others), so this is not an ignored stock — it is a distrusted one.

## Why we might have edge

- **We have the Maine natural experiment and the market may not be watching it.** The most feared risk — fee caps — has been legally in force in one state for nearly a year, and the company's fee rate went *up*. This is a piece of empirical contra-evidence to the contagion story that we verified against the enacted law's press coverage and the company's own reported fee rate, and it was surfaced by our own defense bench, not by any analyst coverage we saw.
- **We did the accounting work.** The float-versus-real-cash decomposition and the preferred-stock adjustment come straight from the [Q1 2026 earnings release PDF](https://s204.q4cdn.com/138970181/files/doc_financials/2026/q1/Q1-2026-StubHub-Earnings-Release-Final.pdf) (pages 5–6), read primary by both benches independently. We also caught that the widely repeated "7.7% short interest" figure understates the crowding — it is 11.8% of tradable float.
- **We know exactly what to read at 4:05pm on August 12, in order:** the customer-float balance (≥ $1.11 billion means forward ticket sales held up), the fee rate (~20.5% means no cap erosion), the G&A expense line (up 49% year over year — where World Cup make-goods and legal costs would land), and the full-year guide. That checklist converts a chaotic report into four yes/no reads.
- **The insider tape is mildly reassuring and was misread publicly.** The July sales were pre-scheduled trading plans executed near $13, and the CEO's August 5 sale was non-discretionary tax withholding at $9.12 — not a discretionary dump at the lows.

## Why it might be priced in

Symmetry demands the concessions:

- **A 68.5% drawdown already prices a lot of bad news.** The bear case — thin real cash flow, regulatory overhang, World Cup mess — is publicly known and heavily shorted. We found no StubHub-specific data (positive or negative) that the market lacks; our edge claims rest on interpretation of public filings, not on private information.
- **The "cheap" claim was weaker than our own thesis stated.** The original owner-earnings-yield figure was computed before the preferred stock's senior claim; corrected, the equity residual is $32.5M per quarter of profit and ~$17M per quarter of real cash — the stock is not obviously cheap on current economics, only on normalized/improving ones.
- **The regulatory campaign is real and compounding.** Maine and Vermont are individually tiny, but Massachusetts is moving and five states have acted or are acting. The Maine experiment refutes the *current* damage, not the trajectory.
- **The stock is covered by major banks.** This is not an orphaned, unanalyzed name where mispricing persists by neglect; if the quarter is fine, the market can reprice it quickly without us.
