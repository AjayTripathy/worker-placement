# PITCH — BELFA — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — a minimum-size starter in Bel Fuse Class A, *blocked* until we can measure the A-vs-B price spread against its own history; no order goes in today.

---

## What we believe

Bel Fuse is a real, growing, cash-generating manufacturer that is being sold on an earnings number the company itself has overstated, and it has two share classes with different prices for nearly identical economics.

The operating business is not in dispute. Second-quarter 2026 sales were $210.7M, up 25.1%, with a 39.9% gross margin, and both segments grew: Aerospace/Defense & Rugged $110.5M (+20.3%, 41.1% margin) and Industrial/Transportation $100.2M (+31.1%, margin up 220bp). There is no bank debt and a $400M undrawn credit line. Both of our benches went looking for damage in the primary filings and neither found any.

What we think we know that the tape may not:

1. **The company's "adjusted" profit is inflated by about 17%, and we can prove it arithmetically.** Bel bridges from GAAP net earnings of $25.5M to "adjusted" $39.1M. The single biggest add-back, $6.738M, is a mark-up of the 20% of its Enercon subsidiary it does not yet own. Under the accounting rules that charge sits *below* the $25.5M starting line — it was never subtracted from it. We verified this by reproducing the company's own reported per-share numbers ($1.80 Class A, $1.89 Class B) and they only tie from $25.480M − $6.738M = $18.742M. So the company is adding back something it never took out.
2. **"Net cash $306M, debt-free" is wrong by a third.** $102.6M of that cash is spoken for: the sellers of Enercon hold a right to sell Bel the remaining 20%, and Bel says in its own filing it intends to buy it by early 2027, and named that purchase as a use of the money it raised in May. Real net cash is about $203.5M.
3. **The multiple everyone (including both our benches) used to hold this back was a data-vendor error.** The "28.7× forward earnings" figure came from a vendor that assigns the wrong share count to each class — it shows 14.44M shares for Class A and 12.66M for Class B when the true counts are 1.04M and 9.11M. Corrected, the honest range is roughly 16× on the company's adjusted number, ~25× on GAAP, and ~31–34× on the number that actually reaches a shareholder's per-share line.
4. **Class A trades 17.4% below Class B** ($210.70 vs $255.11 at the 8/26 close) despite carrying the votes. That is either a liquidity discount we can be paid to accept, or a permanent feature we would be buying into forever. **We do not currently know which, and that is the whole reason this pitch is not an order.**

## What the market believes

The market believes Bel is a good defense-and-industrial component compounder that just beat, and it is paying up: nine "strong buy" ratings, the stock up 89–91% off its 52-week low of $110.55, currently 28–29% below its 52-week high of $293.51.

But the sell side is quietly modeling a stall. Consensus third-quarter revenue is $207.5M against a company guide midpoint of $215M — the guide already beat the estimate and the estimate did not move up. Consensus implies revenue falls 1.5% from Q2, against a prior-year Q2-to-Q3 pattern of +6.4% ($168.3M → $179.0M). And Northland *cut* its Q3 EPS number to $1.82 on 2026-08-05, a week after a beat.

So the market's view is: sales are fine, the earnings that reach shareholders are not going to grow with them. That is a coherent view, and it happens to be right for the reason we identified — the Enercon minority charge eats the incremental earnings. **The market and we agree about the drag. We simply think the reported "adjusted" figure is overstated and the vendor multiples are garbage.**

## Why we might have edge

- **The accounting proof is genuinely ours.** We reproduced the two-class earnings-per-share allocation from the filing and it only ties if the $6.738M is *already* excluded from the starting line. That is an exact arithmetic fit, not an interpretation. If the company's own bridge is wrong, everyone anchoring to "$39.1M adjusted" is anchoring to an overstatement. Source: [Q2-26 8-K exhibit 99.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000729580/000143774926024893/ex_964865.htm) and [Q2-26 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/729580/000143774926025619/belfa20260630_10q.htm).
- **The vendor share-count error is checkable and apparently uncorrected.** Anyone screening on published price/earnings for either class is screening on a wrong number, in opposite directions for the two classes.
- **A modest, mechanism-backed revenue difference.** Against consensus $207.5M, the company's own guide is $215M and segment run-rates support ~$218M — about 3.6% above the street, with a named reason (both segments' current growth rates). This is real but small, and it is on revenue while the disagreement that matters is on earnings.
- **The second class is under-analyzed.** Class A is roughly one-tenth the shares of Class B, likely outside most index products, and priced by far fewer participants.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly: mostly, it is.

- We have **no meaningful disagreement on direction**. Our own red bench's cleanest finding was that we agree with the street, and our blue bench sustained that as a real limitation. A position with no divergence is a quality purchase, not an insight.
- The drag we "discovered" is exactly why Northland cut. The street may not have articulated the accounting mechanic, but it arrived at the same earnings conclusion.
- The stock is not dislocated. It is up ~90% from its low. Being 28% off a high after a near-double is a pullback in an uptrend, not a break.
- **The 17.4% Class A discount is probably at least partly rational.** Class A is smaller, thinner, and non-index. Discounts on illiquid share classes can persist for decades. The bull case requires that today's 17.4% is unusually wide *versus its own history*, and we have not measured that.
- The company's non-GAAP presentation, even if wrong, is unlikely to be the thing that re-rates the stock. Being right about an accounting presentation does not pay unless something forces the market to notice it.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — the Enercon 20% costs much more than $102.6M.** The carrying value is a mark, not a price. If the purchase formula is tied to Enercon's performance and uncapped, and Enercon is the fastest-growing part of the company, then the better Enercon does, the more Bel pays, and shareholders capture less of the growth they are being asked to pay 31× for. The May equity raise already tells us Bel expects to write a large cash check. *Tripwire exists:* the quarterly carrying value (it rose $9.4M in six months), the purchase-agreement exhibit, and any 8-K announcing the transaction. **Monitorable.**

**Failure narrative 2 — the discount is permanent, and we are the only convergence that ever happens.** We buy Class A because it is 17.4% cheaper, the discount never closes, and when we sell we give back the spread plus a wider bid/ask than we paid on the way in. This is the failure that looks like nothing going wrong: the business performs, the stock performs, and we simply own the worse-performing class. *Partially monitorable* — we can watch the ratio, but there is no signal that tells us "this was always structural" until we have already sat in it.

**Failure narrative 3 — the one with no tripwire: an export-control, customer-concentration, or jurisdiction shock we cannot see coming.** Bel manufactures in Dongguan, Guangxi and Shenzhen (China) and, through Enercon, in Netanya, Israel, selling defense components into US prime contractors. Neither bench could run a restricted-party or sanctions screen, and **Bel does not disclose its customer list**, so there is nothing to arm a sensor against. We cannot name the exposure, so we cannot watch it. **Unknown-unknown class: undisclosed counterparty-and-jurisdiction concentration** — the risk lives in a fact pattern the issuer is not required to publish, in two of the most politically volatile manufacturing jurisdictions on earth, on a defense-levered name. Both benches independently flagged this as the live tail and both were unable to check it. That agreement is not comfort; it is a shared blind spot.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** This is the part that most argues against the trade:

- **Our own size is a material share of this instrument.** Class A is 1,042,494 shares total — the entire class is worth about $220M. The only volume figure anywhere in the court record is an inference: our blue bench computed that a 500-share sale was 0.4% of one day's *Class B* volume, implying Class B trades roughly 125,000 shares (~$32M) a day. Class A is one-ninth the size and less followed. **We have never measured Class A's average daily volume — the market-data tool was permission-denied to both benches and again to me while writing this deck.** Under our own ≤1% of daily volume rule, a 1%-of-book position could take weeks to build, and we would be a persistent visible bid in a thin book.
- **We would be bidding into our own predicted flow.** The thesis is "Class A is too cheap versus Class B." Our own buying is the mechanism that narrows that gap. In an instrument this thin, we pay away part of the very edge we are buying, and we cannot distinguish our own footprint from thesis confirmation. Our doctrine explicitly forbids bidding into flow we ourselves predict; here the flow *is* us.
- **Two of our doctrines point opposite ways and we should say so rather than quietly pick one.** The "clean court" rule says that when no fatal objection survives, the default is a minimum starter — and none survived. But the integrity rule says a name carrying a proved reporting defect never counts as fairly-paid risk and must be capped at 0.85× our fair-value estimate. Bel's non-GAAP bridge is a proved defect, by our own arithmetic. **Recommendation: treat this as fairly-paid-with-a-flag** — the starter is allowed, but every entry level below is subject to the 0.85× contingency if the principal adjudicates the presentation defect as an integrity flag (contingency ladder given in the entry section).
- **Adverse selection on the class choice itself.** The people selling us Class A are, on average, people who have held it longest and know most about why it trades where it does.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be** that we bought the cheap-looking share class of a genuinely good manufacturer, were correct about the business and correct about the accounting, and still lost — because the 17.4% discount was permanent liquidity rather than a dislocation, our own thin-market buying was the only convergence that ever occurred, and we exited at a wider spread than we entered while the earnings we paid 31× for were partly bought out from under us by the Enercon minority.
