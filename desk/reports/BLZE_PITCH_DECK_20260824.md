# PITCH — BLZE — VERDICT

## What we believe

Backblaze sells cloud storage. Two businesses live inside it: a consumer/small-business backup product that is slowly dying (Q2-26 revenue $16.1M, down 2% year over year), and a developer-facing object-storage business, B2, that is growing fast (annual recurring revenue $113.3M, up 39%). Total Q2 revenue was $42.7M; the company guides the full year to $172–174M.

The thing that changed this month is that Backblaze stopped being a capital-light software company and became a capital-intensive one, on purpose, in a single quarter. In the first half of 2026 it spent $6.6M on equipment — 8.1% of first-half revenue. The full-year guide implies capital spending of 55–65% of revenue, which means roughly $89–106M in the back half against roughly $92M of back-half revenue. That is capital spending at 97–116% of revenue, up more than tenfold in two quarters.

It funded that on August 18–19 by issuing $175M of convertible notes due 2031 at a 0.00% coupon, converting at $21.94 (30% above where the stock was), with $15.2M of the proceeds spent on a "capped call" that pushes the effective dilution point up to $33.76. It simultaneously amended its bank credit agreement: the revolver goes from $20M to $50M, maturity extends from June 2028 to April 2030, and the financial covenants loosen — the minimum EBITDA test is removed and replaced with a fixed-charge coverage test ([8-K filed 2026-08-19](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1462056/000114036126033612/0001140361-26-033612-index.htm)).

The build is anchored by one customer: CoreWeave, on a contract circulating at roughly $313M net / $335M gross over five-plus years, with contractual minimums that do not start binding until mid-2027.

**Our fair value is $16.00–18.50 per share.** That comes from 4.5–5.5x enterprise value to 2027 revenue of roughly $222M, marked down for two things the company's preferred profit measure hides: stock compensation running at 19.0% of revenue, and a coming step-up in depreciation from the new equipment. The stock is $15.94. **We believe it is roughly fairly priced, not cheap.** The reason to own a starter position is not that we found a bargain — it is that after a full adversarial review, nobody produced a specific reason the business breaks, and we have a house rule that "do nothing" requires a named reason, not just an absence of enthusiasm.

## What the market believes

Two different markets believe two different things.

**Sell-side analysts** believe this is a re-rating story. After the August 3 quarter, price targets were raised across the board: Lake Street to $21 (from $14), Oppenheimer to $25 (from $15), Citizens to $21 (from $16), B. Riley to $24 (from $16). The mean sits near $22.75. Their story is that B2 growth plus a raised adjusted-profit guide (27–29% margin, up from 23–25%) plus a marquee AI customer justifies a much higher multiple.

**The tape** does not believe them. The stock is $15.94, about 30% below that mean, down 34% from its 52-week high of $23.99 — while still up 389% from its 52-week low of $3.26. That is not a stock the market has given up on; it is a stock that ran nearly five-fold and then gave a third of it back. The trading crowd knows about this name.

The gap between $15.94 and $22.75 is the market saying: we do not believe the adjusted margin guide, or we do not believe an object-storage company gets a software multiple once it is buying hard drives with borrowed money, or both.

**We side with the tape, not the analysts.** Our number is below theirs. That is an uncomfortable place to write a pitch from, and we are stating it plainly rather than hiding it: our edge here is not "we see upside the street misses." It is closer to "we think the street is wrong in the other direction, and the stock has already discounted most of that."

## Why we might have edge

1. **We priced the financing correctly and most commentary did not.** The headline is "0.00% coupon, free money." That is wrong — $15.2M of cash went to buy the capped call, which is about 8.7% of the gross raise, or roughly 1.6% a year over the note's 5.4-year life, on top of giving away a call on the stock at $21.94. But the correct comparison is not zero. Backblaze's *entire* existing debt was equipment finance leases — $46.7M outstanding, with more than $150M of undrawn lease capacity it chose not to use. Hardware lease financing costs materially more than 1.6% a year. So this raise is a genuine reduction in the company's cost of capital, at exactly the moment when the bearish story about companies like this is "their cost of capital is going up." That is a real, checkable, contrarian point.

2. **We caught that the story everyone tells about the raise is backwards from the story the numbers tell.** The bull framing was "Backblaze escaped the capital-hungry-infrastructure trap." Our own review destroyed that: raising $175M to fund a spend that equals a full year of revenue is the company *entering* that category, not escaping it. We put that in the pitch instead of leaving it out.

3. **We measured the profit guide against stock compensation and the analysts largely have not.** Adjusted EBITDA in the first half was $22.9M, or 28.2% of revenue. Stock compensation was $15.5M, or 19.0% of revenue. Subtract it and the margin is 9.2%. Share count went from 55.6M (mid-2025) to 61.8M (mid-2026) — 11% dilution a year, every year, which is structurally larger than any of the one-time dilution everyone is arguing about. The guide raise from 23–25% to 27–29% is substantially a bigger add-back, not operating leverage.

4. **We are not paying the story premium.** We would be buying below where we think fair value starts, into a name whose analysts are 30% above the tape. If we are right that the multiple has to migrate from software toward infrastructure, we get paid for being early to that view rather than late to the AI-customer headline.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly: mostly, it is.

- Our own fair value range is $16.00–18.50 and the stock is $15.94. That is a 0–16% gap to a range we built ourselves, with a wide-open middle. That is not a mispricing; that is a fair price with a small cushion.
- Everything we know is public. The convert terms were in a press release. The capital-spending guide was in the earnings release. The stock-comp number is in the filing. The CoreWeave deal was announced. There is no private fact here.
- The stock is well covered — six-plus analysts, all of whom moved after the print. This is not an undiscovered name. It ran 4.9x off its low, which means it has been found, traded, and picked over.
- The 34% drawdown is not a dislocation. It is a giveback inside a huge run, and it lines up with a broader cooling in AI-infrastructure-adjacent stocks. We are not buying a mistake; we are buying a de-rating that may not be finished.
- A convertible bond issue creates mechanical selling — arbitrage funds short the stock against the bonds — but that supply gets absorbed over days, not quarters. It explains part of the recent move and it does not create a lasting discount.

**The honest version: this is not an alpha idea. It is a fairly-priced position in a business we now understand well, entered small and below the market, with clearly dated tests coming that could turn it into a real position or kill it.**

## What could go wrong

### Failure narrative 1 — The multiple keeps migrating, and $16 was not the floor (most likely)

We think a storage company spending 100% of revenue on equipment should trade at 4.5–5.5x forward revenue instead of a software multiple. But multiples do not stop politely at the number in our model. Once depreciation from ~$100M of new drives starts landing — roughly $20–33M a year beginning in Q4, on a three-to-five-year asset life — the GAAP loss (already $11.2M in the first half) widens structurally while the company keeps pointing at adjusted EBITDA. If the market decides the right comparison is 2–3x revenue, like a colocation or hosting company, the stock is $9–12. Nothing about our thesis prevents this; our fair value is a judgment about where the re-rating stops, and we have no strong basis for that judgment.

*Tripwire exists:* GAAP depreciation and net loss in the Q3 and Q4 reports; whether the company starts reporting GAAP free cash flow at the September 9 investor day.

### Failure narrative 2 — CoreWeave and Backblaze fail together

Backblaze is spending roughly $100M of capital in the second half of 2026 to serve a customer whose contractual minimums do not begin until mid-2027. That is three quarters of spending ahead of any binding obligation. That same customer holds 4,194,876 warrants struck at $7.60 — deeply in the money at $15.94 — which were registered for resale in a prospectus that went effective August 14–17, right before this raise ([424B3, filed 2026-08-17](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1462056/000114036126033378/ny20078922x3_424b3.htm)). That is about 6.8% of shares outstanding, in two tranches (3,053,314 already issued, 1,141,562 contingent).

So if CoreWeave hits funding trouble, three bad things happen at once and they are the *same* event: the new equipment is stranded, the receivable is impaired, and the largest strategic holder becomes a forced seller of a position with essentially zero cost basis. This is wrong-way financing — the collateral, the customer, and the shareholder all point the same direction.

*Tripwire exists, partially:* CoreWeave's own stock and credit; ownership filings showing warrant sales.

### Failure narrative 3 — A term inside the CoreWeave contract that we have never seen (NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE)

We cannot see the contract. Backblaze discloses annual recurring revenue and net revenue retention — it does **not** disclose remaining performance obligations, which is the one number that would tell us how much of the CoreWeave arrangement is actually committed backlog versus an intention to buy. The deal size itself circulates in two forms ($335M gross, $313M net of warrant value) and we cannot reconcile them from filings.

That means a termination-for-convenience clause, a capacity-reservation the customer can walk from, a price step-down schedule, a most-favored-nation pricing term, or a minimum that resets rather than accrues could each void the economics of the whole build — and we would find out only when revenue failed to appear, months after the capital was spent. There is no filing, no price level, no data feed that tells us in advance.

**Unknown-unknown class: private-contract opacity.** The load-bearing fact of this thesis lives in a document we have no right to read, in a company that has chosen not to disclose the standard metric (remaining performance obligations) that would summarize it. Everything we "know" about the CoreWeave relationship is management characterization. We should treat the entire revenue ramp as management-asserted rather than verified, and size accordingly.

### The reflexive and structural leg

**Our own size:** the proposal is 0.5% of the book — small in dollar terms. But we have **not measured Backblaze's average daily volume in this review** (the live market-data connection was unavailable, twice, to both benches and again just now). We therefore cannot state our footprint as a percentage of daily volume, and we are not entitled to assume it is negligible on a $985M-market-cap name that just had a convertible issue. This is a genuine gap in a position we are proposing to open.

**Predicted flow, and the trap in our own entry plan:** we are laddering bids *below* the market at $14.75 / $13.80 / $12.60 specifically because we know CoreWeave has a live, registered, unsold, deep-in-the-money stake it can distribute at any time. That is the right instinct — do not pay up in front of known supply. But the reflexive risk is the mirror image: **the only reliable way our lower rungs fill is if CoreWeave is actually selling.** In that case every fill we get is a fill from the best-informed holder of the stock, on the way down, for a reason we will learn about later. Our entry design converts us into the natural buyer for the one seller we should least want to trade against. We accept this consciously; it is not an oversight, and it is the single most uncomfortable structural feature of the trade.

**Doctrine interactions:** this position exists partly because of a house rule, not conviction. Our convention is that after a clean adversarial review with no surviving fatal objection, "stay flat" requires a *named* kill — and the review produced none that was not really an argument about size. So the rule manufactured a starter position out of a fair price. If that rule is miscalibrated, the failure mode is exactly this: a portfolio of fairly-priced names nobody actually wanted, each individually defensible. Separately, this would sit in a taxable account and start a short-term holding clock, so any exit inside twelve months is taxed at the top rate — which means a 20% gain that we take in six months is worth roughly 12% after tax, and the trade has to clear that bar, not the pre-tax one.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we anchored on our own fair-value band as if it were a floor, when in fact the market was in the middle of re-pricing Backblaze from a software company to a capital-intensive infrastructure company — and that re-pricing had further to run than either of our benches modeled, with the depreciation from this very buildout providing the evidence that pushes it lower.**
