# PITCH — SUZ — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — do not buy today at $7.86; hold size at zero, arm the sensors listed below, and revisit only if one of three named conditions fires.

---

## What we believe

Suzano is the world's largest and lowest-cost producer of hardwood pulp — the raw material for tissue, paper and packaging. The stock has fallen about 32% from its 52-week high and sits 4% above its 52-week low, because the price of pulp in China is in a trough. The business is not broken: quarterly revenue is R$11.6bn, and adjusted EBITDA actually rose slightly quarter-over-quarter (R$4,705m vs R$4,580m) even though shipped volume fell 11% year-over-year, which is at least consistent with the earnings trough being in.

But we do **not** believe there is a buy here today, and we want to be blunt about why: the thing that made this idea interesting was a headline free-cash-flow yield of about 26%, and that number is wrong. We disproved it ourselves. What is actually left is roughly 4.3% of the market cap in real, recurring cash generation — thin, and largely committed to creditors for the next two to three years.

The honest summary of what survives: a cheap-looking, cyclically-depressed, competently-run commodity producer whose equity is a levered call on pulp prices recovering, at a moment when we have no evidence that they are recovering and no dated event that would tell us.

## What the market believes

The market believes pulp stays low and Suzano stays levered. Sell-side analysts are actually *bullish* — the consensus rating is Buy with a price target of $12.57 — and the stock trades at $7.86, so the market is dissenting from the analysts, not agreeing with them. Consensus earnings estimates for 2027 are 2.3% *below* 2026, and the revenue growth analysts do model (+6.75%) is essentially just the new Arbex joint venture being added to the accounts. In other words, the street models **zero pulp price recovery**, and the market is pricing something worse than that.

The market is also, correctly, worried about the balance sheet. Suzano was at 3.4x net debt to EBITDA, then closed a US$1.3bn cash purchase of 51% of a tissue joint venture with Kimberly-Clark ("Arbex") on July 1st, which brought roughly another US$1.0bn of joint-venture net debt onto the consolidated picture — while simultaneously reaffirming a target of getting net debt to US$11.0bn and below 2.5x leverage in 2027–28.

This is a widely-owned, widely-covered, heavily-traded name. There is no information asymmetry here. The drawdown tracks the pulp price; it is not a mispricing that nobody has noticed.

## Why we might have edge

Three narrow places, and we should not overstate any of them:

1. **We did the cash-flow arithmetic properly and almost nobody else has.** The company reports an "adjusted free cash flow" that explicitly excludes dividends, interest-on-equity payments, buybacks, *and* expansion capital spending. Annualize the best quarter of that and you get ~26%. Take the most hostile possible reading and you get 0.4%. The defensible number — cash generated before shareholder distributions, after all capital spending, and stripping out one-off derivative settlements — is about **R$1,090m for the first half, or ~4.3% annualized**. Neither of our own benches printed that number on the first pass. If the market is anchored on either of the two wrong numbers, we know something.

2. **A specific, falsifiable read on the deleveraging target.** The target requires retiring roughly US$4.1bn of net debt in ten quarters against real cash generation of roughly US$0.9–1.0bn a year. Absent a pulp price recovery, that target is not reachable. That is a testable statement about pulp, and it gives us a clean tripwire rather than a vibe.

3. **A cost-curve claim we can check that others take on faith.** The bull argument industry-wide is that ~30% of global capacity runs below cash cost, so high-cost supply must close and prices must recover. Suzano's own disclosure cuts against the tidy version of that story: its cash cost including production downtime was R$972/tonne versus R$843 excluding it — R$129/tonne of idling cost, against R$6/tonne a year earlier. The self-described lowest-cost producer in the world is the one taking downtime. If we track that number quarterly, we get an early read on whether the supply response is real.

## Why it might be priced in

Most of it probably is, and we should say so.

- The valuation is not obviously cheap once you do it correctly. Enterprise value including the Arbex debt is roughly US$24.7bn against pro-forma EBITDA of roughly US$4.31bn — about **5.7x**, at what is plausibly a cyclical trough. That is a normal trough multiple for a pulp producer, not a distressed one.
- Our own fair-value range straddles the current price. A trough-perpetual case (pro-forma EBITDA × 6.0) gives **$8.80/share**; a mid-cycle case (2Q25 run-rate EBITDA plus Arbex × 6.5) gives **$14.80/share**. The downside case is only ~12% above spot. There is no margin of safety at $7.86.
- The equity is only ~39% of the pro-forma enterprise value. The other 61% is debt. That is a levered bet on a commodity we cannot forecast.
- The stock's decline maps to the pulp price. That is a beta move, not a dislocation. Everyone can see it.
- There is no dated catalyst. The next earnings print appears to be **2026-11-05**, roughly 55 trading days away, and we could not confirm that date with a company source. Between now and then, the only new information is the monthly China pulp spot price.
