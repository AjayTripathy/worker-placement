# PITCH — FMC — PROPOSED VERDICT: NO ENTRY — STAY FLAT; damage is still arriving and the "26% discount to a strategic buyer's price" story fails on dates — **PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION**

## What we believe

FMC (crop-chemicals maker, ~$10.59 last trade, down 74% from its 52-week high and 6% off its low) is a business where the bad news is still landing, not one the market has mispriced. The idea that went to court was seductive: on June 30 the Belgian chemicals group Tessenderlo agreed to buy roughly 20% of FMC at **$13.30 per share**, so a stock at $10.59 looks like either a bet the deal breaks or a 26% free discount to what an informed industrial buyer just paid.

Both of our internal review benches — the one hired to attack the idea and the one hired to check the attacker — converged on the same answer: **that comparison is broken, because the two prices come from different information worlds.** Tessenderlo signed at $13.30 on June 30, when FMC's full-year guidance ($670–730M EBITDA, $1.63–$1.89 EPS) had been reaffirmed on April 29. Twenty-nine days after signing, on July 29, management cut the year to $620–680M EBITDA and $1.19–$1.49 EPS and guided third-quarter EBITDA down ~45%. Today's price reflects that cut; the $13.30 price never saw it. On top of that, Tessenderlo's shares are locked up for 36 months, so the one party who "disagrees with the market" cannot buy the gap even if it wants to.

We further believe there is no acquisition safety net under this stock. The board announced a review of strategic options in February 2026 and it ended not in a sale but in a 20% minority investment. The whole company was effectively shopped, and no full bid cleared.

## What the market believes

The market is pricing a company that: cut its year four weeks after taking strategic money; guided its next quarter's EBITDA down by nearly half; just paid an 8.00% coupon on **$1.2 billion of senior *secured* notes** (lenders demanded collateral — a junk-credit signal, even though the proceeds usefully retire the 3.20% notes that were due October 2026); and still needs roughly $1.2 billion of second-half cash-ins (the $400M Tessenderlo investment plus the India and Newark divestitures) to hit its own $2.6B net debt / ~4.0x leverage target for year-end. At $10.59, the market is saying: the near-term default risk was bought off at a punitive price, but earnings are falling and nobody is coming to buy the company. Our benches found no evidence the market is wrong about any of that.

## Why we might have edge

Honestly: not much, and the court cut the idea's "worth arguing about" score from 7/10 to about 4/10. What genuinely remains open, where careful work could get ahead of the crowd:

1. **The close calendar.** If Tessenderlo's investment actually closes (the tell would be a Schedule 13D filing — a 20% owner with a board seat must file one), and the India and Newark sales close, the balance-sheet repair is real and checkable before the market fully credits it.
2. **The purchase agreement itself.** Neither bench could read the actual contract (SEC.gov blocked our fetches; we worked from a secondary mirror). If the agreement turns out to have a near-unconditional close — no material-adverse-change escape, a hard deadline — that materially raises the odds the $400M arrives.
3. **Product mix.** The bull case's strongest surviving thread is growth in FMC's differentiated newer insecticide products; two more quarters of that mix growing ~35% with pricing on the older diamide products stabilized would change the earnings trajectory story.

## Why it might be priced in

The core of the court's finding is that the market isn't missing anything — *we* almost did. The stock's decline from the June 30 signing to today tracks the July 29 guidance cut. The "strategic buyer paid $13.30" anchor is stale information. The financing that "fixed" solvency was priced by lenders at 8% with first-priority liens across five countries — credit risk *priced*, not disproved. And even the deleveraging plan, if it works perfectly, lands at ~4.0x leverage against an EBITDA base that is still shrinking — if 2027 EBITDA drifts to $600M or below, leverage creeps back up with no asset sales left to pull. There is also no takeover put: the change-of-control carve-outs in the Tessenderlo covenants leave the door legally open, but the February–June strategic review already tested that door and nobody walked through at a price the board would take.
