## What we believe

Workday sells HR and finance software priced substantially by **how many employees its customers have**. The company says so itself: subscription revenue "is driven primarily by the number of customers, **the number of workers at each customer**, the specific applications subscribed to by each customer, and the price of our applications" ([FY26 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1327811/000132781126000014/wday-20260131.htm)). Subscriptions are 92% of revenue. That makes it the most direct way to be long or short "how many white-collar workers exist."

We believe three things, in order of confidence:

1. **Customers are signing shorter contracts.** Money booked for delivery beyond the next twelve months has grown more slowly every single quarter for eleven straight quarters — from +35.7% to **+8.8%**. Money booked beyond *twenty-four* months has gone from +48.6% (peak, July 2023) to **+5.2%**. Not one reversal in either series. Meanwhile the near-term book (next twelve months) has grown at a steady 15.5–17.6% the whole time. So the entire slowdown is contract *length*, not contract *volume*.
2. **This is a confidence problem, not yet a headcount problem.** A customer who renews for two years instead of three, at the same annual price, produces identical revenue and an identical near-term book. Revenue only breaks if they don't renew — and 97% of revenue does renew. The average contract went from about 3.28 years to about 3.10 years. That is roughly **two months** off a three-year deal.
3. **The stock is expensive once you charge stock compensation as a real cost.** This is the finding that actually decided the case, and it came from the team that was *defending* the stock. Charged properly, Workday trades around **30.5x** against comparable software companies at **12.8–16.5x** — roughly double. Nothing in the growth numbers justifies double.

Conclusion: a real but modest and slow-moving negative, attached to a price that leaves no room for it. We pass.

## What the market believes

The market believes the drawdown already happened. The stock fell about 56% peak-to-trough and is still 20.6% below its 52-week high — but it is also **79.8% above its 52-week low of $110.36**, and it has risen 14.7% in the two weeks since we declined. That is a market that has decided the worst-case "AI eliminates the seats" story is not happening, and is paying for margin expansion instead: management raised the FY27 non-GAAP operating margin target to 30.5% and reaffirmed subscription revenue of $9.925–9.950B (+12–13%) ([Q1 FY27 results release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1327811/000132781126000024/wday-04302026x991.htm)).

The market is not wrong about the timing. Revenue has not broken. Subscription growth bottomed at 13.4% in Q1 FY26 and printed 14.3% in Q1 FY27 — it went *up*, not down.

## Why we might have edge

- **The eleven-quarter monotonic series is not a thing most people compute.** The beyond-twelve-month figure isn't published; you get it by subtracting the twelve-month book from the total. The beyond-twenty-four-month figure requires going into the revenue-recognition footnote of the 10-Q, not the press release. Almost no one carries both series back seventeen quarters. We do, and it shows a decay with zero reversals — which is unusual enough that it is unlikely to be renewal-cycle noise.
- **The single retention number Workday publishes cannot detect the thing we care about.** Workday's "gross revenue retention rate" (97%, down from 98%) explicitly "does not account for additional revenue earned from add-ons or net expansions, **which include volume and price adjustments**." Volume = worker count. A customer who renews with 20% fewer employees still scores ~100% on that metric. So the headline retention number is structurally blind to seat shrinkage. That's a real analytical limitation — though, as noted below, Workday discloses the limitation itself, which is the opposite of hiding it.
- **Stock compensation is running around 19–21% of revenue and the market quotes the stock on numbers that exclude it.** From the audited filings: $459M of stock comp in the April-2025 quarter against $2.240B of revenue = **20.5%**; a year earlier, $385M against $1.990B = **19.3%**. It is rising, not falling. Any "13–15x cash flow" framing that ignores this is not measuring the business.

## Why it might be priced in

We have to be blunt here, because this is the stronger side.

- **The timing kills the story we started with.** We came at this as "AI agents are displacing seats, and it's arriving now." The contract-length decay turned in **July 2023** — about two years before "agentic" entered the conversation. And Workday named the seat problem out loud in **May 2024**: "Our updated subscription revenue guidance reflects the elevated sales scrutiny and **lower customer headcount growth** we experienced during the quarter" ([Q1 FY25 release, 2024-05-23](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1327811/000132781124000089/wday-04302024x991.htm)). That is a 27-month-old, company-disclosed fact. The market has had eleven quarters and a 56% decline to absorb it. It is not our discovery.
- **The seat base is still growing.** Users under contract: 65 million (FY24) → 70M (FY25) → 75M (FY26) → **80M+ (Q1 FY27)**. Coarse, rounded to the nearest 5 million, but it is going the wrong way for the bear case and Workday volunteers it every quarter.
- **The company disclosed the deterioration in the same filings we accused it of hiding it in.** The FY25 10-K added a sentence naming "reduced growth in headcount level commitments upon renewals of existing customers" — absent from FY23 and FY24. It also added a breakdown (60% of revenue growth from existing customers, 40% from new) from which you can back into a net expansion rate of roughly **108%**. A company running a concealment operation does not add those.
- **Backlog coverage of revenue is unchanged.** Total book divided by trailing revenue: 3.00x (Apr-2024), 3.09x (Apr-2025), **2.99x (Apr-2026)**. Statistically identical to two years ago. The 19% growth quarter was the anomaly, not the baseline.
- **The stock is up 14.7% since we declined.** So far, the decline is losing.
