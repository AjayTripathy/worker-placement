# PITCH — TTI — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION**

---

## What we believe

TETRA Technologies is a ~$700M-revenue oilfield chemicals and water-services company that fell 48% from its 52-week high ($12.50 → $6.53–6.77) while its actual operating business stayed roughly intact. We think the market is pricing the stock as if the profit engine broke. It didn't. What changed is that the company started spending heavily on a new bromine plant in Arkansas, and cash flow got noisy at exactly the wrong moment.

Three things we believe, in order of how much weight they carry:

1. **The profit decline is smaller than the cash-flow decline suggests.** First-half operating cash flow fell from $52.3M to $22.5M — a 57% drop that looks alarming. But two-thirds of that drop ($19.4M of $29.8M) is receivables and payables timing, on revenue that *grew* $11.8M year over year. Money owed to the company rose $15.8M in the half versus $4.5M a year ago; money the company owed fell $6.3M versus rising $1.8M. That is a working-capital swing, not lost earnings. It should reverse.

2. **The core business alone roughly supports today's price.** Three-quarters of segment profit comes from specialty chemicals (calcium chloride, completion fluids, bromine products) — $29.9M of $40.7M in Q2 — not from cyclical oilfield services. Put an 8x multiple on ~$117M of annualized profit, subtract $28.7M of net debt across 148.1M shares, and you get about **$6.13/share from the existing business, before crediting the Arkansas plant or the lithium acreage at all.** The stock is $6.53.

3. **The new plant is probably worth something rather than nothing, and possibly a lot.** The company's own investment-decision release claims a $607M net present value at a 10% discount rate, says initial output will run 25% above what it currently buys from a third party, and says nameplate capacity is double that initial rate. Management said on the Q2 call that even after supplying its new battery-electrolyte customer, it will be at 100% of plant capacity *and still buying bromine on the open market*. If that's true, the plant is not merely replacing a purchase — it's short of its own demand, which means it can sell into a market forecast to grow 50% by 2030.

**Our fair value, after both benches argued: $7.10–$7.90** — roughly $6.13 from the base business, $0.50–$1.30 for Arkansas after a heavy haircut for execution and funding risk, and about $0.50 for a lithium interest (65% of an estimated 585,000 tonnes of lithium-carbonate-equivalent in place, plus a royalty on ~35,000 acres) that the company explicitly excludes from its own 2030 forecast.

That is a **thin discount to a fair price, not a bargain.** We are proposing a small starter position on a limit below the current tape, not a conviction long.

## What the market believes

The market is holding two views at once, and they contradict each other.

**The four sell-side analysts covering the stock** are unanimously positive — four Buy ratings, no Holds, no Sells, average price target $13.13 (range $11.50–$14.00). That target is 101% above the current price. It rests on the company's September 2025 "One TETRA 2030" plan: $1.2–1.3B of revenue, $300–350M of adjusted profit, and over $100M/year of free cash flow starting in 2028.

**The tape disagrees violently.** The stock is down 48% from its high and has fallen 29% below the $9.25 price at which the company sold 12.4 million new shares in June 2026 — three months ago. Buyers of that deal are deeply underwater. The market is saying: we do not believe the 2030 plan, or we do not believe you can fund the path to it without asking us for money again.

What the market appears to actually be pricing:
- **The plant is a two-year cash drain with no disclosed unit economics.** The company has told us "$220M of remaining capital expenditure" and "first production early 2028," but has never published tons per year, revenue per year, or profit per year — only the aggregate $607M NPV figure, whose scope (incremental to the company, or the whole bromine business including today's operations?) is not defined anywhere we could find.
- **The funding is not closed.** $220M remaining less $108M raised in June leaves roughly $100M to come from operations or the credit revolver through 2027. Management has referred to "alternate sources of capital." The market reads that as a second stock sale.
- **The chemicals margin is eroding.** That segment's margin went from 36.9% to 26.4% year over year.

## Why we might have edge

**1. We priced the anchor; the street counted ratings.** Four analysts is thin coverage, not a crowd. The gap between our $7.10–7.90 and their $13.13 has a specific, nameable cause: they are valuing the 2030 plan close to face value; we discount $325M of 2030 profit back four years at 12% and then haircut it for the chance the company doesn't get there. We are not disagreeing with a consensus — we are disagreeing with a *discount rate and an attainment probability*, which is a defensible place to differ.

**2. We separated a cash-timing artifact from an earnings collapse.** The 57% cash-flow drop is the single most quotable number in the quarter and it is two-thirds working capital. Anyone reading the headline metric — or the screen — sees a company whose cash generation halved. The underlying statement says otherwise. This is the kind of thing that gets missed by screens and by first-pass reads, including our own first pass (see below).

**3. The chemicals-versus-services mix is mispriced by comparison.** TETRA gets filed and traded alongside oilfield service companies, which carry roughly 6–7x multiples. Three-quarters of its profit comes from specialty chemicals, which do not trade at 6–7x. Applying the wrong comparison set is exactly how a business gets marked 20–25% too cheap without anyone making an analytical error they'd defend.

**4. The Arkansas capital commitment is smaller and slower than the headline.** The contractual commitments actually on the books at June 30 were $28.7M for construction plus $13.5M for power — $42.2M, not $220M. Q2 spending on the project was $10.9M. The "$37M per quarter structural burn" that a bear would extrapolate from the total is an extrapolation, not a schedule the company has signed.

**5. Two free options nobody is paying for.** The lithium acreage is excluded from management's own 2030 targets. And management stated a good portion of 2027 bromine volume is already contracted.

## Why it might be priced in

We want to be straight about this. **Most of the above is knowable from public documents, and the discount is thin.**

- **A 6–10% discount to our own fair value is not an edge.** $6.53 versus $7.10–7.90 is a 9–17% gap on a fair-value estimate that itself carries a wide band. If we're 10% too optimistic on the multiple, the discount vanishes entirely.
- **The working-capital explanation is available to anyone who opens the cash-flow statement.** It's on the face of the filing. We are not decoding anything.
- **The plant's NPV claim is in a press release.** Every holder has seen $607M. If the market is discounting it, that is a judgment about credibility, not an information gap we've closed.
- **The one thing that genuinely isn't resolved — the ~$100M funding gap — is a risk, not an edge.** Knowing about it doesn't make us money. It just tells us when to be small.
- **We are on the same side as an unanimously bullish street.** Buying here means agreeing with the loudest published view, while the tape has been rejecting that view for months. Our valuation is far below the street's, which is some comfort, but we are directionally aligned with the consensus rather than against it.
- **The stock is not at a low.** It is 47.6% *above* its 52-week low of $4.425. "Down 48% from the high" and "halfway up its own year" are both true; only one of them sounds like an opportunity.

## What could go wrong

### Failure narrative 1 — The second stock sale (most likely, has a tripwire)

The company needs roughly $100M more than it raised in June. It taps the equity market again — at $6, or $5.50, or lower — because the revolver is expensive or lenders balk at funding a pre-production plant. June buyers at $9.25 are already down 29%; a second raise at a materially lower price confirms to everyone that the June deal was priced on a forecast that didn't hold, and the stock re-rates down another 20–30%. Our starter is underwater immediately and the case for adding disappears, because the dilution is permanent while the plant's payoff is still 18 months out.

*Tripwire: any S-3 takedown, 424B prospectus, or 8-K announcing a financing. Doctrine says kill outright on an equity raise priced below $7 — not average down.*

### Failure narrative 2 — The margin decline is structural, not a comparison problem (has a tripwire)

We are treating the chemicals margin drop from 36.9% to 26.4% as the prior year being unusually good (a one-off contract) rather than this year being bad. If instead Chinese bromine oversupply is permanently compressing the spread, then 26.4% is the new normal or a waypoint down. Annualized profit is then $95–105M, not $117M, and the base-business value falls from $6.13 to roughly $5.00–5.60. The Arkansas plant becomes worth *less*, not more, because a plant that substitutes for cheap purchased input saves less money. Both legs of the thesis fail together — which is the dangerous kind of correlation.

*Tripwire: Q3 chemicals margin. But note the trap — our own bear bench set "≥32% in Q3" as the recovery test, which is above the company's own guided 25–30% range. That trigger could never have fired. The honest test is: does Q3 land inside 25–30% and does management reaffirm the range, or does either break?*

### Failure narrative 3 — The plant works on paper and not in the ground (NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE)

This is the one we cannot instrument. The Arkansas project extracts bromine from brine in Union County. Between now and first production in early 2028 there is no revenue, no volume, no margin, and no operating datapoint of any kind. Everything we would learn arrives at commissioning, all at once. The specific things that could be wrong and would not surface until then:

- **Brine chemistry or well performance below assumption.** Concentration, flow rate, reinjection behavior, or co-produced impurity load differ from the pilot data. There is no quarterly disclosure that would reveal this.
- **Total project cost is not $269M.** The company has published "~$220M remaining" but never a total. A 30% overrun is a $70–80M funding hole that appears as a single sentence in a future filing.
- **The $607M NPV is not incremental.** We could not verify whether that figure describes the new plant's contribution or the whole bromine business including today's operations. If it's the latter, the project's marginal value is a fraction of what the release implies, and the number is technically true and analytically useless.
- **The undisclosed third-party supplier.** We do not know who supplies TETRA's purchased bromine, at what volume, or on what contract terms — which means we cannot independently check the "25% above the supply agreement" framing that anchors the capacity math. A customs/bill-of-lading check would identify this counterparty and volume; **we did not run it.**

**Unknown-unknown classes named:** (a) *commissioning-truth risk* — a class of failure where all evidence arrives simultaneously at first production and no intermediate observation exists; (b) *undisclosed-counterparty risk* — a class where the key commercial relationship is unnamed in every public venue, so no verification path exists through the issuer's own disclosure.

### The reflexive and structural leg — our own size, our own flow, our own rules

- **Position size:** 0.25% of book. This is deliberately below the level where our fill affects the print.
- **We could not verify our size against average daily volume.** The IBKR market-data permission was denied in the session that built this deck, and the desk pricing/capacity toolkit was outside the read path. **We have no ADV number and therefore no verified check that 0.25% clears the ≤1% of ADV publishing rule.** This is a gap and must be closed before the order is placed, not after. On a ~$970M market cap with normal turnover, a 0.25% starter is almost certainly fine — but "almost certainly" is not the standard, and stating it as verified would be false.
- **We are bidding below the tape, into a name that is falling.** Our limit at $6.10 is 6.6% below the current mark. If it fills, it fills because the stock kept going down — most likely on financing news, which is precisely the scenario in which we said we would not want to own it. This is the structural trap in every limit-below-market entry: the fill is correlated with the bad outcome. The mitigation is that our kill rule (any raise below $7) fires on the *announcement*, not the price, so a financing-driven fill still gets stopped by the event gate.
- **Doctrine interaction — resting orders into a print.** The Q3 report is expected 2026-10-27. A limit order left resting through an earnings release is an accidental bet that the report is fine. The order carries a hard cancel-by of **2026-10-23**. If it hasn't filled by then, it dies and we re-court after the print.
- **Doctrine interaction — the price argument runs both ways.** Our valuation objection is answered by a lower price ($6.10) but our *funding* objection is not; a cheaper stock makes the funding problem worse, not better, because the same $100M costs more shares. Price alone should not be allowed to clear this name — that is why the entry has an event kill attached and not just a level.
- **Two live price marks disagree.** The evidence pack shows $6.53 stamped at 04:54Z, after the 2026-08-25 close of $6.77 on the public tape. That is an overnight mark, not a session print. We could not reconcile them without broker bar data. The gap is 3.5% and does not change the decision, but every price in this deck should be read as $6.53–6.77, not as a single number.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that TETRA came back to the equity market for the remaining ~$100M of Arkansas funding at a price below our entry — permanently diluting the base-business value we bought while the plant that justifies the dilution is still two years from producing anything.**
