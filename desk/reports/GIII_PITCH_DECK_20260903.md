# PITCH — GIII — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — Buy a half-size starter (0.45% of book) in G-III Apparel at $27.43 or better, but only after one specific number is pulled from the fiscal-2025 annual report; if that number comes back near 34%, this is a value trap and we buy nothing.

---

## What we believe

G-III sells branded clothing wholesale. For years its biggest revenue lines were licensed Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger product, and those licenses are expiring. Fiscal 2026 revenue was about $2.96 billion; roughly 28% of it — about $828 million — was Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger. The company has told the market it loses about $460 million of that in the current fiscal year and the remaining ~$360 million dies on 31 December 2026. Revenue is falling about 9% and will fall again.

Our belief is that **the revenue leaving is close to worthless**, and that the market is pricing the revenue, not the profit.

The evidence: in the quarter just reported, revenue fell $59.2 million year over year and gross profit fell $90 thousand. Not $9 million — ninety thousand dollars. That means the revenue that walked out the door carried an incremental gross margin of about 0.15%. Across the first half, stripping out a one-time $102.8 million tariff-refund credit that sits entirely in the first quarter's cost of goods, revenue fell $106.8 million and gross profit fell $1.7 million — a 1.6% incremental margin. If that is the true economics of the expiring business, then losing the remaining $360 million next year costs almost nothing in profit, and a stock trading at roughly 6.9x enterprise value to EBITDA is not a melting ice cube — it is a company whose sales line is shrinking to reveal the profitable part.

Two supporting facts. First, management **raised** the year's earnings guidance in the middle of the cliff: on 12 March 2026 they guided to $2.00–2.10 of adjusted earnings per share and $158–162 million of adjusted EBITDA; on 2 September 2026 they guided to $2.20–2.30 and $174–178 million — a ~10% earnings raise with the revenue guide unchanged at $2.71 billion and the license loss unchanged. You do not usually raise earnings while losing a third of your revenue unless the revenue you are losing was carrying no margin. Second, the balance sheet: $529.2 million of cash against $7.8 million of reported debt before the Marc Jacobs deal, and inventory down 13%.

Marc Jacobs, closed 1 September 2026, is a separate and mostly unpriced item. G-III put in about $500 million of cash and revolver borrowings, bought the operating business, and holds 50% of a joint venture with WHP Global that owns the trademark; G-III operates under a license running to December 2041 with ten automatic renewals and blocking rights on major decisions. Management explicitly excluded it from guidance and called it "slightly dilutive" in fiscal 2027. We treat it as worth zero this year and as an unpriced option thereafter — which is roughly how the stock is treating it.

At $27.425 and 44.338 million diluted shares, the market capitalization is about $1,216 million. With cash roughly offsetting the deal outlay, enterprise value is about the same. That is 12.2x this year's guided earnings and 6.9x guided EBITDA.

## What the market believes

The market believes it is looking at a licensee losing its licenses. Revenue is down 8.2% in Q1, 9.7% in Q2, and guided down about 12% in Q3. First-half adjusted net income fell 86%, from $19.6 million to $2.8 million — six cents a share — against a full-year promise of $2.20–2.30. That means about 97% of the year's promised profit sits in two quarters that have not happened yet. The company has just spent $500 million of its cash cushion on a brand whose trademark it only half owns, and told investors that purchase makes this year worse, not better. The stock is 26.9% below its 52-week high of $37.54 and 11.9% above its low of $24.52.

Importantly, there is no independent Street view to trade against. Published consensus for fiscal 2027 is $2.20 of earnings on $2.71 billion of revenue — that is the company's own guidance, repeated. And there is no published fiscal-2028 number at all, which is the year that actually matters. So "the market believes" here means "the market has not done the work," not "the market has a thesis we disagree with."

## Why we might have edge

1. **The market is reading the revenue line; the profit line says something different.** The incremental-margin calculation above is two subtractions from a filed income statement. It is not in any note or press release, and the company's own commentary points elsewhere (the CEO talked about "substantial gross margin expansion," which is itself just a ratio effect of losing low-margin sales).
2. **Nobody appears to have priced the raise.** The guidance increase from $2.00–2.10 to $2.20–2.30 happened on the same day as a weak-looking headline quarter, and the comparison requires pulling the March release. Neither of our own benches had established the original baseline until late in the argument.
3. **This is a repeatable pattern, not a one-off.** When a licensee loses a large licensed revenue stream, the earnings damage is set by that stream's *incremental* gross margin, not by its share of sales. We have written that up as a reusable detector. If it works here, it gives us a screen for every license-expiry headline that follows.
4. **The Marc Jacobs structure is better than the "licensee again" reflex suggests.** The obvious bear reading — "they lost Calvin Klein as a licensee and now they're a licensee again" — is wrong on the facts. G-III owns half of the licensor, holds a 15-year initial term with ten automatic renewals, and has blocking rights. That is not the same fragility.

## Why it might be priced in

Stated fairly, and some of this is strong:

1. **The two-quarter incremental margin may be a mix artifact.** Comparing fiscal 2026 to fiscal 2025 at the full-year level implies something closer to a **34%** incremental margin on the revenue that left. Two quarters say ~1%; two years say ~34%. These cannot both be right, and if 34% is right, losing the remaining ~$360 million takes roughly $120 million out of EBITDA, and 6.9x today becomes roughly 14x on fiscal 2028. **This single number decides the entire thesis, and it is not yet verified.** See "What remains unverified."
2. **The fourth quarter has to roughly triple.** Implied Q4 adjusted net income is about $34.7 million against $12.9 million a year ago (a $0.30 quarter) — while Q3 is guided *down* 27%. The defusing context: two years ago that quarter earned $1.27, so the path is $1.27 → $0.30 → ~$0.78 — recovering half of a collapse, not setting a record. Still, it is a promise, not a result.
3. **The fiscal-2028 drop is a step, not a stagger.** Everything material — Calvin Klein women's dresses, suits, handbags and small leather goods, swimwear; Tommy Hilfiger dresses and suits — expires 31 December 2026. Only luggage runs to 31 December 2027. So fiscal 2028 loses the whole residual at once. There is no glide path.
4. **Marc Jacobs is genuinely unquantified.** The royalty rate G-III pays the joint venture is not disclosed anywhere. It first appears in the amended 8-K with audited Marc Jacobs financials and pro formas, due within 71 calendar days — roughly 18 November 2026. Until then, nobody can value the deal, including us. And structurally G-III has permanently given away about half the brand's royalty economics to WHP Global, whose 50% stake becomes transferable after three years.
5. **There is a financing cost nobody has explained.** Net interest and financing charges were $5,966 thousand in the quarter against $304 thousand a year earlier, and $7,140 thousand for the half — and that half-year figure is *net* of $3,085 thousand of interest income earned on the tariff refund, so gross charges are about $10.2 million. Against $7.8 million of reported debt, that does not add up; something (factoring, intra-quarter revolver use) is not visible in the summary financials. About 43% of the first-half earnings decline is this line, not operations.
6. **This has been public since March.** The license expirations were disclosed in the fiscal-2026 10-K and quantified in the March guidance. Six months of public knowledge on a $1.2 billion Nasdaq name with real analyst coverage is a reason for humility about "nobody has done the work."

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — The expiring business was actually profitable (most likely).** The 0.15% and 1.6% incremental margins are artifacts of mix, of the timing of the tariff credit, or of markdowns on exiting inventory, and the true figure is nearer the ~34% the two-year comparison implies. Fiscal 2028 EBITDA falls by roughly $120 million, the multiple re-rates from ~7x to ~14x, and the stock is worth low-$20s or less. *Tripwire: monitorable.* Pull fiscal-2025 gross profit and compute the fiscal-2026 incremental margin — available today from a filed document. Then the fiscal-2028 guide, expected around March 2027, settles it publicly.

**Failure narrative 2 — Marc Jacobs is worse than "slightly dilutive."** The undisclosed royalty rate to the joint venture is high, transition-services costs from LVMH run over, and the brand needs investment before it produces anything. Note the uncomfortable implication of management's own language: a $500 million outlay that is only *slightly* dilutive means year-one operating profit roughly equals the license fee plus financing cost — approximately zero economics for half a billion dollars of cash. If it is worse than slightly, we have paid for a hole. *Tripwire: monitorable.* The amended 8-K around 18 November 2026 gives us the royalty rate, minimum royalties, and audited financials; the December print gives the first guidance that includes the business.

**Failure narrative 3 — Demand at the brands we keep quietly rolls over, and we find out at the print (NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE).** The entire thesis assumes the "go-forward" portfolio — DKNY, Karl Lagerfeld, Donna Karan, Marc Jacobs — grows high single digits as management says. We have no independent read on that. Wholesale sell-through is not public; order books are private commercial terms that never get filed; and department-store partners disclose nothing at brand level. The one demand sensor we ran, consumer product heat, scored Karl Lagerfeld as *decaying* when the 10-K shows it grew from $475 million to $580 million to $630 million — the sensor's direction is refuted where we can audit it, so it is not usable here. A second sensor (app-review velocity for the brand apps) was never run. Between now and December we are blind: revenue could deteriorate for three months with no observable signal on our side, and the first evidence would be the print itself, after the price has moved. **Unknown-unknown class: uninstrumented consumer demand — private commercial terms and non-filing counterparties, where our only would-be sensor is calibration-failed for apparel wholesale.** A related member of the same class: the $102.8 million tariff refund credited to cost of goods rests on a legal outcome whose appellate path and accounting reversibility we have not traced.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Our own trading size is not the problem: a 0.45% starter in a $1.2 billion Nasdaq-listed name is small under any plausible daily volume. We should say plainly that we could not verify daily volume, short interest, or how crowded the name is — the brokerage market-data calls were permission-denied to both benches, so that check is simply missing. The real structural risks are on our side of the desk:

- **We would be bidding ahead of a flow we ourselves predict.** The material license expirations hit 31 December 2026. Mechanical screens and index-adjacent models will mark the fiscal-2028 revenue step down in the December-to-March window. That argues for keeping our adds *below* today's price rather than in front of it — we should not be the buyer that absorbs a de-rating we expect.
- **Our doctrines conflict here, and we should name which one wins.** The "clean court, minimum starter" default says a court with no fatal finding gets a starter. The "verify before you advance" rule says we do not add exposure while the single load-bearing number is unverified and cheaply obtainable. The second one wins: the check costs one document pull. This is why our proposal differs from the blue bench's "starter now."
- **The pay-up allowance does not apply.** That allowance is for names whose fair value we have verified. Here the fair value hinges on an unverified quantity, so we buy at or below our band, never through it.
- **Tax.** New position, no existing holding, no wash-sale interaction. Any exit inside twelve months is short-term; the natural holding period runs through the March 2027 fiscal-2028 guide, which is past a one-year clock only if we buy now and hold into late 2027.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that the ~$360 million of expiring Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger business carried real gross margin — nearer the ~34% the two-year comparison implies than the ~1% the last two quarters imply — so what looks like 6.9x today is really about 14x on fiscal 2028, while the $500 million spent on Marc Jacobs replaces none of it in time.

**Where our own benches erred, plainly.** The red bench recommended rejection and built its strongest kill on three findings, two of which failed: it read flat gross profit dollars as bearish when the same fact is the most bullish datum in the release; and it hand-derived a fiscal-2026 first-quarter revenue of $609.7 million when the filings imply $583.6 million, a $26 million error that turned a −2.8% Q4 guide into a fabricated +0.2% "inflection." It also argued Q4 faces "more Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger gone" when the expiry table shows Q4 retains two of three months. It cited the consumer-heat sensor as corroboration without auditing it. Its detector list omitted two required entries and included one not in scope. Its price was wrong.

The red bench was right, and we should say so, on: the residual license revenue being ~$360M (the originating brief was 2x wrong in the bullish direction); the guidance being a stub that excludes Marc Jacobs; the unexplained financing charge; and its own honest ratchet-check overturning a kill on its own side.

The blue bench was right on the arithmetic but overreached on the conclusion: it recommended a starter now while simultaneously naming an unrun check that it admitted could flip the thesis to a value trap. We do not buy in front of an unrun, obtainable, thesis-deciding check.
