# PITCH — OPEN — VERDICT

**ADJUDICATED 2026-08-08 — DECLINE both directions: no long at 3.70x book with negative cash carry after $29M/quarter of property-financing interest and a crowded meme shareholder base; and no short, because the borrow and the squeeze risk make betting against it a losing trade even if we are right about the business.**

---

## What we believe

Opendoor buys houses with borrowed money, holds them, and resells them. Today it owns about $1.85bn of houses financed with about $1.77bn of asset-backed debt — roughly 96 cents of debt against every dollar of house. The equity slice under the inventory is about $79M, or 4.3%. A 5% move in home values is larger than that cushion.

The company's headline profit measure ("contribution profit") is calculated *before* the interest it pays to finance those houses. Last quarter contribution profit was $51M and the interest bill was $29M. So the margin the company markets is roughly double the margin it actually earns. On the bottom line the quarter was a $162M net loss.

The direction of travel in dollars is down, not up. Revenue was $883M versus $1,567M a year ago (−44%). Contribution profit was $51M versus $69M (−26%). Adjusted EBITDA was $(4)M versus $+23M. The percentages improved; every dollar figure shrank. Meanwhile the company bought 4,378 homes and sold 2,339 — it is re-accelerating purchases into 6.66% mortgage rates.

The stock is about $3.36–$3.49 against book value of $914M and 971,091,050 shares — roughly $0.94 of book per share. That is 3.70x book for a business that loses money after its own funding costs. The stock is also still about 6.6x above where it traded in June 2025 before a retail squeeze took it from $0.53 to $7.97.

Our conclusion: this is not a company about to go bust, and it is not a company worth 3.70x book. It is a levered house-flipper priced on attention. We do nothing.

## What the market believes

The market believes Opendoor is inflecting. Gross margin rose from 8.2% to 9.7% year over year and contribution margin from 4.4% to 5.8%. Management has guided publicly to a Q3 contribution margin of 4–4.5% and framed the quarter as broadly improving. A large retail following has adopted the name, and management has just financed off that enthusiasm: on August 12, 2026 the company agreed to sell **$650M of 0.00% convertible senior notes due 2030** — zero cash coupon, funded entirely by the option value of an elevated share price ([8-K, 2026-08-13](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1801169/000180116926000019/)).

That is a real bull point, and we should say it plainly: a company that can raise $650M at a 0% coupon does not have a near-term financing problem.

## Why we might have edge

Three things the tape appears not to be pricing:

1. **The advertised margin excludes the biggest cost.** Contribution margin is stated before property financing interest. Deduct the $29M/quarter and the economics are close to break-even at best. Most coverage of this name quotes the pre-interest figure.
2. **The leverage is at the asset level, not the corporate level, so it hides in plain sight.** Screens that look at "corporate debt vs cash" see $197M of converts against $896M of cash and conclude the balance sheet is fine. The $1.77bn of warehouse and term facilities — including $350M at a 12.50% fixed rate — sits one layer down. The facilities carry borrowing-base deficiency provisions: if collateral value falls or homes age, the company must post more houses or repay. That pulls corporate cash into the asset book without any "wall" ever appearing on a maturity schedule.
3. **Percentages up, dollars down.** Every improving statistic is a ratio. Every declining statistic is a dollar. That asymmetry is exactly the shape of a shrinking book with better unit selection, not a growing business.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly: mostly, yes.

- The Q3 inflection is *publicly guided*. There is nothing to front-run.
- The leverage is disclosed in the 10-Q debt note. We did not find it; we read it.
- The name is heavily covered and heavily owned by a retail base that is not valuation-sensitive. Our formal crowding read was permission-blocked in both benches — we are inferring crowding from the price history, not measuring it.
- Our bearish view has no catalyst we control. The mechanism (negative carry, borrowing-base top-ups) grinds; it does not break.

Which is why the answer is "no position" rather than "short."

## PROPOSED ENTRY BANDS

**No entry. No position, long or short. Size: 0% of book.** There are no adds and no trims, because there is nothing to add to or trim.

The court defines only reopen triggers, and these are reproduced exactly as adjudicated — we have not invented levels around them:

- **price ≤ ~1.2x book (~$1.15) → revisit**
- **Q3 (~early Nov): contribution dollars up YoY after property-financing interest → revisit**

Supporting arithmetic, so the reader can check the level: book value $914M ÷ 971,091,050 shares = **$0.94 of book per share**; 1.2x that is **$1.13**, which the court rounds to ~$1.15. From $3.36 that is a **−66%** move. We are not predicting it; we are stating the price at which the leverage and the negative carry would be paid for rather than paid up for.

Explicitly **not** a reopen trigger: a higher margin *percentage*, a rising share price, or another financing at a low coupon.

**Catalyst dates:**
- **2026-11-05 — Q3 2026 print.** Derived from a data vendor, **not confirmed by a company press release.** Treat as "~early November" until the company names it.
- **~2026-08-19 — indenture expected to be dated for the $650M 0.00% convertible notes due 2030.** The 8-K says "expected to be dated on or around August 19, 2026," so this is a company expectation, not a fixed date.

**Why no short**, stated plainly: the borrow is expensive, the retail base is squeeze-prone, the company has just demonstrated it can raise $650M at a 0% coupon, and management is publicly guiding to an inflection. Being right about the economics and wrong about the flow is how a bear loses money in this name.
