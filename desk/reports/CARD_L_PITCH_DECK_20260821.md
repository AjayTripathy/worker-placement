# PITCH — CARD.L (Card Factory plc) — ADJUDICATED  — STARTER

**ADJUDICATED  — STARTER** — buy the smallest real position at market (~75.5p), label it *carry, not insight*, add nothing at any price before the 29 September 2026 interim results, and pull the resting 68p buy order immediately because it sits underneath the company's own share buyback and can only fill on bad news. Conviction 7.5/10.

---

## What we believe

Card Factory is a UK value greeting-card retailer that throws off a lot of cash relative to what it costs, and the stock has fallen far enough (−34.7% from its 52-week high, +29.5% off its low) that you are paid a large amount of cash to own a business that is not growing profits.

The specific numbers we think are true, all taken from the company's own audited full-year statement published 28 April 2026:

- Revenue £582.7m, up 7.4%.
- Free cash flow **£40.7m**, defined by the company as operating cash flow (£110.3m) minus lease repayments (£45.7m) minus capital spending (£19.4m). Rent is paid *inside* that number, so it is genuinely spare cash.
- That £40.7m against a market value of roughly £260m (about 345m shares at 75.5p) is a ~15.7% cash yield on last year — but last year was flattered, and the company has told us so (see below). On the company's own guidance for this year, the honest number is **~£29–31m, or ~11.5%**.
- Cash actually returned to shareholders last year: a 5.0p dividend (6.6%) plus a £15m buyback (5.8%) = **12.4%**, funded without borrowing.
- Debt is modest: £67.9m of bank net debt against £116.8m of EBITDA, roughly 0.6x. Not distress.

So the belief is narrow and boring: **you are being paid an 11–12% cash return to wait, on a balance sheet that will not break, while profits go sideways-to-down.** That is worth a minimum position. It is not worth a big one, and it is emphatically not the "debt-free compounder" story the original write-up told.

## What the market believes

The market believes profits are going backwards and there is no reason to pay more.

It is right about the first half of that. Revenue grew 7.4% but adjusted pre-tax profit fell 15.2% to £56.0m, and statutory pre-tax profit fell 31.5% to £43.9m. The cause is identified and quantified by the company: roughly **£15m of extra UK minimum-wage and National Insurance cost from April 2025**, partly offset by cutting store hours 9%. This is a labour-heavy business selling a £1.50 average card; small cost increases eat a large share of the profit per item.

Analysts forecast this year's adjusted pre-tax profit at £54.8m–£60.5m (average £58.2m), and the company itself says it expects to land "in line with the current market consensus." Berenberg cut the stock from Buy to Hold and cut its price target from 110p to 80p.

Two things follow. First, **we do not disagree with the market's profit forecast** — we have no better number, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Second, at 75.5p the shares are trading *below* even the downgraded 80p target, so the market is not merely agreeing with the bears; it has run past them.

## Why we might have edge

Four things, none of them heroic:

1. **The cash is real and the arithmetic closes.** We rebuilt the year: cash out was Funky Pigeon (£27.8m) + dividend (~£17m) + a £5.0m December buyback ≈ £49.8m, against £40.7m of free cash flow, implying debt should rise ~£9.1m. It rose £9.0m. The bridge closes to £0.1m. That is a strong test that the free-cash-flow figure is not an accounting optic — and neither the sell-side note nor our own first pass ran it.

2. **The growth test at the next print is genuinely clean, and we were the ones who worked out why.** Card Factory's wholesale/partnerships division grew from £22.2m to £47.2m, and the statement warns the figure "includes annualisation of prior-year acquisitions of Garven and Garlanna." But both deals closed in the second half of the prior financial year (Garlanna 4 Sep 2024, Garven 4 Dec 2024), so the acquisition step was already fully absorbed into the *last* half-year (£6.6m → £16.5m). This September's half-year comparison is therefore organic. It can fail. A test that can fail is worth having.

3. **The shares are below the downgraded broker target,** and the total cash return (12.4%) exceeds what the equity market typically pays for a no-growth business with modest debt. The value case does not require a re-rating; a re-rating is the option on top.

4. **Two new pieces of growth evidence exist that the bear case did not account for:** a **first US wholesale agreement** and a multi-year extension with The Reject Shop (Australia/NZ), both disclosed in the April 2026 statement.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly and at full strength:

- **The yield is decaying, by the company's own instruction.** Cash conversion was 98.9% last year against a stated 70–80% target, and management says this year will be "at the lower end" of that range on higher capital spending. Roughly a quarter of the cash yield disappears on guidance alone. Both of our internal benches quoted the flattered year.
- **The cost pressure is structural, not one-off.** The company quantified last year's wage hit at ~£15m but **declined to quantify this year's**. That silence is itself information, and it is not friendly.
- **The next print will look bad on the headline.** The first half is Card Factory's Christmas stock build and its debt peak. Net debt was £74.9m at the 2024 half, £78.9m at the 2025 half. Rolling £67.9m forward with ~£20m of seasonal build plus ~£10–15m of buyback spend gives **~£95–103m at 31 July 2026** — a large, ugly, entirely predictable number. And the half-year profit is small and was falling last time (£13.2m vs £14.5m, about 24% of the full year); the shares fell ~5% on that print.
- **There is no gap between our forecast and the street's,** because we do not have a forecast. The whole return is carry plus a possible re-rate.
- **A 50% gain requires ~113p, about £390m, or a 7.8% forward cash yield** — a rating that flat-profit UK value retailers do not sustain. The realistic prize is the coupon, not a double.
- **We cannot see inside this business before the print.** Our one external early-warning tool for hiring came back contaminated (see table). The print is genuinely un-anticipatable, which is another reason to stay small.

## What remains unverified

- **Whether we hold anything.** The evidence pack says no position and no orders; both benches worked from 5,000 shares and a 2,500 @ 68p resting order. Unresolved, and it changes what "cancel the order" means. Check the broker first.
- **The organic split of partnerships revenue.** The company has never disclosed it. Our whole growth test depends on the company giving us a figure it has not previously given.
- **This year's wage and National Insurance cost.** Unquantified by the company. This is the largest single driver of the profit decline and we are guessing at it.
- **The Funky Pigeon £5m synergy claim.** No independent evidence gathered. The one tool that could test it was not run.
- **The Reject Shop / Dollarama supplier review.** Flagged by the defence bench, no primary document read.
- **Consensus figures and the Berenberg downgrade** come from secondary sources (news aggregation), not from the broker note itself. Directionally reliable, not citable as primary.
- **Buyback programme status.** We know roughly £10–15m of a £15m programme appears executed; we have not confirmed completion, and completion timing determines when the price support ends.
- **Exact share count and treasury holding**, which are moving weekly as repurchased shares are cancelled. Our ~345.0m figure is a point estimate; every per-share number carries that uncertainty.
- **Cash conversion for the current year.** Guided to "the lower end" of 70–80%. If it prints below 70%, the only leg of this thesis is gone. This is the single risk that most threatens the position, and it is the defence bench's own stated residual doubt.
