## What we believe

Alphatec sells spine implants and the instruments, imaging and nerve-monitoring that go with them. Its differentiating pitch is a way of doing lateral spine fusion with the patient face-down instead of on their side, which lets the surgeon do the front and back of the operation without flipping the patient.

Three things we believe:

**1. The business is genuinely growing and the growth is real revenue, not accounting.** Q2 2026 revenue was $213.5M, up 15%, with surgical revenue of $196M up 17% and case volume up 20% ([8-K Exhibit 99.1, 2026-08-04](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1350653/000119312526332870/atec-ex99_1.htm)). Those are audited-adjacent, filed numbers. Surgeons are switching to this company.

**2. The profit improvement is not a stock-compensation trick, and the sharpest argument against this name got that backwards.** Our red bench's headline kill was that the margin expansion is just add-back of stock comp. We checked it against the company's own reconciliation. Stock comp went from $15.6M to $18.1M — up 16%, against revenue up 15%. It is flat as a share of sales. So earnings *before* the add-back went from $7.8M to $17.8M, more than doubling, and the margin improvement excluding stock comp (+409 basis points) is within a rounding error of the headline improvement (+420bp). The stock comp did not cause the inflection. It is large in level, and that is a separate and legitimate complaint, but the causal claim was inverted.

**3. It is the cheapest thing in its own peer group on the only multiple that works here.** At $9.425 the equity is worth about $1.46B; add roughly $457M of net debt and enterprise value is about $1.91B — 2.17x this year's guided revenue, 2.35x trailing. Globus Medical trades near 3.5x growing under 7%. SI-Bone trades near 3.7x with negative earnings. Alphatec is the cheapest of the three and grows fastest. Nobody on either bench had pulled that comparison until adjudication forced it.

**What we are not claiming:** that this is a bargain. It is a fairly-priced, capital-hungry, levered compounder that the market has already punished — down 60% from its 52-week high of $23.29 — and there is a real chance it deserved it. That is why the position is a half-percent and not a real one.

## What the market believes

The market believes two things at once, and the tension is the whole setup.

**Sell-side analysts are bullish:** 14 covering, a "Strong Buy" consensus, average target of $15.79 — about 70% above the current price. Institutions own roughly 76%.

**Actual traders are not:** the stock fell 7.5% over the two days around the August print (close $10.15 → $10.35 → $9.57) and has drifted lower since. It fell 20-32% in May on a revenue miss and a guidance cut, and the company has now cut its 2026 outlook twice — from about $890M and 17% growth in January down to $882M and 15% growth in August. Short interest is 11.2% of the float, just under six days of average volume.

So the market's real position is: *the story is fine, the cash isn't, and the guidance keeps going the wrong way.* That is a fair reading and we largely agree with it. Where we differ is on what price that reading justifies.

## Why we might have edge

**The specific edge is arithmetic, not information.** Sell-side models the company's own "adjusted profit" figure of about $140M for 2026. Strip out the ~$81M of annual stock compensation embedded in it, and the cash version is about $59M — against capital spending running near $64M a year. On that basis the enterprise trades at roughly 32x cash earnings, not the 14x the original thesis claimed and not the 50x the red bench claimed. Nobody in the published coverage set decomposes it this way. That gap between the number the street models and the number the business generates is a real, mechanically-explained divergence.

Note what that edge actually implies: it is a *bearish* correction that still lands on a buy. Being right about how expensive it really is, and finding it still cheapest in its group with the fastest growth, is a narrower and more defensible claim than the original bull case.

**Second, we have a documented tendency to be too conservative.** Our own red bench built a valuation band topping out at $8.13 — 12% *below* where the stock actually trades — and then declared the stock overvalued. Our desk has a measured ~13% low bias in fair-value work. Corrected, red's own band lands at roughly $9.19, essentially at the tape. A rejection whose own fair value equals the market price is not a rejection; it is a statement that the stock is fairly priced. That is what we acted on.

**Third, we cleared the integrity question against our own interest.** Six law firms announced investigations in May and June 2026. Our red bench opened the Stanford securities case page everyone was pointing at and found it is the *2010* Alphatec case — class period April to August 2010, dismissed with prejudice in 2013. There is no 2026 complaint on that docket and none disclosed in the 10-Q's legal note. A bench that only prosecutes is useless; this one cleared a flag that would have helped its own case.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly: quite possibly all of it.

- **The bull case is consensus.** A Strong Buy with a $15.79 target is not a lonely position. We would be joining the crowd, not fading it.
- **The cash story is public.** Anyone can compute trailing free cash flow of $2.3M on $815M of revenue — 0.28% — and see that it is *falling* (it was $6.9M a quarter ago).
- **The market already sold the print.** The de-rating is not a pending event we are anticipating; it happened, twice, and the second time the company cut guidance again. The damage is arrived, not absent. Our blue bench made this point cleanly and it is correct.
- **Cheapest-in-peer-group is a weak edge.** It is the most-crowded value argument in existence and it is often cheapest for a reason — here, negative book equity of −$12.1M, an accumulated deficit of $1.48B, and 5% annual share issuance.

The best statement of the bear case is not that the numbers are bad; it is that *the numbers are known and the discount is deliberate.* We do not have a good answer to that. We have a small position instead.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — The slow bleed: growth that is bought, never earned.** *(Monitorable.)* This company buys case volume with capital — surgical instrument sets placed with surgeons. In the first half of 2026 it added $50.9M of revenue and $21M of adjusted profit and its free cash flow got *worse*: −$10.2M versus −$9.7M the prior year, with capital spending up 58% while revenue grew 14%. If that ratio never inverts, revenue compounds forever and cash never does, and the stock is a permanent 2x-sales asset that slowly dilutes. **Tripwire:** capital spending as a share of revenue, and quarterly free cash flow, at each print. This one we can see.

The one mitigating fact: our blue bench showed the year-over-year comparison is contaminated. The company refinanced on May 1, 2026 and repaid $63.3M of converts on July 30, so the 2026 half absorbs $22.7M of interest against a lower prior-year base. Netting the refinancing-driven interest out, operating cash conversion actually improved about $12M — and capital spending consumed all of it. The mechanism is real; "zero improvement" was an artifact.

**Failure narrative 2 — Price per case erodes permanently and there is no evidence pipeline to stop it.** *(Half-monitorable, and the sensor is broken.)* Revenue per case fell 2.7% year over year and the company cut its full-year outlook for that metric from flat to a low-single-digit decline. Management attributes about three-quarters of the shortfall to biologics — bone graft tissue, which is a commodity. The bull answer is that the surgical technique commands a price premium. The independent literature does not support that: the pooled meta-analysis of 15 studies finds the face-down approach improves operating time and length of stay but is statistically *null* on complications (P=0.64) and on segmental lordosis (P=0.28), and the evidence grade across those studies is 10 low / 4 moderate / 1 very low. And the company has never registered a single sponsor trial of the procedure that carries its pricing claim; its one nerve-monitoring study is 22 months past its own completion date with no results, single-site at headquarters. There is no mechanism by which this fork resolves in our favor.

**Why the tripwire is broken:** revenue per case appears *nowhere* in the 10-Q and *nowhere* in the 8-K press release. Neither filed document contains the phrase. It exists only in the earnings call, is defined by the company, and has an undisclosed denominator. Our armed price stop is explicitly conditioned on "revenue-per-case deterioration" — which means it is conditioned on a sentence management chooses to say. That stop will either never fire or will fire on narrative. **This needs re-conditioning on filed metrics (EOS revenue and capital-spending-adjusted free cash flow) before it is trusted.**

**Failure narrative 3 — NO TRIPWIRE EXISTS. The case-volume number itself is contaminated at source.** The specific allegation running through the six investigation notices is not generic: the company said it had "committed to a number of units" it "did not fulfill." That is a channel/distribution allegation. If it is true, then the metric doing all the work in this thesis — 20% case volume growth — has a denominator we cannot see, produced by a company that discloses it only in a transcript. There is no filing, no scheduled disclosure, and no data source in our sensor bank that would reveal this before a complaint is filed or a restatement lands. We ran fourteen detectors on this name; twelve did not fire, and the two that did (the disclosure-venue check and the clinical-trials check) both tell us we are *blind*, not that we are safe.

**Unknown-unknown class: channel and counterparty integrity — the KPI's denominator is unobservable to us.** We can audit what the company reports. We cannot audit what sits underneath a number that appears in no filed document. Every sensor we own points at cash flow and capital spending; none points at whether the unit count is real.

**The reflexive and structural leg.**

- *Our size versus the market's:* irrelevant, and worth saying plainly. A 0.5% starter is roughly $25,000 on a $5M book — about 2,700 shares against an estimated 2.5-4.0M shares of daily volume. That is about one-tenth of one percent of a single day. We cannot move this stock and we will not be trapped in it. Impact is not a risk here.
- *Bidding into our own predicted flow:* this is a live risk. With 11.2% short interest and nearly six days to cover, a clean Q3 print produces a squeeze — exactly the move that would satisfy our second-tranche gate. If we buy the second tranche at market on a squeeze we are paying for flow we ourselves predicted. **The second tranche must be a limit order placed after the print, never a market chase.**
- *A doctrine violation baked into the order as written:* the starter is a good-till-cancelled limit at $9.00, and the next scheduled catalyst is 55 days out. A resting buy limit left standing through an earnings date is an accidental bet against volatility — it will fill on a gap-down caused by exactly the bad print that should stop us buying. **The GTC must be cancelled or converted to day-orders by the close on 2026-10-28.** This is the single most likely way we hurt ourselves mechanically, and it is entirely within our control.
- *Tax interaction:* 2026 is a harvest year. A September entry that goes wrong can be harvested before December 31 as a short-term loss usable this year, which is mildly favorable. Any option expression must expire in November or December 2026 for the same reason; a January 2027 expiry pushes the loss into the wrong year and is excluded.
- *One unchecked overlap:* we have not confirmed whether Alphatec already sits inside the Parametric direct-index sleeve. If it does, we are double-counting exposure and creating a wash-sale conflict on any harvest. Drainable from the current holdings file; not drained.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be** that the 20% case-volume growth was bought rather than earned — paid for with instrument-set capital and with price concessions on commodity biologics — so revenue compounded while cash never did, and we will have paid 2.2x sales for a business whose true multiple on cash earnings was never below 30x and whose only defense of its pricing was a procedural claim it never ran a trial to prove.

## 4. MEASUREMENT TRAPS

Enumerated before a bench builds a kill on one.

1. **Revenue per case has no external comparator.** Neither Globus, Medtronic nor Stryker discloses a revenue-per-case metric. Any statement that ATEC's RPC is worse/better than peers' is unbuildable from primaries. Worse: RPC appears **only in ATEC's earnings call** — not in the 8-K press release and not in the 10-Q. It is an unaudited, company-defined metric with an undisclosed denominator.
2. **Surgical vs total vs U.S. spine.** ATEC's +17% is surgical-only; its +15% includes EOS; Globus's +7% is U.S. spine only and its +5.9% includes neuromodulation and trauma. Pick one perimeter or flag the mismatch.
3. **Adjusted EBITDA excludes stock comp, and ATEC's SBC is structurally large.** The evidence pack's SBC series is mis-stamped and cannot be used; take SBC from the 10-Q cash-flow statement before comparing ATEC's 16.8% "adjusted" margin to anyone's GAAP margin.
4. **Segmental vs global lumbar lordosis.** Marketing and single-arm series quote *segmental* gains (5.1° vs 2.5°; 9.9° vs 0.5°). The pooled meta-analysis finds **no segmental difference (P=0.28)** and only marginal global LL benefit (P=0.05). Different estimands, opposite impressions.
5. **Single-position vs prone.** The 103 vs 306 minute figure compares *single-position* to *dual-position* workflow — it confounds patient repositioning with the approach itself. It is not evidence that prone transpsoas is a better dissection.
6. **"Transient" is a timeframe, not a finding.** Sensory/motor deficit rates differ by an order of magnitude depending on whether they are measured at discharge, 6 weeks, or 12 months. The 8.2% / 5.8% figures are postoperative; the 0.6% figure is *permanent*. Do not compare across those windows.
7. **Radiation dose units.** Entrance skin dose ≠ effective dose ≠ dose-area product. The 4–8× EOS-vs-CT claim derives from a pelvimetry protocol in pregnant women, not from spine deformity imaging, and CT dose is protocol-tunable. A dose comparison that does not name the metric and the protocol is not a comparison.
8. **BMD T-scores are not interchangeable.** AutoDensity T-scores are derived from EOSedge dual-energy AP spine L1–L4 with its own calibration; DXA T-scores use a different reference population. FDA's cleared IFU forbids substitution. Any thesis that treats EOSedge as a DXA replacement market is off-label by the label.
9. **The OASIS trial studies a different procedure.** OsteoAdapt SP's pivotal is anchored on **single-level TLIF** (with ALIF/LLIF expansion), a posterior procedure — not the PTP/lateral case mix driving ATEC's volume growth. A positive OASIS result does not automatically transfer to the cases ATEC actually does.
10. **Fusion is an endpoint with three definitions** — CT-defined bridging, plain-radiograph criteria, and "clinical" fusion — with materially different rates. rhBMP-2's history is precisely a history of endpoint and harms-reporting selection ([PMC3687771](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3687771/)).
11. **Guidance is being compared across two different bases.** The January outlook ($890M, ~17%) and the August guide ($882M, ~15%) are both "FY26 revenue." The delta is a real cut, not a rounding difference.
12. **Close vs intraday on the Q1 drop** (>20% vs 31.7%) — do not average; state which basis you are using.
