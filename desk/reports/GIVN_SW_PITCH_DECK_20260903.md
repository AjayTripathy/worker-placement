# PITCH — GIVN.SW — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — No position. The "non-alcoholic beer needs flavour houses" idea dies on size: even if every piece of the chemistry is true, it is worth roughly 0.3–1.3% of Givaudan's sales, which cannot move a CHF 30bn stock. The stock itself is fairly priced, not expensive — that correction belongs in the desk record.

## What we believe

Removing alcohol from beer really does break the flavour. That part is not in dispute and it is well documented: ethanol is a solvent that controls how aroma molecules leave the liquid and reach your nose, it contributes body and sweetness directly, and it hides the "wort" off-notes (three aldehydes active at microgram-per-litre levels) that malting and boiling create. Strip the alcohol and you get less aroma, less body, and the off-notes you used to be able to hide become obvious ([Muller et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1590/fst.32318); [Castro-Muñoz 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13197-019-03751-4)).

The thesis was that whoever sells the chemistry to fix that gets paid on every non-alcoholic launch, and Givaudan is the biggest flavour house.

We now believe the second half is wrong, for four reasons that survived both benches:

1. **The gap is mostly closed before a flavour house is called.** The dealcoholization equipment itself recovers roughly half the aroma for sub-0.5% beer and about a quarter for true 0.0 beer — the equipment vendor's own number, and the single most load-bearing figure in the whole case ([Alfa Laval FAQ](https://www.alfalaval.com/industries/food-dairy-beverage/beverage-processing/beer-production/webinar/beer-dealcoholization-webinar-21-nov-2019/faq/)). What the machine misses, brewers close by blending two brews and by using special yeasts that make little alcohol — those yeasts are already in commercial use in Germany and Italy.
2. **The money is too small.** Our best estimate of the entire non-alcoholic flavour pool across every flavour house — beer plus the higher-value spirits, wine and ready-to-drink categories — is €150–500m a year. Givaudan's share at roughly a fifth is €30–100m, or 0.4–1.3% of group sales. Our own materiality bar is 2%. The *top* of the range is two-thirds of the bar.
3. **Even that overstates it, because non-alcoholic beer is mostly cannibalising normal beer,** which already carried flavour and hop spend. Non-alcoholic volumes grew 8% in 2025 while beer overall fell 1% ([IWSR](https://www.theiwsr.com/insight/under-pressure-but-not-out-of-options-beer-in-2026/)). The correct number is the *extra* dose per litre, not the whole dose. Netting that plausibly halves the beer leg again.
4. **The intellectual property sits somewhere else.** Givaudan's own beer page names no platform, no trademark and no performance number. Its one "alcohol sensation" patent *amplifies* ethanol — it does not replace it ([WO2009065239](https://www.freepatentsonline.com/WO2009065239.html)). The patents that actually control non-alcoholic off-flavours are held by brewers — Kirin, Asahi, Heineken, AB InBev ([WO2021155258A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2021155258A1/en)).

Separately, and against our own first instinct: **the stock is not expensive.** At about 19.3x enterprise value to adjusted profits versus a ten-year median around 23.5x, the market is already pricing an 18% permanent de-rating. That may well be deserved, but "Givaudan is overpriced" is not a finding this court can support.

## What the market believes

Nineteen analysts cover it and their average target is CHF 3,466, about 5.6% above the current price. That is a "hold with a small upward drift" — no one is arguing this is cheap and no one is arguing it is a bubble. The stock is 8.6% off its 52-week high and 27.9% above its 52-week low. Year to date it is up about 3.6%.

What the market is actually debating is the Taste & Wellbeing division. First-half 2026 sales there grew 0.5% on a like-for-like basis while the Fragrance & Beauty division grew 6.5% — thirteen times faster ([Givaudan H1 2026](https://www.givaudan.com/media/media-releases/2026/2026-half-year-results)). The market is deciding whether taste is temporarily soft or structurally losing share. Nobody in the market is talking about non-alcoholic beer, and that is not because they have missed something — it is because it is too small to discuss.

## Why we might have edge

Three places, honestly ranked, and none of them is large enough to buy on:

- **The cannibalisation correction is genuinely new.** Every "picks and shovels" write-up we have seen sizes the non-alcoholic opportunity as new-category volume times ingredient dose, with no subtraction for the beer it replaces. That is an arithmetic error that recurs across substitution stories (plant milk vs dairy, electric vs combustion aftermarket, vapes vs tobacco flavour), and we have written it into the knowledge graph as a reusable test.
- **We read the actual segment disclosure instead of the vendor summary.** A widely-circulated data-vendor split says Taste & Wellbeing is 36% beverages. It cannot be right as stated: it lists four categories summing to exactly 100% when the company's own site names seven. Givaudan discloses Taste & Wellbeing as a single CHF 3,642m number split by geography only — no beverage line exists.
- **We checked the comparison set and found it broken.** The upstream idea grouped "IFF (Nourish segment)" with the others. IFF has no Nourish segment; its reportable segments are Taste, Health & Biosciences and Scent, and its Food Ingredients business is being sold to CVC for about $3.8bn ([IFF Q2 2026 8-K, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000051253/000005125326000028/q22026991pressreleaseofiff.htm)). Any share-of-market arithmetic built on that grouping over-allocates.

The one place where the underlying idea does appear to have a live example is **Kerry Group**, not Givaudan — Kerry's statutory half-year report explicitly credits beverage growth to "low / no alcohol solutions," with beverage at €922.9m, 27.7% of group. That is a separate name and deserves its own court.

## Why it might be priced in

Almost all of it. The non-alcoholic beer trend is on the front page of every drinks trade publication; German alcohol-free beer crossed 10% of retail beer turnover in 2025. If this were a real earnings driver for Givaudan it would show up in the taste division's growth rate, and it does not — that division grew 0.5%. The full-year 2025 release named the areas of good growth as "snacks, health care, dairy, and sweet goods." Beverages were not on the list, and the words "alcohol-free" and "low-alcohol" do not appear anywhere in it.

Coverage is nineteen analysts on a 150-year-old index constituent. This is a well-discovered, well-measured stock. There is no information asymmetry to harvest here — the correct description is that there is no story, not that the story is undiscovered.

## What could go wrong

**Failure 1 — the omission failure, with a monitorable tripwire.** We never read the one document that could reverse the kill: the FY2025 Annual Report segment note. If it shows beverages at 36%+ of the taste division *and* discloses a low/no-alcohol sub-line at 2%+ of group, our central estimate moves from a rounding error to the bar, and we sat out a re-rating toward CHF 4,100. Tripwire: fetch the annual report PDF and read the segment note. It exists today. This is the cheapest open item on the list and it is embarrassing that both benches left it unread.

**Failure 2 — the reopen failure, with a monitorable tripwire.** Suppose the price falls to our re-court level of CHF 3,050 and a separate quality court buys it. The risk is that we bought a permanent decline rather than a dip: the taste division's 0.5% growth turns negative as Kerry and dsm-firmenich take share, and the multiple goes to the bottom of its ten-year range (15.5x). That prices at roughly **CHF 2,537 — a 17% loss from a 3,050 entry.** Tripwire: the like-for-like growth rate of the taste division at the 13 October sales print and the 27 January full-year print. Monitorable, semi-annually and quarterly.

**Failure 3 — NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE EXISTS.** Givaudan has an adverse Missouri judgement over lung injury from diacetyl and 2,3-pentanedione, plus live competition-authority investigations, contributing to CHF 103m of one-off charges in the first half (CHF 83m of it litigation settlements and provisions). The provision is disclosed only in aggregate. **There is no series we can watch.** Givaudan is a Swiss filer: it produces no US quarterly legal note, no customer-concentration disclosure, no docket that maps to group exposure, and it reports twice a year. We would learn that the reserve was inadequate from the release itself, at the same moment as everyone else.

The unknown-unknown class here is **latent-toxicology tort re-rating** — the class where a decades-old, apparently-reserved chemical liability suddenly re-prices because a new causation theory lands or a single jury verdict resets the settlement grid for thousands of pending claims (the talc / PFAS / glyphosate pattern). By construction it has no leading indicator: the thing that changes is a legal standard, not an operating metric. The second, related class is **antitrust fine timing** — competition authorities announce on their own schedule with no pre-notification and no public fine base.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Our own size is not the risk here, and we should say so plainly rather than pretend. Givaudan trades an estimated CHF 80–120m a day; a full 1% position on the deployable book is roughly CHF 27,000 — about eight shares, under 0.03% of a day's volume. We cannot move this stock, we cannot be trapped in it, and the publishing rail's 1%-of-volume ceiling is not remotely in play. There is no predicted flow of ours to front-run and no ladder to bid into.

The real reflexive risk is **our own doctrine**, in three places. First, *the 2% materiality bar is ours, not the market's.* We chose it. It is a heuristic, and it has now killed this thesis three times. A revenue line worth 1.3% of sales growing at a 7% volume compound rate at high incremental margin can matter to a *multiple* even when it cannot matter to a revenue growth rate — our bar tests the wrong variable, and we should know that we may be systematically killing small-but-compounding optionality by construction. Second, *we very nearly recorded a false valuation kill.* The red bench set fair value equal to the street's own target and then multiplied it by 0.85 — scoring our number against theirs, with no independent anchor. Had that entered the record, we would have carried "GIVN is 10% overvalued" as a desk fact for months and it is not true. Third, *currency*: this is a Swiss-franc asset held against a dollar book, and the franc's annual move can easily exceed the entire expected equity return computed below. Any reopen must be sized knowing the currency leg is comparable in size to the equity leg.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we stayed flat on a fairly-valued compounder because a 2% materiality bar of our own choosing killed a side-thesis that was never the reason to own the company in the first place.
