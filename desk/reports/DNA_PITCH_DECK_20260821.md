# PITCH — DNA — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — Do not buy. Both benches land on no position; the reason that survives scrutiny is that Ginkgo's customer prepayment balance is *shrinking*, not that the stock is expensive.

---

## What we believe

Ginkgo Bioworks (ticker DNA) rents out automated biology labs. Customers pay it to run experiments on robotic systems, and increasingly to buy the robotic systems outright. The company sold off its biosecurity division in April 2026, so what's left is a smaller, faster-shrinking business: $20.2M of revenue in the June quarter, $39.6M in the first half.

The original case for buying was simple: the company's order book for automated labs is visible from the outside — government awards, university contracts, press releases — so we could count the backlog ourselves and know whether revenue was about to turn *before* management said anything on the November call. If the backlog was real and about to convert, the stock at $6.96 was too cheap.

We now believe that case is wrong, and we believe it for a reason neither bench started with. The order book **is** countable from the outside, but not from press releases — it sits on the balance sheet as money customers have already paid in advance. That number **fell** during the first half of 2026, from $94.1M to $89.8M. Customers are consuming what they already bought faster than new customers are signing on. And Ginkgo itself has told the SEC that 85% of what remains ($76.5M of $89.8M) will *not* be recognized as revenue within the next twelve months. That is management's own tagging, not our estimate.

So: the thing we were going to count, we counted. It says no.

Separately, we believe the stock is genuinely cheap on the narrow measure — market value $469M against $302M of cash and marketable securities, so the operating business is being valued at roughly $167M against ~$83M of expected revenue. Cheap is not the same as investable when the backlog is contracting and cash is burning at $186M a year.

## What the market believes

Three analysts cover the stock. Their consensus revenue forecast for 2026 is $83M, cut 22% from $106M. The rating is Sell. The average price target is $7.00 against a $6.96 tape.

That means the market believes almost exactly what we believe: revenue lands near $83M, the business is shrinking about 48% year over year, and the stock is worth roughly what it costs. Roughly 12.4–12.6% of the freely traded shares are sold short — a meaningful group is betting it goes lower, mostly on cash burn and dilution.

The stock is down 60% from its 52-week high of $17.51, but it has already bounced 30% off the $5.37 low. This is not a fresh, un-bought panic.

## Why we might have edge

Three honest candidates, in descending order of strength:

**1. We can read the balance sheet faster than the tape does.** With three analysts on a $469M company, there is no institutional machine grinding through the contract-liability footnote each quarter. The current-versus-long-term split of customer prepayments is a direct, management-signed statement about when revenue arrives, and it is available the day the 10-Q posts — no earnings call needed. We used it. Almost nobody else appears to be.

**2. The valuation convention is contested, and that creates a real disagreement.** Ginkgo has $404M of long-term lease obligations on buildings. Whether you treat that as debt (making the company expensive) or as ordinary rent already inside the burn rate (making it cheap) changes the answer by a factor of three. Most screens treat it one way mechanically. A judgment call on that is a legitimate place to differ from a machine.

**3. There is unrun physical evidence.** Ginkgo leases roughly 260,000 square feet of newly built Boston space and owns 193,000 square feet in West Sacramento. Revenue has halved and no lease impairment has been recorded. Satellite thermal imagery would tell us whether those buildings are actually occupied. Nobody ran it. If they are half-empty, this stops being a valuation question and becomes a write-down question.

## Why it might be priced in

Bluntly: most of it is.

Our revenue number *is* the street's revenue number. Our price target is the street's price target, to the cent. There is no gap between what we think and what the price says — and without a gap there is nothing to be paid for.

The shrinking-backlog finding, which is our best original work, points the same direction the stock has already gone. It confirms the 60% decline rather than contradicting it. Being right about *why* a stock fell is not the same as having an edge on where it goes next.

And the short interest (12.4–12.6% of float) tells us the skeptical case is already crowded. Being the tenth person through the same door is not edge.

The one thing plausibly *not* priced: dilution timing. Cash of $302M against a $186M annual burn is about 19.5 months of runway, ending around Q1 2028. A capital raise very likely comes before the business reaches the size that would justify a higher price. Nobody has put a date on that, and it argues for lower, not higher.

## Why we might have edge — the roof

Worth stating separately because it is the number that decides everything. For this to be worth +50% from here, at a generous 4× revenue multiple, Ginkgo needs revenue somewhere between **$100M and $125M** depending on how you treat the leases — call it 1.2× to 1.5× consensus. That is a far shorter ladder than one bench claimed. But funding the ramp to get there costs roughly $390M of burn over three years, which at today's price is about 83% dilution. The ladder is reachable; the roof falls on you while you climb it.

## PROPOSED ENTRY BANDS

**No entry. No starter. No resting order.** Position stays flat at zero.

Our default is to take a small starter position in any name that survives court without a specific named reason to refuse. This name has one: **customer prepayments are shrinking ($94.1M → $89.8M) and 85% of what's left is management-classified as more than a year out.** A business whose forward book is contracting does not get the benefit of the doubt.

Because the reason we're out is *evidentiary*, not *valuation*, a lower price does not by itself fix it. The reopen conditions below are therefore in two parts, and **the evidence condition must clear first.**

**Reopen — evidence (required, first):**
- Q3 2026 10-Q shows **total contract liabilities rising** versus $89.8M, **and** the long-term portion migrating into the current portion. This is observable from the filing alone, no management commentary required. Expected early-to-mid November 2026.
- *Or* an 8-K announcing restructuring or lease termination with a named dollar figure large enough to supply the missing 37–64% burn step-down.
- *Or* disclosure splitting the $84.565M restricted balance between the surety bond and customer prepayments. Predominantly surety, released on delivery, materially improves the cash story.

**Reopen — price (secondary, only once evidence clears):**
Both benches' math, run under both lease conventions, brackets fair value at consensus $83M revenue and a 4× multiple:

Given a contracting book, we would require entry below the *bearish* convention, where even a full charge for the leases implies no premium:
- **Starter band: $2.75 – $3.42** (0.25% of book), and only with the evidence condition cleared.
- **Add band: below $2.50** (to 0.75% of book), and only after **two consecutive quarters** of rising contract liabilities.
- **Hard ceiling: 1.0% of book.** This name can never be sized as a fair-and-fully-paid holding while the related-party overhang stands.
- **No exits or trims to specify** — we own none.

## What remains unverified

Stated plainly, including the parts that weaken our own conclusion:

1. **The split of the $84.565M restricted cash** between the surety bond and segregated customer prepayments is not disclosed. The SEC data element for it returned an error. This is the single item that could most improve the cash story — surety released on delivery is very different from money that is simply spoken for.
2. **The prior-year revenue comparison of $38.9M** — the denominator of our headline "down 48%" — appears in no primary filing. It exists only in third-party renderings that disagree with each other ($38.5M / $38.9M / $39.1M). Our central descriptive fact about this business is sourced to feeds we have separately proven unreliable on this name.
3. **The November 5 earnings date is unconfirmed.** No company release names it.
4. **Whether the leased buildings are occupied.** Unrun. See sensors 8 and 9.
5. **The mechanism behind the reaffirmed burn guidance.** Second-half spending must fall 37–64% per quarter and no restructuring has been announced. We know the gap exists; we do not know whether it closes through genuine operating improvement, restricted-cash release, or working-capital reversal.
6. **The current portion of the operating lease liability** was never pulled directly; the ~$427M total-liability figure is inferred.
7. **The right multiple.** Both benches used 4× revenue. On a business shrinking 48% a year that is generous, and the second bench said so. Every fair-value figure in this deck moves proportionally if it is wrong.
8. **Today's actual price.** The pack says $6.96; one bench asserted $7.055 without support; the live broker lookup was blocked this session. Immaterial to a no-entry verdict, material to any future entry-band arithmetic.
9. **Whether the shrinking-backlog finding is genuinely ours.** With three analysts covering, the balance-sheet read is plausibly uncrowded — but 12.4% short interest suggests someone has done comparable work. We have not established that our best finding is not already in the price.
