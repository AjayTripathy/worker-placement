# PITCH — KRZ.ID — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — both benches land on reject at zero size and both argue the idea should be retired rather than shelved; this deck is written so the adjudicator can rule against that, not to be filled.

## What we believe

The original idea was simple. Taking the alcohol out of beer breaks the drink in ways that are hard to fix, so every non-alcoholic launch has to buy a repair kit. Non-alcoholic drinks are one of the few genuinely growing corners of a flat beverage market. So instead of guessing which brand wins, buy the company that sells the repair kit to all of them — and Kerry, a €13.4bn Irish taste-and-nutrition supplier, was our candidate.

The science holds up. Alcohol is not just a flavour; it is the solvent that decides which aromas escape into the air above the glass. Remove it and the liquid becomes more water-like: the fruity notes leave too readily and the cereal-like "wet grain" compounds stay behind and dominate. You cannot fix this by adding the original flavours back at the original dose, because the dose-to-perception relationship has moved. That is a real formulation problem, and it was the strongest support the idea ever had ([*Foods* 2021, full text read](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods10071602)).

What we believe **after** court is different, and it is not a matter of degree. The repair kit is mostly cheap. The published fixes are glycerol, mineral salts, malt extract and a different yeast strain — commodities and a strain licence, not a flavour house's intellectual property ([*Membranes* 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/membranes13030283)). And the layer is too small to matter to a company this size. Hand Kerry a worldwide monopoly on every flavour-side repair sold into every non-alcoholic beer, competitors erased, and it buys roughly €18–55m of profit against a €15.6bn enterprise value — a 1.5–4.7% move in a stock that trades 1.3% on an average day.

The defence bench then made it worse for us, and this is the finding that ends the case. Rebuilding the market by physical volume rather than by retail value: world beer output is about 1.896bn hectolitres, non-alcoholic is ~2.3% of that today and heading to ~4% by 2028, so roughly 76m hectolitres. For the position to be worth a 50% re-rate, Kerry would have to collect **€20.5 of flavour ingredients per hectolitre of beer, worldwide, forever** — somewhere between a third and two-thirds of the entire raw-material cost of making the beer. That is not a hard number to hit. It is a physically impossible one.

So what we believe is: the mechanism is real, the money is not, and no amount of further research changes that.

## What the market believes

The market believes Kerry is a decent, slightly dull ingredients compounder having an ordinary year. Analysts model FY26 revenue of €6.83bn (+1.1%) and €5.10 of earnings, FY27 €7.16bn (+4.8%) and €5.64 — twelve analysts covering, average target €97.25 against €84.25, range €81–€111, thirteen buys and six holds. First-half results showed volumes up 3.3%, profit margin up 0.6 points to 16.7%, with management pointing at a 2030 goal of 3–5% volume growth and a 20–21% margin ([H1 2026 results](https://www.kerry.com/about/news-and-media/2026/kerry-group-h1-results-2026)).

Nobody is arguing about non-alcoholic beer, because nobody thinks it moves Kerry's numbers. On the arithmetic, they are right.

## Why we might have edge

Honestly stated, and then honestly withdrawn:

- **The science is genuinely under-appreciated.** Most people assume de-alcoholised beer is beer minus alcohol. It is beer minus alcohol *minus a biased slice of its aroma*, with a biased slice returned — the recovered condensate in the peer-reviewed run came back at 38.7% alcohol while the aroma sat in a separate water fraction, and only some compounds transferred (esters well, higher alcohols at 3.5%). Knowing that ahead of the crowd is a real informational advantage about the category.
- **Kerry does not disclose by end-use market.** Its segments are geographic only — Americas €1,818m, Europe €687m, Asia-Pacific/Middle East/Africa €831m — so no one can see a non-alcoholic line even if one exists. Management did attribute beverage volume growth partly to "low/no alcohol solutions through integrated taste" on the July call, with no number attached. Opacity sometimes hides an asset.

Neither survives. Being right about the chemistry earns nothing if the chemistry's whole addressable spend, captured entirely, is a rounding error on the enterprise value. And the opacity cuts the other way: what is hidden is at most ~0.7% of revenue, so there is nothing large enough behind the curtain to be worth the search.

## Why it might be priced in

"Priced in" is the wrong frame here, and saying so plainly is more useful than pretending otherwise. This is not a case where the market has already discounted our insight. It is a case where our insight, if fully correct, would change the forecast by **+0.02 to +0.06 percentage points of group growth** — inside the rounding error of any analyst's model. We agree with the consensus not because we lost the argument but because there is no arithmetic route from our thesis to a different number.

The tape adds nothing to buy either: €84.25, only 5.6% below the 52-week high, 31% above the low, above both the 50-day (€83.11) and 200-day (€75.69) averages. This is a well-owned, well-covered, well-followed large cap with no dislocation. That conditions size and timing; it is not what kills the idea.

## What could go wrong

**Failure one — we buy the label and own the company.** We take a position on a non-alcoholic thesis and end up owning 99.4%-plus ordinary Kerry: a food-ingredients business growing revenue ~1% at ~15.7x forward earnings. Volumes soften, the margin ratchet stalls short of the 2030 goal, the multiple compresses toward the low end of its history, and we lose 15–25% on a business we did no work on. *Tripwire: monitorable* — group volume growth, EBITDA margin trend, and the FY guidance line at the Q3 update and full-year results.

**Failure two — the rejection is right and also expensive.** We are correct that non-alcoholic beer is immaterial, we retire the ticket, and Kerry compounds anyway: +15.4% to the average target, volumes +3.3%, margin +60bps, buybacks against a share count that already fell ~2.9m on the dairy transaction ([completion RNS](https://www.kerry.com/about/news-and-media/2025/kerry-group-completion-of-kerry-dairy-ireland-transaction)). We lose the money we did not make, and worse, we lose it *because of how our own process is wired* — a rejection stamped on this ticket becoming an informal blacklist on the name. *Tripwire: monitorable, but only by us* — the fix is procedural, and both benches said it explicitly: the compounder case must open its own file with its own profitability test and must never inherit this one.

**Failure three — the one with no tripwire.** The arithmetic is right, we still lose, and we never see it coming, because the thing that decides it is invisible by construction. Kerry is an EU-domiciled business-to-business supplier whose commercial work sits entirely under non-disclosure agreements. It reports segments by geography, discloses no customer above 10%, files no US quarterly report (Irish issuer, no SEC filings), and — the part that matters — its relevant shipments are Europe-to-Europe, so the customs-manifest tool that would normally identify a supplier's customers is *structurally blind here*, not merely unrun. The defence bench recorded that as a standing gap for this whole class of issuer, not a Kerry-specific miss.

Name the unknown-unknown class: **contract-perimeter opacity at a business-to-business supplier with no end-use disclosure and no observable customer channel.** Anything that lives inside a private supply contract — an exclusivity we cannot see, a per-customer systems deal, a repricing, a loss of a large account, or an acquisition that buys the capability we said was absent — can move this stock with zero advance signal available to us. There is no sensor to arm. There is no filing that carries it. We would learn about it from the price.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Liquidity is not our problem: about €14.2m trades a day, so a disciplined ladder at 1% of daily volume is roughly €142k a day and a full slot fills inside one session. We would not be pushing the price around and we would not be bidding into flow we ourselves created. The real reflexivity is in our own rulebook. Our default says a court that produces no *named* kill owes a minimum starter position — so the difference between "we found nothing wrong" and "we found three specific things wrong" is the difference between mechanically owning this and correctly owning none of it. The defence bench was explicit that flat was *earned* by three named novel kills, not defaulted into. If a future reviewer softens those tags, the rulebook manufactures a position nobody wants. Two other defaults are also live and both are switched off here: the fair-price entry default does not apply because the kill is on the size of the prize, not the price; and the small pay-up allowance does not apply because there is no verified fairness to pay up against.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we never owned the thesis we bought — the non-alcoholic layer is under 1% of Kerry, so the profit and loss would be settled by the 99%-plus food-ingredients business we did no work on, and settled against us by exactly the volume and mix deceleration this ticket never examined.
