# PITCH — USNA — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — conditional 0.25% starter, blocked today by one unopened document; we hold no shares and no orders.

---

## What we believe

USANA Health Sciences sells nutritional supplements through a network of independent distributors, mostly in Asia, plus two acquired brands: Hiya (children's vitamins, subscription) and Rise (protein bars/snacks). The stock is $14.37, down 55% from its 52-week high of $32.29 and 14% above its 52-week low of $12.61. Market value is about $265M on 18,476,534 shares.

The company holds $168.6M of cash and has nothing drawn on its credit line. Strip the cash out and the operating business is being valued at roughly $97M. Against that, the business still throws off somewhere between $26M and $35M a year of cash an owner could actually take (after real capital spending, after the very high cash taxes it pays, and after treating stock compensation as the cost it is). So the operating business trades at roughly 3 to 4 times what it earns for an owner. If you insist on also counting the $44.7M minority stake in Hiya that the company doesn't own, it's roughly 4 to 5.5 times.

What we believe, precisely: **this is cheap enough that ordinary decline doesn't lose money, and the question that decides the trade is not "is the business shrinking" — it obviously is — but "does the cash belong to shareholders in any practical sense."** Our two benches split on exactly that question and the tiebreaker is a table in a filing that neither bench read in the original.

What we do *not* believe: that this is a turnaround, that the guidance is trustworthy, or that the decline stops. Core customer counts fell 8.1% year over year and 5.0% quarter over quarter, to 384,000. Hiya's subscribers went 200,400 → 181,000 → 186,000 → 166,000. Both of those are real and neither bench disputed them.

## What the market believes

The market believes management can't forecast its own business and that the cash is not really available. Both beliefs have evidence.

On May 5, 2026 the company reaffirmed full-year revenue of $925M–$1.0B and adjusted earnings of $1.95–$2.29 per share. Ninety days later, on August 4, it cut to $910M and $0.76, and reported a GAAP loss of $(0.61) per share and a net loss of $11M ([Q2 2026 earnings release, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000896264/000089626426000050/q22026earningsreleaseex991.htm)). Wall Street was carrying $945.1M and $2.12 into that print — analysts were 179% above the company on earnings the day before the company told them. A stock does not recover quickly from that.

The market also sees a company whose profits are earned abroad while the U.S. parent loses money. The first half of 2026 shows a **pretax loss of $3.4M and an income tax expense of $17.6M** — the company pays roughly $35M a year in cash tax on essentially zero consolidated pretax income, because losses in some countries generate no tax benefit while profits in higher-taxed countries keep generating tax. Mainland China alone is 48.4% of sales. The natural conclusion is that the cash pile is in the wrong place and the "cheap on cash" argument is an illusion.

Finally, the market noticed the buyback stopped. The company repurchased $27.5M of stock in the first half of 2025 at roughly $30. It repurchased **$0 in the first half of 2026 at $14–$20**, with $34M of authorization still open. Management is not buying a stock it claims is trading at close to one times its own EBITDA estimate.

## Why we might have edge

Three things, in descending order of confidence.

**1. The "second guidance cut is arithmetically forced" argument — which is the loudest bear argument, and which our own red bench made — is built on a selective calculation.** Red computed the gap between the new full-year guide and the current run rate for Rise and Hiya only. Those two segments are 18% of the guided revenue. Rise printed $13.7M in Q1 and $3.4M in Q2, so red concluded the full-year $35M needs three times the exit rate. But the Q1 spike was a one-time shipment: the company disclosed in February that Q1 included "the launch of Protein Pop at a large club retailer" ([Q4/FY2025 release, Feb 17 2026](https://ir.usana.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/815/usana-health-sciences-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year)). A stocking order is not a run rate. Run the same test on Core Nutritional — 82% of the guide, which red never tested — and it produces a $29M *cushion*, not a hole. Netted out, and adjusted for the normal second-half seasonal decline, the shortfall is roughly $8M on $910M of revenue, under 1%. That is guidance noise, not a forced cut. Only the Hiya leg genuinely strains: it needs $64.6M in the second half against a $28.3M exit quarter and a falling subscriber count.

**2. The cash may not be trapped, and the loudest reason to think it is came from a bad search.** Red concluded there was no foreign-cash disclosure because a full-text search for the phrase "held by our foreign subsidiaries" returned nothing across USANA's filings. The disclosure exists — it's a table, not that sentence. As of the FY2024 10-K: China $101.2M, U.S. $40.1M, other $40.5M, with an **annual dividend of about $75.8M remitted from China to the U.S., net of applicable taxes** ([FY2024 10-K, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/896264/000089626425000092/usna-20241228.htm)). If that's right, the cash is China-weighted but demonstrably mobile at a cost that is already inside the tax rate we're using. That flips the single largest bear pillar.

**3. The high tax rate is chronic, not new.** The first half of 2025 carried a 44.5% effective rate. We are not discovering a deterioration; we're paying for a known, priced feature. And the near-zero pretax number this year is distorted by a non-cash goodwill writedown that isn't tax-deductible — excluding it, first-half pretax income was $25.7M.

The honest form of the edge is narrow: *we may be better than the market at separating "the business is shrinking" (true, and priced) from "the cash is fake and the guide will be cut again" (probably not true, and also priced).*

## Why it might be priced in

Several ways, and we take them seriously.

The stock has already bounced 14% off its low. Whatever the dislocation was on August 4, part of it has been collected by somebody else. We did not run our crowding check (the tool couldn't read outside the working directory), so we cannot say whether this is an undiscovered stub or a well-known value trap that a dozen small-cap value funds already own.

Cheapness on shrinking earnings is the oldest trap there is. At 3–4x, the business could decline 15% a year and the multiple would simply never re-rate — you'd earn nothing while the numerator shrinks. Nothing in the record identifies a mechanism that converts cheapness into a return: no buyback (it stopped), no special dividend announced, and a large founder-family control block that likely forecloses a takeover or activist path — though we did not open the August 13 13G/A to confirm the size of that block, so that's an assumption, not a fact.

And our own house record on this exact setup is bad. Our template of buying stocks that gapped down on an earnings miss is **0 for 7**. The reason is consistent: the headline *is* the news, and the market prices it same-day. There is no obvious reason USNA is the eighth case that breaks the pattern.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — The Hiya leg breaks and October 21 is a second cut.** The one part of the bear arithmetic that survives is Hiya: the second half needs $64.6M against a $28.3M exit quarter, while subscribers fell from 200,400 to 166,000 over four quarters. Our consumer-heat check on Hiya reads decaying, at zero. Add rising inventory writedowns ($3.1M vs $2.0M) against a $98M inventory balance, and the $87M EBITDA guide may not survive even if revenue does. A second cut inside six months would reset the stock to the low teens and destroy what's left of management's credibility. *Monitorable:* yes — Hiya app-review cohort delta, consumer heat re-run, and the Q3 print itself.

**Failure narrative 2 — Working capital was the whole story and the cash engine is worse than it looks.** First-half operating cash flow of $33.1M looked like an improvement over $27.7M. It isn't. This year included a $9.4M release of inventory; last year included a $13.0M build — a $22.4M swing in our favor for reasons that have nothing to do with earnings. Excluding inventory, the comparison is $23.7M against $40.7M: **down 42%**. Both benches agreed on this and it is the single most important number in the file. If the remaining $98M of inventory is stale rather than sellable, the writedowns continue and the release reverses into a charge. *Monitorable:* partly — inventory balance and writedown line each quarter, but only after the fact.

**Failure narrative 3 — China. No tripwire exists.** Mainland China is 48.4% of sales, holds the profit pool, and holds $101.2M of the $168.6M in cash. Direct selling in China operates under a licensing regime that has been abruptly tightened before, without warning, and USANA's local entity is a joint venture structure with a minority partner. A licensing action, a campaign against multi-level marketing, a currency-control change that stops the annual dividend, or a local-partner dispute would simultaneously destroy the earnings, strand the cash, and remove the only path by which shareholders get paid — all at once, in a single announcement, with no advance signal we can subscribe to. **There is no monitorable tripwire for this.** We have no primary-source Chinese regulatory feed, no local-language filing plane, and no satellite or alternative read that resolves it (the one candidate — thermal imaging of the Beijing/Tianjin facility — measures utilization, not legal standing, and we haven't run it). The unknown-unknown class is **sovereign/regulatory discontinuity in an opaque jurisdiction**: a category where the first observation is the outcome, not a leading indicator. Neither bench named this as its kill until blue raised it at the end; that it went unnamed for most of the court is itself a warning about how the file was framed.

**The reflexive and structural leg — how we might cause or compound our own loss.**

- *Size versus liquidity is unverified.* We could not get a live quote or an average-volume reading; our broker tools weren't permissioned in this session. On a $265M market cap with a large family control block, the genuinely tradable float is much smaller than the market cap suggests. A 0.25% position may be several days of volume to exit, and we would be exiting into exactly the conditions that make everyone else want out. **We are proposing a position whose exit cost we have not measured.**
- *We would be bidding into flow we can predict.* A stock down 55% year-to-date is a prime tax-loss-selling candidate in November and December. Our own rule is not to bid into flow we ourselves forecast. If we buy in September at $14.37 and the tax-loss wave arrives in December, we will have paid up for the privilege of watching mechanical selling take it to $12. The correct read of that rule here argues for a later, lower entry, and we are flagging that our proposed starter partially violates it.
- *Doctrine interaction — the position may exist because of a rule, not a thesis.* Our house rule says that when an adversarial review produces no named kill, the default is a small starter rather than nothing. Blue leaned on exactly that rule to permit 0.25%. That's a rule designed to stop us from talking ourselves out of good ideas, and here it is doing work that conviction should be doing. **If we own this, we should be honest that a procedural default, not enthusiasm, put it in the book.**
- *Tax-lot interaction.* This is a taxable account. A starter bought now with a strong chance of a 20% drawdown into a January print creates a short-term loss lot — which is not a disaster (it's harvestable) but does mean the position's first job would likely be tax, not return. Any exit needs the short-term/long-term clock checked before it's sized.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we bought a statistically cheap stub whose profits and cash both sit inside mainland China, and the event that impaired them — a regulatory, licensing, or capital-controls change there — was not observable to us at any point before the price had already moved.
