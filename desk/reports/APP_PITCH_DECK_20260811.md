## What we believe

AppLovin is not a broken business, but it is not a bargain either — it is a very good business whose growth engine just showed its first visible slowdown, priced by a crowd that can see the same thing we can.

The specifics: the stock has fallen 57% from its high ($745.61) to fresh 52-week lows (~$322, intraday low $319.50) after the August 5 second-quarter report. Our original pitch framed that report as a "0.85% revenue miss" punished by a 20% drop — an overreaction worth buying. The court dismantled that framing. Advertising revenue grew 52.8% year over year, but the company guided the next quarter to 46–48% — a deceleration — and the quarter came in below the company's **own** guidance midpoint ($1.93 billion guided, $1.924 billion delivered), the first time that has happened. Management's explanation, that AI-model improvements were "lighter than normal," refers to an internal cadence nobody outside the company can observe or verify. Every insider who *can* observe it sold stock between $484 and $600 this spring and summer, and none has bought a share at $322.

What survives on the positive side: the company generates real cash (roughly $4.6 billion annualized operating cash flow against $3.05 billion of cash), stock compensation is modest and falling, the share count is shrinking, and a full accounting-trap sweep came back clean. So the verdict is "not now, at not this price, on not this evidence" — not "never."

## What the market believes

The market believes the growth story cracked. At least seven analysts cut their price targets the same day. The consensus view embedded in the price is that 70–80% growth is over, that the 46–48% guide may itself be optimistic, and that a company whose key growth metric exists only as an orally-presented, non-reconcilable figure deserves a discount until it proves otherwise. The sell-off was not mechanical or forced — no index rebalance, no margin cascade, no fund liquidation. It was a large group of informed people simultaneously marking down their estimate of how durable the growth is. That is the market working, not the market breaking.

## Why we might have edge

Honestly: less than our original pitch claimed. What remains:

- **The quality is real.** The trap sweep (both benches concur) found genuine free cash flow, honest stock-compensation accounting, net share shrinkage, and no leverage tail. If the growth stabilizes anywhere above ~30%, today's price is likely cheap in hindsight.
- **A defined re-entry test exists.** The Q3 report (early November) either quantifies the e-commerce advertising vertical *in the filing* and clears $2,055M in revenue, or it doesn't. That is a checkable, near-dated resolution most sellers today are not waiting around for.
- **The panic may overshoot.** A stock down 57% making new lows with sentiment this one-sided can fall below any reasonable fair value; the courts left a second-look level ($302) armed for exactly that.

## Why it might be priced in

This is the stronger side, and both benches said so:

- **No forced seller means no mispricing mechanism.** This is a heavily-covered stock being repriced by consensus on real information. The efficient-market case is the default here, and nothing in our work rebutted it.
- **The "cheap at ~21× next year's earnings" defense is circular.** That multiple uses an earnings estimate that assumes the disputed 46–48% guide holds. The stock is only cheap if the very thing in question is true.
- **The people with the best information sold.** All insider activity May–July was selling at $484–$600; there has never been an open-market purchase, including after the crash. (The blue bench fairly reduced the weight of this: only three trading sessions had passed post-report, likely inside a blackout window, the sales were probably pre-scheduled plans, and growth-company insiders almost never buy. But the absence of a single buy is still zero evidence in our favor.)
- **The one thing that would prove the story — the growth metric — cannot be independently verified**, and the SEC inquiry's closure exists only as a statement on a conference call, with no written filing.
