# PITCH — 7239.T — VERDICT
**ADJUDICATED 2026-08-20 — ADVANCE TO COURT — the one name where the TSE mechanism actually applies**

*Tachi-S Co., Ltd. (7239.T) — automotive seat maker, Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime. No position. No orders. No sensors armed.*

---

## What we believe

Tachi-S makes car seats, mostly for Nissan and its affiliates. The market values the whole company at about ¥78.8 billion. The company holds roughly ¥41.7 billion in net cash and carries essentially no debt (debt-to-equity 0.04). So if you buy the stock today, more than half of what you pay is cash sitting on the balance sheet, and you're paying roughly ¥37 billion for a business that the bull case says throws off about ¥8.8 billion of free cash a year.

That is the entire argument in one sentence: **you may be paying about four years of cash flow for the operating business, with the cash pile handed to you for free.**

Two things make us think this could actually get resolved rather than sit cheap forever:

1. **It trades below the value of its own net assets.** Price-to-book is 0.74 — the market says the company is worth less than the accounting value of what it owns. We verified this against live data on 2026-08-20.
2. **The Tokyo Stock Exchange is leaning on exactly this.** Since 2023 the TSE has been publicly pressuring Prime-listed companies trading below book value to publish plans to fix it — raise the dividend, buy back stock, sell non-core assets, or explain themselves. Tachi-S is a **Prime** listing at real scale. That pressure is a live, named, institutional force on this specific company. ([JPX follow-up on "Action to Implement Management that is Conscious of Cost of Capital and Stock Price"](https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/equities/follow-up/index.html))

This last point is why this name survived and its cohort-mates didn't. We looked at a basket of cheap Japanese companies sitting on cash. Most of them are small companies on the TSE's *Standard* market, where that reform pressure is far weaker and the free float is too thin to matter. Tachi-S is the one name in the group where the mechanism the whole idea depends on genuinely applies.

Meanwhile the dividend is **4.94%** — and we get paid that while waiting.

## What the market believes

The market is not confused about the balance sheet. It can see the cash. What it doesn't believe is that the cash flow is durable, and here is why that's a reasonable position:

- **Tachi-S is a component supplier, not a brand.** Its operating margin is about 4.1%. That is thin. A customer demanding a 5% price cut can wipe out most of the profit.
- **Its biggest customer is Nissan, which is restructuring.** Nissan is closing plants and cutting volume. Seats are made near the plants that install them. Fewer plants means stranded capacity for the supplier.
- **China is in an automobile price war.** Domestic Chinese brands are cutting prices hard, and the Japanese makers are losing share there. Tachi-S has plants exposed to that market.
- **The capital spending footprint is global and heavy.** Seat plants are physical, located near assembly lines, and expensive to relocate or shut.

So the market's view is: *the cash is real, the earnings that produced it are not safe, and the company will spend the cash defending a shrinking business rather than returning it.* That is a coherent thesis. It is not a mispricing on its face.

And one uncomfortable fact that neither of our review teams mentioned: **the stock is not beaten down.** It trades at ¥2,327 against a 52-week high of ¥2,354 and a 52-week low of ¥1,894. It is **1.1% below its one-year high** and **23% above its one-year low**. Whatever this is, it is not a stock the market has just abandoned. Someone has already been buying.

## Why we might have edge

Being honest about which of these are real edges and which are just reasons we like it:

**Possibly real edge:**
- **Cohort selection.** We screened a group of cheap, cash-rich Japanese companies and then asked a question most screens don't: *does the reform mechanism I'm relying on actually apply to this listing?* For most of the cohort the answer was no — wrong market tier, too small, no float. That filtering step is a genuine process advantage over a raw low-price-to-book screen.
- **Adverse-selection awareness on the customer.** We already hold 7222 (Nissan Shatai), another company in the Nissan chain. That means we have context on the Nissan restructuring that a first-look investor wouldn't. It also means we have a concentration problem — see below. The context is an edge; the concentration is a liability.

**Not edge, though it feels like it:**
- The cheapness itself. Price-to-book 0.74 with net cash is visible on every screen in Japan. Thousands of people can see it. If the only reason to own this is "it's cheap on book," we have no edge at all.
- The dividend yield. Also visible to everyone.

## Why it might be priced in

- **The stock is at its one-year high.** If our thesis is "the market hasn't noticed the cash," the tape disagrees. A stock 1% off its high has been noticed.
- **The whole Japanese value-and-governance trade is crowded.** Foreign investors have been buying cheap Japanese balance sheets on the TSE-reform story for three years. We would be arriving late to a well-attended argument.
- **Low price-to-book can be correct.** A company earning a 4% margin in a shrinking end market *should* trade below book, because the book assets — plants near dying assembly lines — may not be worth book.
- **A buyback we can't predict is not an edge.** "TSE pressure sustains buybacks" is a hope about corporate behavior on an unknown schedule. That is the weakest load-bearing part of the bull case, and it was stated in our own blue-team write-up as if it were a fact.

## What remains unverified

1. **Free cash flow of ¥8.8 billion.** Not traced to a filing. The valuation case is built on it.
2. **All company financials.** Our automated evidence pack returned nothing for this issuer — no filings, no quarterly statements. Everything here is from an aggregator. Nothing has been read from a TDnet or EDINET document.

5. **The AAGS convertible-bond allottee question**, and the correct date of the February disclosure.
6. **Whether management has actually published a TSE cost-of-capital response plan.** The whole reform argument assumes the pressure translates into action at this company specifically. We have verified that Tachi-S is in the pressured group. We have not read what, if anything, it has said in reply.
7. **Whether the cash is genuinely distributable** — or committed to capital spending, pensions, or customer-support obligations. Net cash on a screen is not the same as cash a board is willing to hand out.
8. **Why the stock is already at its highs.** Someone knows something we haven't found. Until the fresh court explains the last 23%, we're the least-informed buyer in the room.
