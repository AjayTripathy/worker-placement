# PITCH — 8135.T — VERDICT

**ADJUDICATED 2026-08-20 — courtable small, no urgency**

*(Company: ZETT Corporation, Osaka. Sporting goods wholesaler, founded 1920, listed on the TSE Standard market. Price ¥433 as of 2026-09-02. We own none of it and have no orders working.)*

---

## What we believe

ZETT is a ¥58.7bn-revenue sporting goods distributor whose stock market value (¥8.5bn) is less than the cash and securities sitting on its own balance sheet. At ¥433 a share:

- Book value is ¥782.80 a share. You are paying 55 cents on the dollar of stated book.
- Net cash is ¥7,565M, or ¥386.50 a share — 89% of the entire market value.
- Add the investment securities at a punitive 50% haircut and liquid assets alone are ¥10,533M, or **¥538 a share — 124% of what the whole company costs.**
- Strip the net cash out of the price and the operating business is priced at roughly ¥911M, against ¥980M of operating profit the company itself guides to this year. You are paying about one year of profit for a hundred-year-old business, and being handed the balance sheet for free.
- The dividend is ¥18 a share, a 4.16% yield, and it is an *ordinary* dividend — not a one-off. We verified that in the filing footnote after our own brief claimed the opposite.

The honest version of the thesis is not "this is a great business." It is: *this is a cheap, dull, solvent orphan, and nobody is looking at it.* Zero analysts cover it.

## What the market believes

The market believes there is no reason to own it, and it is not obviously wrong:

- Operating margin is around 2% and management guides it *down* to about 1.65% this year. This is a low-margin distribution business, not a brand.
- Revenue has compounded at 4.8% a year since 2019, and this year is guided at +1.4%.
- Underneath is a demographic problem: youth baseball participation in Japan is shrinking, and that is the core end market.
- Nobody at the company acts like an owner. There is no buyback, no cancellation of the 527,253 treasury shares, and no schedule to sell down the cross-shareholdings.
- It trades about $51,600 a day. It is uninvestable for anyone managing real money, which is exactly why it is cheap and exactly why it may stay cheap.

## Why we might have edge

Three things, in descending order of confidence:

1. **We read the documents and the consensus didn't, because there is no consensus.** No analyst covers this. Every number in this pitch came off the company's own quarterly and annual reports, not a data vendor. The screens that surface names like this get the numbers wrong (ours did — see below), so the few people who look, look at bad data and move on.
2. **The cheapness is arithmetic, not a forecast.** We don't need growth, a re-rating catalyst, or a management change for the balance sheet to be worth what it's worth. Our own harshest fair value — the bear bench's, built by discounting the trapped cash 50% and the securities 50% — is ¥593 against a ¥433 price. A less punitive read is around ¥670. The price gate passes on both.
3. **We caught two accounting artifacts that a casual reader would trade on backwards.** The cash flow "collapse" last year was a ¥1,058M tax payment on the prior year's one-time gain, not an operating break. The 14% quarterly "cash drain" was cash moved into securities — total liquid assets actually *rose* ¥732M in the quarter, after paying the dividend.

## Why it might be priced in

Bluntly: most of it probably is, and the discount may be the correct price for a permanent problem.

- **The cash is not ours.** ¥2,041M — 24% of the entire market value — was moved out of cash and *into* investment securities last quarter. We verified this was a purchase, not a market re-valuation (the unrealized-gain reserve barely moved). Over two years management has pushed roughly ¥4.07bn of shareholder cash into securities while returning ¥0.70bn in dividends. That is not a company waiting to be unlocked; it is a company actively burying the asset we're underwriting.
- **The operating business is going sideways, not inflecting.** The Q1 "+32% beat" is an easy comparison. Operating profit was ¥438M two years ago, ¥328M last year (depressed by a bad-debt provision), and ¥434M this year. Two years, no progress. Neither of our benches caught this until the very end of the court.
- **The one time value was released, somebody else forced it.** ITOCHU — which owns both Descente and Converse Japan — drove the one asset monetization. Left alone, ZETT sold ¥110M of a ¥3.9bn securities pile in a full year: 2.8%. It also drew a ¥300M long-term loan while holding ¥9.1bn of cash, from a lender that is also a 5% shareholder.
- **The yield doesn't pay you to wait, quite.** 4.16% gross becomes about 3.17% after Japanese withholding and US tax, against roughly 2.5% after tax on a Treasury bill fund. Seven-tenths of a point for equity risk, unhedged currency, and no exit.

## What could go wrong

**Failure 1 — Dead money, forever (most likely). Tripwire: yes.**
We buy at 0.55x book and in three years it is still 0.55x book, we have collected ~3.2% a year after tax, and the currency has eaten it. The mechanism is already visible: management converts cash into securities faster than it returns it, so the discount never has to close. *Monitorable:* the November half-year report — does operating cash flow exceed ¥1.0bn now that the one-time tax is gone, and did securities purchases continue? And the annual securities report's cross-shareholding note, which neither bench read.

**Failure 2 — The yen. Tripwire: yes, and it fired during the court.**
We buy in yen and report in dollars. USDJPY traded 159.59 with a session high of **160.393 on the day this case was heard** — through the tripwire the Japan sweep pre-registered. A move from 160 to 180 is an 11% loss in dollars with nothing wrong at the company. Our own memo says unhedged, principal decides. *Monitorable:* the FX rate, daily.

**Failure 3 — Something is arranged behind a closed door that we cannot see. Tripwire: NONE.**
Unknown-unknown class: **counterparty intent under a disclosure regime that shows us nothing between filings.** Concretely, any of these would hurt and none of them are watchable:
- The ¥2,041M is not ordinary listed cross-holdings but something related-party — an affiliate placement, a captive fund, a directed investment. We cannot know without a document nobody has pulled.
- ITOCHU, having cashed out of the relationship, restructures ZETT's sourcing or brand arrangements. The company reports a single segment with no customer, product, or geographic detail, so we would learn about it in the revenue line a quarter late.
- The founding family or the employee shareholders' association takes the company private at a price anchored near the cash — using our own thesis against us, at a premium to ¥433 and a large discount to ¥782.
- We also have **no way to test sourcing at all**: the Japanese customs trade-statistics connector we'd need does not exist on this desk. It is the highest-value missing tool in the case.

A fourth, related one with no tripwire: the youth-baseball demographic decline. There is no series we subscribe to, and single-segment reporting means it never appears as a line item — it just shows up as revenue that quietly stops growing.

**The reflexive and structural leg — our own footprint and our own rules.**
The stock trades ~19,000 shares a day, about $51,600. A $12,500 position is a quarter of a day's volume; the disciplined build is 100-share clips at 1% of daily volume across 25 sessions. That means **we cannot get out quickly.** Exiting is the same 25 sessions, and it will be into a market that has no natural buyer, because there is no analyst and no institution.

If the bear bench's seller hypothesis were true — a bank unwinding a 5% stake, roughly 54 days of volume — we would be the marginal bid financing a patient, price-insensitive seller, which is exactly the situation our own flow rule tells us never to buy into. The bull bench overturned that read (the heavy-volume days were *up* days, and the recent five-session volume has halved), but it overturned it on inference, not on a filing. Nobody has identified the seller. We are choosing to buy without knowing who is selling.

And there is a doctrine interaction worth stating plainly: our minimum-starter rule says a court that ends below fair value with no fatal finding owes a starter position rather than a flat pass. That rule is what is producing this position. It is not conviction — it is a rule. The position is deliberately too small to matter to the book, which means the real cost of owning it is not capital, it is the attention it will consume for years.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we correctly priced a balance sheet we never had any claim on — management kept converting the cash into securities we can't reach, the operating business stayed flat, and the yen fell — so we were right about the value and still never got paid.
