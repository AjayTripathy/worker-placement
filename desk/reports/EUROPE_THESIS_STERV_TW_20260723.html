Europe value: the forest and the builder

July 23, 2026 · the Europe leg of the rotation-sleeve build · envelopes 109–112 staged (two half-starters, ~$11k at full fill) · sized vs the $1,000,000 base

The European value screen surfaced five candidates. The courts' first act was to throw out the three the market had already found: two cyclicals sitting at 52-week highs (+84% and +32% on the year — the value realized, the entry gone) and one loss-maker falling with the knife still in motion. What survived are the two genuine live dislocations: Stora Enso, where the forest on the balance sheet is worth more than the entire company is priced at, and Taylor Wimpey, the best-capitalized name in a sector at the bottom of its cycle, paying ~10% a year to wait. Both are owned as fairly-paid special-situation carry — labeled that way, sized that way.

Stora Enso (STERV, Helsinki) Half-starter — asset-backed special situation

Staged: 300 @ €8.80 · 250 @ €8.30 (GTC, second rung below the 52-week low) · live ~€8.84 · Finnish forest-products group

The case: the market prices the operating company below zero

Stora Enso's own accounts carry its forest assets at €10.80 per share — against a share price under €9. At this price, the €9.3bn-revenue paper-and-packaging business comes attached for less than free. The obvious objection is that balance-sheet forest marks are paper numbers — and that's exactly the objection the court could kill with a transaction: in May 2025 the company sold 12.4% of its Swedish forest for €900M, "in line with accounting fair value." The mark clears in cash. And the discount now has a clock on it: the company states the Swedish forest business will be separated into its own publicly-listed company, completed during the first half of 2027.

What the court verified — including the scary headline

The stock fell ~11% on its Q2 report the day of the court, and the drop decomposes into an honest, disclosed non-event: adjusted operating profit actually rose +27%; the reported IFRS loss came from −€83M of impairments and −€61M of biological-asset fair-value swings, both named in the release. Share count, equity, and the resulting 0.65× book were independently recomputed from the filings; leverage is 2.2× and falling (from 3.3×). Why only half-size, then? Because the operating engine is genuinely weak right now — trough earnings (~55× excluding the fair-value noise), roughly breakeven free cash flow while a major mill build absorbs capex, and a net dividend of only ~1.8% after Finland's 35% withholding. The asset floor protects the value; nothing protects the time.

Risk: the 2027 demerger slips, or is structured so minority holders of the Helsinki R-shares don't get full value — either turns this into dead money for one to two years with the discount intact. Tripwire: the Q3 report (~late October) — the separation must still be on track for H1-2027 and leverage ≤2.5×. A slip past 2027 or leverage above 2.8× cuts it to watch. Do not chase above €9.20 — the entry is the below-forest-value margin.

Taylor Wimpey (TW, London) Half-starter — quality at the bottom of the cycle

Staged: 2,700 @ 80.0p · 2,700 @ 75.0p (GTC — prices in pence, verified against the live 82p tape; +0.5% UK stamp duty) · one of the UK's largest housebuilders

The honest framing first: this one doesn't diversify

The court said it plainly and so does this memo: Taylor Wimpey stacks UK-housing and UK-rates exposure — the same axis where a peer (Vistry) was refused outright this month. It earns its half-slot on one argument only: if you're going to own the sector at its cycle trough, own the balance sheet that survives every version of the trough — and get paid handsomely to wait. The position is deliberately capped: no second UK housebuilder alongside it.

The corrected math, and the anti-Vistry tells

The screen's headline "9.3% dividend yield" is an artifact — the company moved part of its distribution into a £52M buyback, so the trailing cash figure overstates the dividend. The court rebuilt it forward: ~7.1% cash yield plus a ~2.5%-of-NAV buyback ≈ 10.6% total return of capital, on a stated policy floor (7.5% of net assets, minimum £250M). What separates it from the peer we refused: net cash all the way through the year (£343M at year-end, £221M on average — not window-dressed for the photo), cladding remediation front-loaded (£226M already taken while the peer keeps topping up), a pension in surplus, and one reaffirmed guidance. Book value was recomputed from the stated accounts: 0.70× tangible net assets (the court corrected an earlier, flattering 0.61× estimate — the discipline cuts against our own numbers too).

Risk: a UK autumn-budget shock or rates re-accelerating extends the trough past 2027 — the likely damage is dead-money carry (you still clip ~7% cash) rather than capital loss, but the re-rate slips. Tripwire (imminent): the trading statement due around end-July — walk if the net private sales rate drops below ~0.70 per outlet and the distribution floor is cut; add if the sales rate holds ≥0.80 or a Bank of England cut lands. One structural plus: UK dividends carry zero withholding for a US holder — the cleanest foreign carry in the book.

How the two fit the book

Stora Enso is the genuine diversifier — Finnish forest products correlate with nothing else we own, and its return driver is a dated corporate event, not the market cycle. Taylor Wimpey is a deliberate, small, paid-to-wait cyclical bet that the court refused to dress up as diversification. Together they're ~$11k at full fill — two half-starters whose entries rest below the market, one of which (Stora's second rung) only fills below the 52-week low. Both are structurally safe for a US taxable holder (passive-asset shares of 15% and 9% — nowhere near the foreign-fund tax line even in a deep drawdown), and both carry pre-registered exit conditions with dates attached.

Honest limits

Neither position claims alpha. Both are risk-premium carry: Stora pays you in discount-plus-catalyst, Taylor Wimpey in cash-plus-cycle, and both courts scored conviction 6/10 — the grade of a fair trade, not a table-pounder. Neither counts toward the desk's edge scoreboard, by design. The withholding asymmetry is real (Finland takes 35% of Stora's dividend; the UK takes nothing of Taylor Wimpey's) and is priced into the sizing. The screen-level lesson is recorded for the machine: a value screen ranked on last year's accounts will happily recommend last year's winners — the court's first job in Europe was separating still-cheap from was-cheap.
