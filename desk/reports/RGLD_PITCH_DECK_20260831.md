# PITCH — RGLD — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — do not buy at today's $262; place a three-step buy ladder starting at $225 and below, because both benches' own arithmetic puts fair value under the current price.

---

## What we believe

Royal Gold owns paper, not mines. It buys royalties and streams — contracts entitling it to a slice of another company's gold production, or the right to buy that metal at a fixed low price — and then collects cash without paying for labor, diesel, or mine construction. In the first half of 2026 it collected $919.7M of revenue and $628.7M of operating cash flow, and its cash operating costs are a rounding error against that.

Three things we believe, in order of how much weight they carry:

1. **The business is a genuine cash annuity for the next several years, not a treadmill.** The company's own five-year outlook of 430,000–480,000 gold-equivalent ounces per year is built from assets it already owns — expansions at Khoemacau and Platreef, Corani, Great Bear, and the Hod Maden royalty — and explicitly excludes deals not yet done ([2026 guidance 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/85535/000008553526000013/pressreleasedatedmarch3120.htm)). Volumes rise roughly 25–35% over five years with no new acquisitions required. That is the load-bearing fact of the whole thesis, and it comes from a primary filing.

2. **The accounting understates the cash.** Reported earnings carry a large non-cash depletion charge created by purchase accounting when Royal Gold absorbed Sandstorm and Horizon in October 2025. That is why the stock looks expensive on earnings (about 21.6x consensus 2026 EPS of $12.15) and cheaper on cash (about 17.9x cash flow). Both numbers are true; the cash one describes the business better.

3. **But it is not cheap today.** At $262 the stock trades above every fair-value range either bench could construct. The honest range, after charging realistic maintenance spending, is **$211–252 per share**. We are being asked to pay a 4–20% premium to that.

So: a good business, correctly understood, at a bad price. The pitch is the ladder, not the purchase.

## What the market believes

The market believes roughly what we believe, and prices it. Six analysts model 2026 revenue at $1.91B and EPS at $12.15 — above our own straight-line H1 annualization of $1.84B — and those are full models that already carry the depletion charge. There is no screen artifact to arbitrage here: the marginal buyer is not a spreadsheet confused by non-cash charges, it is a person who has read the same 10-Q we have.

What the market has done recently is more interesting than what it believes. Royal Gold traded as low as **$186.33 in July 2026** and closed June at **$199.61**. It is now $262 — a **+27% move in roughly one month**, and about +41% off the July low ([monthly price history](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/rgld/history/?p=monthly)). Gold itself is *down* 20.3% from its January 29, 2026 high of $5,597, now around $4,459. So the equity has re-rated hard while its underlying commodity has not recovered. The market has decided, in the last four weeks, that royalty companies deserve a higher multiple. That decision is what we would be paying for.

## Why we might have edge

Honestly: not much, and we should say so.

- **We do not have an information edge.** Every dispositive fact in this analysis came from the 10-Q, a guidance press release, and the public price tape. Nothing proprietary.
- **We do not have an analytical edge on the multiple.** We agree with the street's numbers.
- **The one edge we may have is patience and a written price.** The stock was $186 six weeks ago. Most buyers at $262 are chasing a month-old move. Our contribution is a pre-committed ladder that only fills if the move partly unwinds — and the price history says a 30%+ round trip in this name is normal, not exotic.
- **A secondary edge is portfolio-shaped, not stock-shaped.** A contractual claim on gold production, with no operating cost line to inflate and no construction risk, is a different kind of asset from anything else we hold. If we want gold exposure that survives a cost-inflation shock, this is the cleanest instrument. That is a diversification argument, not an alpha argument, and it does not justify overpaying.

## Why it might be priced in

It probably is. The specific reasons:

- Consensus already carries the depletion step-up. The "hidden cash flow" story is not hidden.
- The five-year volume outlook has been public since March 2026. It is in the price.
- The August re-rate suggests the market has *already* discovered the annuity framing that we consider our core insight. We may be showing up one month late to our own idea.
- The stock's fall from $306 to $186 and recovery to $262 traced roughly **1.9x** gold's own drawdown. That is a normally-behaving, well-arbitraged, high-beta gold instrument. It is not a neglected security.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — We paid for a multiple, and the multiple goes back.** (Most likely.) Nothing breaks. Gold drifts sideways at $4,400. The August enthusiasm for royalty companies fades the way sector enthusiasms do, and the stock returns to the 12–14x cash flow it traded at in July — roughly $190–210. We lose 20–25% on a business that performed exactly as forecast. *Tripwire: monitorable.* The price gates below fire, and the Q3 numbers would still be fine, which is precisely how this failure feels — correct thesis, wrong price.

**Failure narrative 2 — Gold breaks and the leverage works against us.** Gold is already 20% below its January high. Another 15% down, to roughly $3,800, is not a tail event; it is a normal commodity correction. At the measured 1.9x drawdown ratio, that is Royal Gold at roughly $185–190 — below our lowest ladder step, with all three tranches filled into a falling market. *Tripwire: monitorable* (gold spot, our own price gates), *but the tripwire does not save us*, because the ladder is designed to fill on exactly this path. The risk is not that we miss the signal; it is that we deliberately buy the whole position into a decline and then it keeps going.

**Failure narrative 3 — Something goes wrong inside a mine we do not own, and we find out when everyone else does.** *This one has no monitorable tripwire and we should be blunt about that.* Royal Gold's assets are contracts on other companies' operations: Mount Milligan (12.8% of revenue), Pueblo Viejo (10.0%), Kansanshi in Zambia, Hod Maden in Türkiye, Corani in Peru. A pit-wall failure, a labor action, a royalty-contract dispute, a tax renegotiation by a host government, or an operator's own financial distress all hit our cash flow — and every one of them surfaces first in *the operator's* disclosure, on *the operator's* timetable, in jurisdictions where we have no sensor and no standing. The unknown-unknown class here is **non-observable counterparty state**: we own a claim on a process we cannot watch. Our entire detector library was consulted and every single detector came back not-applicable or unfired for exactly this reason — satellite build-out monitoring, thermal plant readings, customs data, all of it observes the *operator*, not us. We cannot arm a sensor for this. We can only size for it.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Our own size is not the problem: the intended position is $130–150k against an estimated $178M of daily dollar volume, well under 0.1% of a day's trading — though we should note that the 679,151-share average volume figure is **unverified**, because the brokerage contract-search tool was permission-denied during both benches' work. We have no live tick, no independently confirmed volume, and no options-implied move. Executability at our size is not seriously in doubt, but we are asserting it rather than having measured it.

The real structural risks are two:

- *We are bidding into our own predicted flow.* Our ladder only fills if gold falls. But if gold falls, it will also be dragging down other real-asset exposure in the book at the same moment, and the household's tax-reserve logic assumes gold-adjacent assets move opposite to the broad market — a correlated gold drawdown breaks that assumption in two places at once. We would be adding gold exposure precisely when our existing gold exposure is hurting us, on the same trigger.
- *We are buying an asset whose whole appeal is behaving differently from everything else, at the moment it stops doing that.* Royalty companies are supposed to be the low-drama way to own gold. The measured 1.9x drawdown ratio says this one is not low-drama. If we size it as ballast and it behaves as leverage, the sizing is wrong regardless of whether the thesis is right.

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be **that we paid a full multiple for a well-understood gold annuity immediately after a 27% one-month re-rate, and gold simply consolidated instead of falling — so the ladder never filled at our prices, we compromised and bought higher anyway, and the multiple went back to where it was in July.**
