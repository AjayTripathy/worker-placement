# PITCH — XE — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — No position, no order: stay out through the October share-unlock, then buy only if the stock trades at or below **$12.75**, starting at 0.5% of the book and capped at 1.0%.

## What we believe

X-Energy builds small nuclear reactors (the Xe-100, 80 megawatts each) and the special pellet fuel they run on. It listed publicly in April 2026 at $23.00 and now trades at **$18.19** (evidence pack, 2026-08-28 15:24Z; both benches worked off a slightly earlier $17.955 print — neither is a tick I took myself today). The stock is **51% below its 52-week high of $37.10** and 37% above its low of $13.29.

Three things we think are true and under-appreciated:

**1. The revenue line is mostly the government paying half of X-Energy's own bills, not customers buying things.** In the June quarter, revenue was $54.6M. Of that, **$50.1M (92%) came from cost-reimbursement contracts** and **$47.7M (87%) came from a single counterparty, the Department of Energy**. Commercial revenue — actual third-party customers — was **$5.3M**. Source: the 10-Q filed 2026-08-13 (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2088896/000119312526347752/xe-20260630.htm). Cost reimbursement means roughly zero margin by construction: the more the company spends, the more "revenue" it reports. Comparing X-Energy's $54.6M to a rival's $75k is comparing two different kinds of number.

**2. The one real project isn't X-Energy's to fund.** The flagship is four Xe-100 units at Dow's Seadrift, Texas plant. The owner is **Long Mott Energy LLC, a wholly-owned Dow subsidiary** (https://corporate.dow.com/en-us/news/press-releases/dow-and-x-energy-submit-construction-permit-application-to-the-u.html). The DOE is putting in up to $2.15B on a 50/50 basis — **Dow has to match it**. Dow has not announced a final decision to build. Meanwhile Dow cut its dividend by half, cut a billion dollars of 2025 capex, and shelved its comparable Fort Saskatchewan decarbonization project indefinitely. X-Energy is the vendor; the decision that matters is made in someone else's boardroom.

**3. The valuation already assumes most of a pipeline that isn't contracted.** X-Energy talks about roughly 144 units / 11.5 gigawatts of pipeline. Four of those are under a real project. Using the benches' own vendor economics (about $400M of X-Energy scope per unit at a mature 25% gross margin, plus about $6M/year of fuel margin per unit), today's ~$5.5B enterprise value implies somewhere between **88 and 122 units delivered** — depending on which discount factor you use. The only-what's-contracted case is worth about **$5.66 a share**, of which **$4.68 is just the cash on the balance sheet**.

Put plainly: at $18.19 you are paying roughly three times the value of everything currently under contract, for an option on 140 units that no one has signed for, funded by a chemical company that is conserving cash.

## What the market believes

Eight sell-side analysts cover it. Average price target **$38**, high $57, **low $23** — the lowest target on the street is still 26% above today's price. Ratings are 4 Strong Buy / 2 Buy / 2 Hold. Consensus 2026 revenue is **$213.5M**, with losses of about $0.61/share this year and $0.54 next (https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/xe/forecast/).

The market's story is: this is the best-capitalized name in advanced nuclear — about $1.9B of cash, no debt, a live NRC construction-permit docket, a fuel factory going up in Oak Ridge, and the biggest electricity-demand story in a decade (AI data centers) behind it. Four institutions filed new ownership disclosures in the five weeks after the IPO, so real money has been buying at these levels.

The mechanical part of the disagreement is clean: **the street applies a revenue multiple (~34x enterprise value to 2026 revenue) to a line that is 92% zero-margin reimbursement.** Even the street's own lowest target of $23 implies more units than the entire announced pipeline under our margin assumptions. Either their per-unit economics are far richer than ours, or they are capitalizing a pass-through.

We should also state the uncomfortable version: **the market is not obviously wrong about the direction.** It is wrong, if it is wrong, about how much of an unsigned pipeline you should pay for today.

## Why we might have edge

- **We separated the two numbers the story fuses.** Bulls cite +154% revenue growth; bears cite cash burn going from -$20.0M to -$97.3M in a quarter. On a 50/50 cost-share these are the same activity measured twice. The blue bench correctly pushed back on the red bench here — the reimbursement *reduces* the burn, it doesn't cause it — but both benches agree the number is not an earnings stream. Almost no published model says this out loud.
- **We relocated the funding risk to the right party.** The court's original hypothesis was that X-Energy faces a ~$2B equity call. That is wrong and our own red bench killed it: X-Energy *receives* DOE money. The real exposure is Dow's willingness to write the matching check — a risk that is worse, because we can't monitor it and can't influence it.
- **We found the earliest tell for that risk.** Dow's payments to X-Energy as a related party **fell from $2.857M to $2.394M year over year** (-16%). It is a small number and it may just be work shifting from Dow-funded engineering into DOE-cost-shared licensing — Dow did file the construction permit and did clear its environmental review in the same window, which are expensive things a disengaging partner doesn't do. But it is the line that would move first if Dow slowed down, and nobody watches it.
- **We read the commitment schedule.** X-Energy's only disclosed purchase commitment is **$50.9M of graphite through 2029**. No engineering-and-construction contract, no long-lead forgings, no reactor vessel. For a plant claimed to deliver power in 2030-31, that is the signature of a project that has not been given the go-ahead.
- **The disagreement is gradeable.** We have an external anchor (the analyst range, $23–$57) and a named mechanism for why we differ. That's the shape of a real call rather than a vibe.

## Why it might be priced in

- **The October share unlock is on everyone's calendar.** 180 days from the 2026-04-23 IPO lands around **2026-10-20/21**, freeing 118.9M Class B shares plus most pre-IPO Class A. This is public arithmetic from the prospectus. It is a reason to time an entry, not a reason to think we know something.
- **This is a crowded, loud trade.** AI-power/nuclear is the most-discussed retail theme of 2026. Where a story is this well-covered, our starting assumption should be that obvious facts are in the price.
- **The cost-share disclosure is in the 10-Q.** Any analyst can read the same table we read. Their models show large losses through 2027, so they clearly know the company is pre-commercial. They may simply be valuing it as an option and not pretending the revenue is profit.
- **The stock has already fallen 51%.** A lot of disappointment is priced. Buying more downside at a 51% drawdown is a well-known way to be late to a bear case.
- **The institutions filing ownership disclosures bought here, not at $37.** Someone with a real process thinks $18 is attractive.
- **Our own bear case has a hole in it.** The valuation kill (below) does not survive its own arithmetic cleanly. That is the single best argument that we are talking ourselves into a view.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — Dow says yes and the stock re-rates without us.** A Dow final investment decision, or an engineering-and-construction award that turns the $50.9M commitment line into $500M+, converts the whole thesis. The stock would gap on that news and our $12.75 gate would never fill. We would have been right about the facts and made nothing. *Monitorable:* yes — 8-K, Dow capital guidance, the commitment schedule in the next 10-Q. This is the most likely way we lose, and what we lose is opportunity, not capital.

**Failure narrative 2 — we buy the unlock dip and it keeps going.** Our gate is at $12.75, which is **below the 52-week low of $13.29** — it has never traded there. The realistic path to it is the October unlock producing forced-looking supply. If we buy that supply, we are on the other side of pre-IPO holders who know the project timeline better than we do. If the unlock reveals genuine insider exit rather than mechanical rebalancing, $12.75 is not a floor, it's a waypoint; the contracted-only value is $5.66. *Monitorable:* partly — post-unlock ownership filings and any follow-on offering registration would confirm it after the fact, which is too late to help the first tranche.

**Failure narrative 3 — the reactor or the fuel doesn't work on schedule, and we never see it coming.** The Xe-100 needs TRISO fuel, which needs high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU), which is allocated by the Department of Energy through a non-public process, from a domestic supply chain that barely exists. A slip in fuel qualification or a HALEU allocation that goes to a competitor would push first power years right and reprice everything — and **there is no filing, no docket entry, and no data feed that would tell us in advance.** Neither bench raised it until the blue bench flagged it as unverified. **This is our no-tripwire risk, and its unknown-unknown class is: single-source input allocated inside a government process with no public disclosure plane.** The same class covers any NRC technical issue resolved in non-public correspondence. We cannot instrument it; we can only size for it.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Our proposed position is 0.5% of book, capped at 1.0% — small enough that our own buying is not the issue, *except* that **we never measured this stock's average daily volume**, so we cannot say we are inside our own ≤1% of volume rail. That is an unfixed gap in this deck. The bigger reflexive problem is that our entry level and our predicted flow are the same event: we are proposing to bid into a dip we ourselves forecast, which is exactly the pattern that turns a "cheap" fill into adverse selection. And two of our own doctrines pull against each other here — the rule that a widely-known calendar event (the unlock) can't by itself justify sitting out, versus the rule that a valuation disagreement should set a price, never a refusal to participate. The blue bench resolved that by converting the bear case into a price gate rather than a permanent no. That resolution is correct in form but it produces a gate 30% below the market, which in practice is indistinguishable from a no — an honest reader should call this what it is: **a polite way of passing.**

If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be ___ that we bought the October unlock dip from pre-IPO holders who knew, better than we did, that Dow was not going to fund Seadrift on the announced schedule.
