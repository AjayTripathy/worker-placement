## What we believe

Ebara Foods is a small Japanese food company built around one thing people actually ask for by name: yakiniku (grilled-meat) sauce, where it is the #1 brand. It is unusually financially safe — 71% of the balance sheet is shareholder equity, debt is close to zero, and a large share of the market value is just cash sitting on the books.

Two things are true at once, and the deck refuses to collapse them:

1. **The business genuinely got better this quarter.** Operating profit — the money the actual business made before financing and one-offs — rose 20% year over year (¥818M vs ¥680M). That is a real, verified improvement, most plausibly because the company finally pushed higher input costs through to shelf prices.
2. **The eye-catching headline is flattered.** The "profit up ~4x" number everyone will quote is a bottom-line comparison against a weak prior-year bottom line. It is arithmetically correct and economically misleading. Twenty percent is the honest number.

The stock trades at 0.69x book value. Strip out the cash, and you are paying a very small price for a profitable branded business. But cheap-on-assets is not a thesis by itself. A cash-rich food brand only re-rates when returns on capital inflect or when management gives the cash back. Neither has happened yet. Until one does, this is a watch item, not a buy.

## What the market believes

The market believes this is a shrinking domestic staple in a shrinking country, and prices it accordingly — below the accounting value of its own net assets. Specifically, the market is pricing:

- Japan's population is falling, so domestic food volumes fall with it.
- Supermarket private-label products keep taking share from branded sauces on price.
- Input costs (soy, wheat, sugar, packaging, energy) squeeze the middle.
- The cash pile will sit there forever and never reach shareholders — so discount it.

That last belief is the one the market is *most* confident about and the one most likely to break. It is also, notably, the only one of the four our own red bench did not put a number behind.

## Why we might have edge

- **We separated the two profit lines and the market headline did not.** The financial press number is "4x." Ours is "+20% operating, and here's why the 4x is inflated at both ends." Anyone who buys the 4x will be disappointed at the next print; anyone who dismisses the whole thing as an accounting artifact will miss a genuine 20%.
- **We caught a factual error in the bull case before acting on it.** The dividend is 1.93%, not the 3.3% our blue bench claimed. That matters more than it sounds — see below.
- **We know the seasonal shape.** January–March runs an *operating loss* every year (−¥584M). October–December carries the whole year (+¥1,912M operating profit). Most casual readers will treat a loss-making Q4 as deterioration. It isn't; it's the calendar. This gives us a cheap-entry window in a predictable place and protects us from panicking at a scheduled loss.
- **Almost nobody covers this.** It is a small Tokyo-listed name with no US listing and thin English-language coverage. Mispricing survives longer here than in a screened, crowded name.

## Why it might be priced in

Stated plainly, because this could easily be a value trap:

- **0.69x book has been available for a long time.** A discount that persists is usually a market judgment, not a market oversight. The judgment is: the cash is not yours.
- **The red bench's three objections are real and structural.** Private-label erosion, input-cost inflation, and Japanese demographic decline are not fixable by one good quarter of price increases.
- **Price increases are a one-time lever, not a growth engine.** If the +20% is entirely pass-through, it laps out in a year and reverts to flat.
- **The carry argument is now dead.** The original bull case said: even if nothing happens, you're paid 3.3% to wait. At 1.93% you are paid roughly half that. In a name whose whole appeal was "safe and patient," the thing that made waiting tolerable is 1.7x smaller than advertised. This single correction is why the verdict is WATCH and not STARTER.

## What remains unverified

Stated flatly, without hedging:

1. **The net cash figure.** ¥16.72B / 67.8% of market value was never graded. Everything that makes this look cheap-after-cash depends on it. To be explicit about how much rides on it: *if* it holds, the enterprise value — market value minus net cash — is roughly ¥7.9B, against a business that produced about ¥2.1B of operating profit in the three quarters we can see. That would be an extremely low price. **That entire calculation is desk arithmetic on an unverified input and should not be repeated as fact until the balance sheet is opened.**
2. **What happened below the operating line.** Prior-year Q1 converted only 24% of operating profit into net profit; this year converted 78%. Normal is 65–70%. Something depressed last year and something may be helping this year. Until we read the actual P&L detail, we do not know whether even the *net* improvement is clean.
3. **Whether the +20% is durable or a one-time price increase.** This is the whole gate. We have one quarter.
4. **The full-year operating profit.** We have three quarters of shape (Q1 +¥818M, Oct–Dec +¥1,912M, Jan–Mar −¥584M) and no verified Jul–Sep figure. Any full-year multiple in circulation, including any implied above, assumes a quarter we have not seen.
5. **The originals.** We have read a data aggregator, not the company's filings. The quarterly report on TDnet and the annual securities report have not been opened. Standard practice says pull the equity and share-count notes from the primary document before quoting per-share or net-cash math.
6. **Red's three objections — none of them quantified.** No private-label share trend, no input-cost curve, no volume-vs-price decomposition of the revenue line. We rejected this name at 9/10 conviction on assertions. If we reopen it, red gets re-run with evidence or its score gets marked down.
7. **Whether we can trade it, and whether orders are live.** Both open.
