**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION: Do not buy today. Re-open with a starter position of at most 1.5% of the book only if the stock trades at or below ~$152 (confirming a forward P/E under 17× at the moment of entry), or after the November 4, 2026 report if organic growth of 4–5% and free cash flow hold with no further guidance cut.**

## What we believe

Honeywell Aerospace (HONA) is a newly spun-off, high-quality aerospace franchise — record backlog reported up 9%, orders up 8% — that stumbled badly in its first quarter as a standalone company. Management cut its full-year operating-profit guidance by roughly $300 million because a shortage at a small number of casting suppliers (about 2% of the supply base) is starving the high-margin aftermarket business. We believe the underlying demand is real and the lost profit is mostly deferred rather than destroyed: planes still need these parts, and the backlog did not shrink.

But we also now believe — after our own court process corrected us twice — that this is not the screaming bargain our original write-up described. The company carries roughly $14.8 billion of net debt (it issued $16 billion of bonds at the spin and sent $15.1 billion of the proceeds to its former parent, Honeywell). Including that debt, the whole business is valued at about $68 billion, which works out to roughly 15.5× this year's cut operating-profit guidance and about 21.7× guided earnings per share at the recent ~$168.51 price. That is cheap **relative to peers** — GE Aerospace, Safran, and RTX trade 25–50% higher on the same measures — but it is not distressed. The discount is at least partly fair compensation for leverage near 2.9× cash earnings (peers run 1–2×) and for a management team that cut guidance eight weeks after its Investor Day.

Net: the right posture is patience with pre-set triggers, not a purchase today.

## What the market believes

The market's verdict has been harsh and, importantly, was forming **before** the bad quarter. From its post-spin high of $297.50, the stock had already fallen about 32% by August 5. The earnings report on August 6 — the guidance cut of roughly 11–14% versus the analyst consensus of $8.86 in earnings per share — knocked it down a further ~25% intraday to a low near $150, since retraced partially to ~$168.51. The stock sits about 43% below its high.

The market appears to believe some combination of: (1) the supply problem will last into 2027 and may get worse before it gets better (management itself said resolution comes "2027 at the earliest," and re-qualifying aerospace casting suppliers typically takes 12–24 months); (2) guidance from this team cannot yet be trusted, since the cut came weeks after they marketed $16 billion of bonds on the old plan; and (3) the leverage deserves a meaningful discount to cleaner-balance-sheet peers. The CNBC Investing Club publicly downgraded the stock on August 5, which adds a retail-flow headwind. Sell-side price targets (~$239 average, Buy-rated) are mostly stale, set before the cut.

## Why we might have edge

- **The peer gap is large and specific.** At ~13.4× enterprise value to cash operating earnings, HONA trades 25–50% below GE Aerospace (28.2×), Safran (18.6×), and RTX (~18.5×) — figures our second bench verified the same week. Even granting a leverage discount, that gap prices in a lot of pessimism against freshly *cut* (i.e., de-risked) numbers.
- **The problem is narrow and demand-side intact.** The constraint is ~2% of suppliers. Backlog grew. Deferred aftermarket revenue in aerospace historically comes back — planes must be maintained.
- **The balance sheet is levered, not impaired.** Operating profit covers interest about 5.5 times over, and management still guides to $1.0–1.5 billion of free cash flow in the second half — roughly $2–3 billion a year of debt-paydown capacity. This is a company that can grow into its debt.
- **Forced and sentiment-driven selling is plausible.** A six-week-old ticker sliding from the start is also the classic window in which index funds and inherited holders sell regardless of price — the tape is consistent with rejection *or* mechanical selling; it doesn't prove the bearish read.
- **Insider signal is about to become informative.** Executives were in a blackout from the June 29 spin through the first report. Now the window is open: any open-market purchases from here are real information (the CEO's direct stake is tiny — 2,261 shares — and his options are struck at $190.51, so he has reason to care).

## Why it might be priced in

- **The discount has identifiable causes.** Leverage of ~2.9× versus peers' 1–2×, plus a management team that is now twice-burned on credibility (Investor Day, then the bond roadshow, then the cut), can justify most or all of a 25–50% multiple gap. "Cheaper than GE" is not automatically mispricing.
- **There is no dated catalyst.** The fix arrives "2027 at the earliest," the repair is expensive (tooling spend must double, and there was an inventory write-down taken even alongside the record backlog), and first cuts in supply-chain stories have a poor base rate — a second cut into the November 4 report is a live risk with no hedge.
- **The panic price is gone.** The stock has already bounced ~8–12% off the low; roughly a third of the crash-day move retraced before we could act. Our evidence pack's price ($156.47) was stale by the time the benches checked — the live mark was $168.51.
- **The demand numbers are management's numbers.** Backlog and order growth are company-reported metrics, not audited line items (true of all aerospace peers, but still — the deferral thesis rests on them).
