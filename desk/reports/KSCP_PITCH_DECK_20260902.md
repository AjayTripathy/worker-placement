# PITCH — KSCP — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — no position, no order, no entry price; both benches reject both directions, and this deck asks for that rejection to be ratified with the reopen conditions below.

---

## What we believe

Knightscope sells security robots on a subscription. For a decade the robots have cost more to run than customers pay for them. In February 2026 the company bought a human-staffed event-security business (Event Risk, LLC, closed 2026-02-27), and the combined revenue line tripled. That triple is the acquisition, not the robots.

The specifics we are willing to stand behind:

- **The robot business, isolated, still loses money on every dollar of revenue.** The Q2 filing's acquisition note discloses what the acquired unit contributed, so the legacy business can be backed out by subtraction: first-half 2026 legacy revenue **$5.84M** (vs $5.67M a year earlier — **+3%**) on **negative $0.87M** gross profit. Cost of revenue is **114.9%** of revenue — and that is the best half-year the legacy business has ever printed. The acquired human guards earn a **21.7%** gross margin; the robots earn about **−15%**.
- **Like-for-like growth is 12%, not 165%.** The same filing gives pro-forma prior-period combined figures: first-half 2025 combined revenue **$13.40M** vs first-half 2026 actual **$15.04M** = **+12.3%**. On that identical perimeter the combined net loss *widened* from **−$14.54M** to **−$24.41M** — 68% worse.
- **The company is out of cash and must sell stock.** Cash went $20.6M (12/31/25) → $11.4M (3/31/26) → **$8.16M (6/30/26)**. First-half operating cash burn was **−$23.10M**, matched by a net loss of **−$24.41M** (so it is real losses, not a working-capital swing). Financing supplied **+$17.91M** in the same half. The Q2 10-Q carries live going-concern language: *"substantial doubt about the Company's ability to continue as a going concern for the twelve months from the date of this report."* ([Q2 2026 10-Q, filed 2026-08-12](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1600983/000110465926094859/kscp-20260630x10q.htm))
- **The share count is the moving part, not the price.** Weighted diluted shares went **6,995,145 → 17,925,096** in four quarters — a 2.56× — *after* a roughly 1-for-50 reverse split. There have been ten shelf takedowns since 2023-02-09.
- **Even a fully-funded version isn't worth today's price.** If someone handed the company all the money it needs, you'd own a business annualizing ~$36M of mostly human-guard revenue. Guarding companies trade around 0.6× sales → about **$21.6M** enterprise value. Today's enterprise value, counting $4.39M of debt and ~$4.7M of deferred acquisition payments, is **$28–33M**. The best case sits *below* the current price.

Conclusion: **flat.** Not because it's cheap-but-early — because the funded outcome is worth less than the tape, and the funding itself destroys the buyer.

## What the market believes

At **$1.35** the stock is down **82.3%** from its 52-week high and sits **2.3%** above its 52-week low of $1.32. That is a market that has already priced dilution and did not reward the "revenue triples" headline.

But the market is still paying about **$32M** of equity value for a business whose fully-funded guarding-comp value is roughly **$22M**. So the market and we disagree in one specific place: the market is paying roughly **$10M of option value** for the possibility that the robot line eventually works, or that the "434 clients across 42 states / Autonomous Security Force" story gets bought, funded, or squeezed. We think that option is worth materially less than $10M, because the entity that owns it is arithmetically compelled to sell shares against it.

The other thing the market appears to believe — reasonably — is that this is a retail-narrative stock. That belief is why the short doesn't work either.

## Why we might have edge

1. **The acquisition disclosure lets us split the company; almost nobody does that arithmetic.** The headline press release is titled ["Preliminary Q2 Revenue Triples to Approximately $9M"](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1600983/000110465926084853/kscp-20260720xex99d1.htm) with no attribution to the acquisition anywhere in it. The acquisition is disclosed in the 8-K/A and the 10-Q note. A reader of the press release and a reader of the filing come away with opposite pictures of the same quarter. That gap between what the company says loudly and what it files quietly is a repeatable pattern, and it is the reason we can price this business and a headline-reader can't.
2. **Pro-forma prior-period numbers are published and almost never used.** The filing hands us the like-for-light comparison (+12.3% revenue, 68% worse loss). It takes one subtraction and no modeling.
3. **The company is an orphan.** No usable analyst coverage was obtainable. There is no consensus number for the tape to converge to, which means fundamental work has less competition here than usual. (We do not treat this as a reason to buy — it cuts both ways.)
4. **We were willing to look at the enterprise value properly.** Adding debt and earnout consideration moved the fully-funded prize from "equal to the tape" to "below the tape." That flipped the answer from *maybe at a lower price* to *no price we can name today*.

Honest limit on all four: **none of this is a mispricing we get paid for.** Being right that the equity is a residual claim on a company that must issue 145% of its own market cap to survive a year does not, by itself, produce a trade. It produces an exclusion. That is the value here.

## Why it might be priced in

Plainly: most of it probably is.

- Going-concern doubt is in the audited filing, in the quarterly, and in the press coverage. Everyone can read it.
- An 82% drawdown to within 2% of the low is not a market that missed the story.
- Serial dilution from a company with ten shelf takedowns is the most-anticipated event in the microcap universe.
- The one genuinely under-read item — that the triple is entirely acquired — is under-read by *headline* readers, but the stock's behavior (it did not sustain a re-rate on the "triples" PR) suggests the marginal price-setter already discounted it.

So our differentiated content is narrow: the exact size of the legacy business's negative gross margin, the like-for-like loss trajectory, and the shrinking backlog. Those sharpen the case; they do not create a payday. **A pitch whose edge is "we understand why it's cheap and it's still not cheap enough" is correctly resolved as flat, not as a trade.**

## What could go wrong

*(Pre-mortem. Assume the adjudicator overrides this deck and puts on a small long, since that is the only direction anyone would be tempted by.)*

**Failure 1 — We buy the residual and the financing takes it. (Most likely. Tripwire exists.)**
Cash was $8.16M on 6/30/26 against roughly $11.5M of quarterly operating burn. That is about nine weeks. Nine weeks from 6/30 is *now*. The company raises — via the standing at-the-market program, a new shelf takedown, or a structured deal — at whatever price clears. The share count goes up 30–100%, the stock goes down, and the business is unchanged. We lose 30–50% on the announcement and never get it back, because the same thing happens next quarter.
*Tripwire:* any 424B5, S-3, or 8-K Item 1.01/3.02 financing disclosure; and the share-count line on the next 10-Q cover page.
*Important caveat:* at-the-market sales **do not require an 8-K**. They surface in the next quarterly. So this tripwire is partially blind for up to three months — which is exactly long enough to lose the money.

**Failure 2 — The acquired business turns out to be the whole company, and then it gets impaired. (Tripwire exists but fires late.)**
$23.2M of a $55.9M balance sheet is goodwill and customer intangibles from the acquisition, against $29.6M of book equity. Event-security is a low-margin, high-churn, people-dependent business bought for ~$18M by a company that had ~$20M of cash at the time. If the acquired customer relationships don't renew, a single impairment charge takes book equity below the market value of the stock, and the "we at least own a real guarding business" floor disappears.
*Tripwire:* annual impairment testing disclosed in the FY2026 10-K (~late March 2027); any interim triggering-event charge; guard headcount or client-count disclosure reversing.
*Why it fires late:* impairment is recognized after the deterioration, not during it. By the time the charge prints, the price has it.

**Failure 3 — The numbers we built the entire case on are management's, unaudited, and wrong. (NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE.)**
Our decisive finding — that the robot line runs at 114.9% cost while the acquired guards run at 21.7% — does not come from a segment disclosure the company is audited on. It comes from subtracting a management-prepared acquisition-contribution note inside a **reviewed, not audited**, interim filing. Purchase-price allocation, revenue attribution between the legacy and acquired lines, and the allocation of shared cost of revenue are all management judgments made by a company with an acute incentive to make the robot line look less bad. If those allocations are aggressive — or simply wrong — our split is wrong in an unknowable direction, and we would have both rejected and (on override) sized a position on arithmetic that a restatement erases.
**Unknown-unknown class: audit-scope gap — management-prepared allocations inside an unaudited interim disclosure, where the only observable confirmation is a restatement that arrives after the fact.** There is no filing, price, or dataset we can watch that tells us in advance whether a purchase-price allocation is honest. The sibling class, equally untripwired here, is **private-negotiation blindness**: a take-private, a strategic investment, or a large municipal contract is unobservable until the 8-K that has already repriced the stock. That one hurts the *flat* decision rather than a long, and we accept it.

**The reflexive / structural leg.**

- **We would be bidding directly into a seller we ourselves predict.** Our own arithmetic says a share sale is due within weeks. Buying stock in front of a supply event we forecast is the single clearest rule violation available to us here. This is not a marginal consideration — it is the reason the long is dead independent of valuation.
- **Our size vs. daily volume is UNMEASURED.** We did not pull average daily volume this session — a real gap. Directionally: a 0.25% starter against roughly $5M of deployable capital is about $12,500, or ~9,300 shares, which is almost certainly immaterial to this tape. The liquidity risk is not entry — it is *exit* on a gap-down financing print, where a $1.35 stock can trade $0.90 before we get a fill. Small size does not protect the exit; it just makes the loss small.
- **Doctrine interactions.** (a) Our rule against shorting heavily-retail-traded microcaps closes the short leg regardless of how right the fundamental case is. (b) Blue added the mechanic that seals it: at $1.35 against a $1.32 low, a Nasdaq $1.00 minimum-bid deficiency and another reverse split are the live path — and this company has already run one. Post-split float compression into a robot narrative is precisely the setup that kills shorts. (c) Our rule that a flat call must name a specific kill is satisfied four times over. (d) A falling price alone is not a dislocation; −82% here is a continuous bleed, not a shock off a base, so the "buy the dislocation" template does not apply. (e) Any position would be on a short-term tax clock — immaterial at this size but noted.
- **One structural risk to being flat:** our own rejection ratchet. We have now rejected this name on strong evidence. If the funding picture genuinely changes, the memory of a 9/10-conviction kill will make us slow to reopen. The reopen conditions below exist to force that re-look mechanically rather than leaving it to whoever remembers.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we bought a residual claim weeks before a dilutive equity raise we ourselves forecast, and the share count — not the price — was the variable that moved.**
