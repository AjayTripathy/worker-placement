Viemed Healthcare (VMD): a −15.9% answer to a −1.5% question — worth owning lower, not worth chasing here

Verdict: 5/10 — WATCH, below-market ladder only. This is not an at-market buy. Viemed is a net-cash home-ventilator specialist that fell −15.9% on August 4 against a full-year EBITDA guide trim of exactly $1M (−1.5%) — a revision that was actually free-cash-flow accretive, because the capex guide fell more than the EBITDA give-up. The market's real complaint is legitimate: the unchanged back half requires a margin step with no precedent in the company's own history, and a fresh, dated Medicare documentation rule lands on its largest billing code mid-quarter, unmentioned. So the plan is a three-rung limit ladder that monetizes any further overshoot, with the bottom rung below bear-case fair value — paid for the regulatory risk even if it bites.

03

Where the review corrected its own opening take — printed here on purpose

Our first note framed this as "a record quarter punished for a rounding-error trim." Three of that note's load-bearing readings did not survive verification against the company's own filings, and the corrected versions are less flattering:

  • "Record census" conceals a three-quarter stall. The 12,635 ventilator-patient census is a record, and the release's +4.0% y/y / +4.5% sequential are accurate. But run the company's own two growth rates backward: June-2025 census was 12,149 and March-2026 was 12,090 — below where it stood nine months earlier. Census was flat-to-down for three quarters, then jumped once in Q2. "Record and accelerating" is true arithmetic that masks a stall.
  • 62.9% of the revenue growth is resupply, not the ventilator pillar. Of the +$15.0M y/y revenue growth, equipment & supply sales contributed +$9.5M (62.9%); ventilator rentals contributed +$2.6M (17.2%, i.e. +7.7% y/y). The growth engine is the lower-margin resupply business, not the pillar the release leads with.
  • Mix explains only about a third of the margin compression. Adjusted EBITDA margin fell 502bp y/y (22.7% → 17.6%). The clean bridge:

Why −15.9% was mostly deserved: the unchanged back half is arithmetically heroic

Estimates barely moved — the guide trim was $1M. What broke was belief in the bridge. H1 came in at an 18.26% adjusted-EBITDA margin ($28.0M on $153.5M). The unchanged guide midpoint therefore requires H2 to deliver $38.0M on $163.5M — a 23.23% margin, a +497bp half-over-half step. Last year's actual H1→H2 step was +104bp.

  • The guide needs H2 quarters averaging $19.0M — 38.6% above Q2's $13.7M, and above the all-time record quarter ($18.2M, Q4-25). Two consecutive quarters averaging above the record, guided as routine.
  • The eight-quarter read is worse than any single quarter: revenue grew +34.7% over two years (58.0 → 78.1) while adjusted-EBITDA dollars went 13.95 → 13.72, i.e. −1.6%. Two years of revenue growth have produced zero EBITDA dollars, and Q2 is the second consecutive sequential decline off the Q4-25 peak.
  • Apply last year's actual +104bp seasonal step to the H1 base: H2 margin 19.3% → H2 EBITDA $31.6M → FY26 $59.6M, 6.9% below the low end of the guide. Even a generous +200bp step lands at $61.2M — still below the low end.
Our number: p(FY26 adjusted EBITDA lands inside the $64–68M guide) = 0.30–0.35. The −15.9% is not an overreaction to a $1M trim; it is the market marking the probability that the unchanged back half is unreachable. The honest offset the market ignored: the capex guide was lowered to 8.5–10.0% of revenue (from 9.0–10.5%) because the growth is shifting to less capital-intensive revenue — at the $317M midpoint that saves ~$1.6M of capex against ~$1.0M of EBITDA given up, so the revision is free-cash-flow accretive and EBITDA is the wrong metric for this mix shift. Real, but not enough to make the name a buy at market.
05

The regulatory file — the feared channel is refuted; a different one is live and dated

Bear-refuting, verified on primary sources: competitive bidding is NOT a kill channel on the visible horizon. CMS removed non-invasive ventilators from Round 2021 before implementation, then also removed oxygen and PAP; those contracts expired December 2023. The next bidding round is limited to remote-item-delivery categories — continuous glucose monitors, insulin pumps, urological and ostomy supplies, catheters, off-the-shelf braces. Viemed furnishes none of these. The rate channel is also benign: the CY2026 fee-schedule update is +2.0%. Anyone pitching the bear on bidding rounds or rate cuts is refuted by the rule text itself.

What IS live — found by the review, not by the company:

  • CMS-6109-N (91 FR 47972), published July 30, 2026 — four days before the print. It adds E0466 and E0467 — home ventilators, Viemed's largest revenue code — to the required face-to-face-encounter and written-order-prior-to-delivery list, effective October 28, 2026. CMS's stated rationale: ventilators sit "within the top 20 DMEPOS service types with improper payments in 2025" and were flagged as "vulnerable to fraud." Scoped precisely: this is a documentation condition of payment at setup — it is NOT prior authorization and NOT a rate cut.
  • An adverse audit ruling on exactly this code, disclosed only in the 10-Q. A CMS program-integrity contractor (Qlarant) issued an extrapolated overpayment determination on the core operating subsidiary's Medicare claims for monthly rental of non-invasive home ventilators: ~$5.9M, reduced to ~$2.2M on appeal — and in June 2026 an administrative law judge ruled against the company on the remainder. Viemed paid in April 2026 (booked to prepaid expenses, no accrual, further appeal intended). The dollars are immaterial; the mechanism is not: a lost extrapolated audit on ventilator medical-necessity documentation invites further audits, prepayment review, and permanently higher documentation cost per patient.
  • Magnitude, bounded from analogues: when home health got a face-to-face requirement in 2013–14, setups slowed 5–15% and denials spiked for 2–4 quarters until referral workflows adapted. Medicare ventilator setups are ~16% of Viemed's revenue; a 10% setup slowdown for 2–3 quarters is ~1.6% of revenue at peak, plus incremental documentation cost on an SG&A line already growing 5pp faster than revenue.
  • The latency trap: Q4-26 sees only ~2 months of exposure and the Q3 print (~Nov 3) lands before any of it. The first true read on this channel is the Q4 print, ~March 2027. A good Q3 does not retire the regulatory question.
The bull counter is real and is the basis of our bull scenario: Viemed's model staffs licensed respiratory therapists who visit patients — it may already collect the face-to-face documentation the rule requires. If commodity DME rivals cannot comply cheaply, the rule becomes a referral-share moat rather than a tax. Nobody knows yet; that is exactly why the position is a ladder with a dated review, not a conviction buy.

The honesty read: every number reconciles — the story is what diverges

This is not a fraud finding. Every figure in the release ties to the audited filing data. The finding is takeaway-vs-data divergence by omission, at the exact moment the omitted risk acquired a date.

The marketed takeawayThe company's own file, same day

The June 2026 adverse judge's ruling on extrapolated Medicare overpayments — on the largest revenue code — appears in the 10-Q filed that evening and nowhere in the press release; it appears zero times in the prior annual report and zero times in the Q1 filing
The CMS documentation rule published four days earlier with an effective date inside the guided year: zero mentions of face-to-face or written-order requirements in the release or the 10-Q — while the 10-Q's risk-factor section states verbatim, "There have been no material changes in our risk factors"

The annual report even carried the contingent version: "if CMS were to implement prior authorization or additional documentation requirements applicable to products we furnish…". That hypothetical materialized on July 30 with a Federal Register citation and a date, and the 10-Q filed August 3 asserts no material change and never names it. Within the letter of the disclosure rules — each item individually defensible — but the pattern is a polished story diverging from its own regulatory file on the one axis that matters.

We grade this a flag, not fraud — and the flag has a price implication: under our discipline, a management this polished about its file is a name to buy lower, not a name to buy because it fell. It is a core reason the verdict is a ladder and not an entry.
07

The mix-shift bear, modeled instead of vibed — it's bounded, and SG&A is the real swing

The headline bear ("resupply is diluting the margin, the business is breaking") gets smaller when you put numbers on it. Solving the two segments' gross margins from the disclosed mix shift: rentals-and-services ~59.3% vs equipment/supply sales ~52.8% — only a 650bp reported gap (on a cash basis, adding back equipment depreciation, roughly 72.8% vs 52.8%).

  • Run resupply hot — +40%/+35%/+30%/+25% over four years — against +6% rentals: blended cash gross margin erodes only ~80bp per year (67.6% FY26 → 65.3% FY29).
  • With ~300bp/yr of SG&A leverage: adjusted-EBITDA margin settles at 20–22% and EBITDA dollars still compound ~18%/yr (FY26 ~$63M → FY29 ~$105M).
  • Without any SG&A leverage (SG&A grows with revenue): margin settles at 18–19% and EBITDA compounds ~14%/yr.

Either way, mix alone does not break the business. The swing factor is SG&A — the line that just grew 28.9% against 23.9% revenue growth, and the line the Q3 print will grade first.

Honest caveat: the company does not disclose segment margins. The 59.3%/52.8% split is a two-equation solve from the reported mix shift and blended gross margin, not a disclosed figure — and the three-year model rests on it.
