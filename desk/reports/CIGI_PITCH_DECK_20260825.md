# PITCH — CIGI — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — a 0.25% starter placed as a resting limit at $99.00 (not at the $105.55 tape), second rung 0.25% at $91.00, no at-market fill, with a mandatory re-hearing if the stock never comes to us.

## What we believe

Colliers is a global commercial-real-estate services and engineering firm. The stock is $105.55, down 38% from its 52-week high of $171.34, though it has already bounced 19% off its $88.38 low. It trades at roughly 14.2–14.5x our own build of this year's adjusted earnings.

Our belief, stated narrowly, is this: **the market de-rated Colliers as if commercial real estate transaction activity were dead, and the company's own filings say it is not.** In the June quarter, the Commercial Real Estate segment — which contains the leasing and capital-markets brokerage businesses that would show the damage first — printed $997.3M of revenue against $893.2M a year ago, up 11.7%. Company-wide "internal growth" (growth excluding acquisitions) was disclosed at 8% in local currency. Those are not the numbers of a broken transaction market.

We also believe the ugliest number in the quarter is largely an accounting artifact. Reported operating earnings were $98.4M against $99.2M last year — *down* 0.7% on revenue *up* 16.7%. That looks like a business losing all its operating leverage. But Colliers closed its $690.0M acquisition of Ayesa, a 3,300-person Spanish engineering firm, **late in the second quarter**. The quarter therefore absorbed essentially all of the deal's transaction costs and a stub of new amortization while booking almost none of Ayesa's revenue. Of the $22.2M year-over-year increase in operating expenses, $19.1M is amortization plus acquisition-related items. The margin "collapse" mechanically reverses next quarter when the acquired revenue actually shows up.

Our fair value, using 16–18x our estimate of $7.30 of adjusted earnings per share, is **$117–131, midpoint about $124** — roughly 18% above the current price. Street targets average $139.78; we are deliberately below them.

Source for every figure above: the company's 6-K filed 2026-08-05, exhibit 99.1 — https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/913353/000117184326005248/exh_991.htm — and the Q2 earnings release, 6-K exhibit 99.2 filed 2026-07-30 — https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000913353/000117184326005053/exh_992.htm

## What the market believes

The market is pricing three things, and it is not obviously wrong about any of them.

**One: earnings quality is deteriorating, permanently.** Colliers markets itself on "adjusted" earnings that exclude amortization of acquired intangibles and acquisition costs. The gap between reported earnings and adjusted earnings is not shrinking — it is being manufactured. Ayesa alone added $440.8M of goodwill and roughly $247M of new intangible assets that will amortize through 2027 and beyond. A buyer who does not accept the add-backs sees a company whose reported earnings went nowhere. Reported net earnings attributable to the company were $28.5M in the quarter on $1.57B of revenue.

**Two: the balance sheet got heavier fast.** Total debt went from $1.63B at year-end to $2.51B at June 30 — $872M added in six months. Interest expense was $26.4M in the quarter versus $15.5M a year ago, up 70%, and that was on a *partial-quarter* balance. Net leverage is about 2.8x against the company's own stated long-term target of 1.5–2.0x. A shelf registration (Form F-10) was filed 2026-03-27 and went effective 2026-04-02, so the company has live capacity to issue equity.

**Three: the second half has to do a lot of work.** Management's full-year guidance implies second-half adjusted earnings of roughly $4.77–4.83 per share against $4.00 in the prior-year second half — up 19–21% — at the same time management guided third-quarter leasing to mid-single-digit growth and capital markets to about 15%, down from 23%.

Put simply: the market thinks it is watching a roll-up buy revenue with debt to paper over a soft cycle. That is a coherent and respectable view.

## Why we might have edge

**The segment attribution.** The single most common bear argument — "their growth is bought, not earned" — is true in aggregate and false where it matters. Colliers spent $770.1M on acquisitions in the first half versus $59.7M a year earlier, a 13x step-up. But $690.0M of that $770.1M is Ayesa, and Ayesa is an **engineering** business, booked to the Engineering segment. The commercial-real-estate brokerage numbers that carry the thesis are in a different segment and are essentially untouched by it. Someone reading only the cash flow statement will impeach the wrong number. Our own bear bench did exactly that (see below), which is decent evidence the mistake is easy to make.

**The disclosed organic figure.** The company states internal growth of 8% in local currency in the release. Both of our benches initially argued around it rather than from it — the bear side asserted the organic split was "never stated," in a document it had itself cited. If our own analysts overlooked a printed number, sell-side notes summarizing "Adjusted EBITDA +14%" plausibly did too.

**The timing artifact.** A large deal closing in the last weeks of a quarter always produces one quarter of fake margin collapse. Screens and quant systems that read a reported operating-margin series will register real damage where none exists. That is a structural, repeatable source of mispricing, not a one-off.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly: quite a lot of it probably is, and this is the weakest part of the case.

- **We are only ~18% below our own fair value midpoint, and we are the optimists relative to nothing.** The bear bench built full-year earnings of $7.06–7.14; correcting for the Ayesa contribution it excluded gives $7.26–7.44. Street is $7.51–7.57. So our *entire* disagreement with the Street is 2–4% on the earnings number — inside normal estimate noise. This is not a case where we see something they don't; it is a case where we think a de-rating overshot by a modest amount.
- **The stock has already bounced 19% off the low.** Whatever fresh dislocation existed is partly harvested. We are not early.
- **The earnings-quality complaint is not refuted, only contextualized.** We argue this quarter's margin drop is an artifact. We do *not* argue that the growing gap between reported and adjusted earnings is an artifact — it is real and widening, and a multiple that permanently reflects that is a legitimate market judgment, not an error.
- **Nobody has demonstrated the multiple should re-expand.** 16–18x is our assumption, not a finding. The stock was 26x. If the correct number is 13–14x for a debt-funded serial acquirer, the stock is already fair and there is nothing here.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — The de-rating was correct and continues (most likely).**
We are calling a multiple compression a mistake when it is the market correctly repricing a business that must keep buying revenue to grow. Each acquisition manufactures new amortization faster than old amortization runs off, so the "adjusted" earnings we are paying 14x for drift further from cash reality every year. Reported net earnings attributable to shareholders were $28.5M in the quarter; on that number the stock is not cheap at any multiple. Stock-based compensation, also added back, grew 21% year over year ($55.6M in FY25 vs $46.0M in FY24). *Tripwire: the reported-to-adjusted gap widening again in Q3 and Q4; each new deal announcement above ~$200M.* This one is monitorable.

**Failure narrative 2 — The balance sheet forces the equity (severe, moderate probability).**
Net leverage is 2.8x against a 1.5–2.0x target, the shelf is effective, and there is a separate $1.09B claim (below) that can be settled in shares. If the second half disappoints and leverage does not come down, the natural fix is an equity raise into a depressed price — the worst possible sequence for us. *Tripwire: any prospectus supplement or drawdown under the F-10; leverage above 2.8x at the Q3 print; a further large acquisition announced without asset sales.* Monitorable.

**Failure narrative 3 — Producer attrition at a people business (NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE).**
Colliers has no factories and no owned buildings. Its earning assets are brokers, and brokers leave, taking client relationships with them. A roll-up that has just absorbed 3,300 engineers in Spain while integrating dozens of smaller tuck-ins is exactly where cultural and compensation friction shows up. There is no filing, no tape signal, and no third-party dataset that tells us a team walked out until it appears in a revenue line two or three quarters later — by which time the stock has already moved. **Unknown-unknown class: unobservable human-capital flight at a services roll-up.** We have no sensor for this and cannot build one. It is a live, uninsured exposure and we are accepting it knowingly. The related class — private-market counterparty and valuation risk inside the Investment Management segment, where marks are self-reported and lag — belongs in the same bucket.

**The reflexive and structural leg.**
- *Our size.* At 0.25% of book in a $5.4B-market-cap, dual-listed (Toronto and Nasdaq) name, our order is almost certainly a rounding error against daily volume. But we did not measure it: **average daily volume was not pulled this session**, so "we are small enough not to matter" is an assumption, not a verified fact. It should be verified before the order goes in.
- *Our own predicted flow.* Our first rung sits at $99.00 and the second at $91.00 — both below the market. We are not bidding into a decline we ourselves are predicting; we are waiting for one someone else causes. That is the right structure.
- *A doctrine interaction we should name plainly.* Our house rule says a clean court — one where the bear case produces no fatal objection — defaults to at least a small starter rather than doing nothing. In this case the bear bench raised five objections and the bull bench dismantled or downgraded all of them. So the starter here is generated by **the absence of a surviving kill**, not by the presence of a proven positive thesis. That is a real and uncomfortable asymmetry: "we couldn't find a reason not to" is a weaker foundation than "we found a reason to," and the rule mechanically converts one into the other. The adjudicator should treat this as the central question, not a footnote.
- *A second doctrine interaction.* Our rule that a resting order into an earnings print is an accidental bet on calm markets applies here. The $99.00 and $91.00 rungs must be pulled before the Q3 print (approximately 2026-10-28 to 2026-11-03) and re-placed after it, or they become an unintended bet that the print is quiet.
- *The de-facto-flat risk.* A ladder that never fills is indistinguishable from having done nothing, while still consuming attention. That is addressed in the entry section below with a forced re-hearing.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we treated a widening gap between reported and adjusted earnings as an accounting artifact of one late-closing deal, when it was actually the permanent operating condition of a debt-funded roll-up — and the market was re-rating earnings quality, not the real-estate cycle, the whole time.**

## What remains unverified

1. **Commercial Real Estate segment internal growth.** The company discloses 8% internal growth *company-wide*. It does not break internal growth out by segment. Roughly $80M of non-Ayesa tuck-in acquisitions is unallocated. So our central claim — that capital-markets growth is organic — is **inferred from segment attribution, not read off the page.** This is the load-bearing unverified item and the reason our own bull bench capped its conviction at 7 out of 10.
2. **A segment-level operating-earnings bridge.** We have argued the 110-basis-point margin decline is deal-timing plus mix. We have not seen a segment-level reconciliation of reported operating earnings proving it.
3. **Credit facility pricing.** We modelled second-half interest at roughly 5.7% implied from the quarter. The actual pricing grid on the facility was not pulled, so the +$8–9M per quarter figure is an estimate.
4. **The Q3 earnings date.** No company confirmation exists.
5. **Current price.** No independent live quote; broker data access was denied.
6. **Average daily trading volume.** Never measured. Our claim to be small enough not to move the stock is untested.
7. **The margin add-back discrepancy.** Our two benches computed +11.7% and +12.1% for the same bridge. Unreconciled.
8. **Whether 16–18x is the right multiple.** This is an assumption we imposed, not a conclusion we derived. If a debt-funded serial acquirer deserves 13x, the stock is fully priced today and this entire case dissolves.
9. **Third-quarter confirmation of the artifact thesis.** The core prediction — that operating margin recovers mechanically once Ayesa's revenue arrives — is testable exactly once, at the Q3 print, and has not been tested.
