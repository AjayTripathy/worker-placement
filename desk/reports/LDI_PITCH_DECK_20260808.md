# PITCH — LDI — PROPOSED VERDICT

**REJECT — no entry; tripwire-gated watch only. PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION.** (Red bench: REJECT, conviction 7/10. Blue bench: partially overturned red's reasoning but sustained the rejection on narrower grounds, conviction 8/10.)

## What we believe

loanDepot's business is genuinely getting better, and it doesn't matter yet — because the debt market is telling us the company may not make it to the payoff.

The improvement is real. In the second quarter of 2026, loanDepot originated $7.99 billion of mortgages, up 18.7% from the same quarter last year. Revenue rose 19.4% year over year to $337.3 million. The net loss narrowed to $6.6 million from $25.3 million a year ago, and loan units grew 26%, driven largely by a fast-ramping home-equity product built with Figure. Our red (bear) bench initially charged that this "improvement" was just normal spring-season strength dressed up as a turnaround; that charge was checked against the year-over-year numbers and it failed. The turnaround is not a seasonal illusion.

But the company owes $934.5 million on two bond maturities (November 2027 secured, April 2028 unsecured) against $229.1 million of cash — and that cash is down 44% from $408.6 million a year ago. The bonds tell the story: loanDepot bought back its own secured 2027 notes at 90 cents on the dollar in the second quarter and at 86 cents through late July. Buyers demanding roughly a 14–15% yield on *secured* 15-month paper are pricing meaningful odds that the refinancing fails. The stock, at $0.94, is the last-loss claim sitting underneath that paper. Meanwhile the company is selling $9.7 billion of its servicing portfolio (about 8% of it) to raise cash for debt repurchases — selling a piece of the asset that earns more when rates stay high, at a moment when mortgage rates are near 6.66%.

Our belief: this is a real operational recovery trapped inside an unresolved balance-sheet question, and the equity is not ownable until the debt question is answered.

## What the market believes

The stock closed at $0.9413, which is 0.9% above its 52-week low of $0.9325 and 81% below its 52-week high of $5.045. The market saw the same improving quarter we did — the earnings came out August 4 and the 10-Q on August 6 — and responded with the worst down day of the year (−10%) and a new low. The credit market is bidding the company's own secured bonds at 86–90 cents. In plain terms: the market believes the operational improvement is subordinate to the refinancing risk, which is exactly our conclusion. There is also a small activist (Randian Capital, holding roughly 250,000 shares) publicly urging the board to explore a sale ([open letter, July 15, 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/07/15/3327656/0/en/Randian-Capital-publishes-Open-Letter-to-loanDepot-Inc-Board-calling-for-them-to-consider-evaluating-strategic-alternatives.html)) — but founder Anthony Hsieh's voting control makes that optionality, not a catalyst.

## Why we might have edge

- **The bear consensus overstates two things, and we can prove it.** First, the "it's just seasonality" story is wrong — the year-over-year column is positive across originations, revenue, units, and loss. Second, "they sold their hedge" (the servicing book, which earns steadily while rates are high) is overstated roughly twelvefold: the sale is ~8% of a $123.4 billion portfolio, which still grew 5% year over year and stands near $113.7 billion after the sale.
- **The prize if the debt clears is large and quantified.** The entire equity is valued around $318 million for a company producing $337 million of revenue per quarter and now near breakeven. If a committed refinancing appears, that stub repricing could be violent. We know exactly which gates to watch (below), which most holders at $0.94 likely do not.
- **Price-as-verdict is weakest exactly here.** A sub-$1, founder-controlled stock with a shrinking analyst audience is where the market's "vote" is least informative — testing that is this desk's core purpose.

## Why it might be priced in

Symmetry requires saying this plainly: the market may simply be *right*. The bond market — a more sober judge than the stock market — is pricing material refinancing risk at 86–90 cents on secured paper, and cash halved in a year. The stock made a new low *after* the good quarter was public, so "the market hasn't seen the improvement" is not available to us; it saw it and sold. The sub-$1 price starts a New York Stock Exchange delisting clock (Rule 802.01C) once the 30-day average breaches $1, with a reverse split as the likely cure. And the insider signal is mildly negative: on August 7, five executives' performance-based stock awards were cancelled and replaced with simple time-based awards — meaning management itself did not expect the performance targets to pay.
