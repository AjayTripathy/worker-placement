02

The pattern that demands respect: two ~20% crashes in 90 days — both on beats

Twice this year Centuri has reported a quarter that beat expectations and lost a fifth of its value within a session. When a stock is punished twice for good headlines, the market is not confused — it is repricing the quality of the revenue, not the quantity. Our review examined whether that repricing was a mistake, and concluded it was not.

03

The margin autopsy: record revenue, zero incremental profit

First half of fiscal 2026: revenue up $411M (+32%). Operating income: $19.407M vs $19.446M a year ago — flat to the third decimal. That single comparison is the whole crash.

The $9M Chicago write-off is not part of this story. It is a 2019-vintage claim against the City of Chicago, denied by the agency in 2024 and now written off to zero after an April 2026 court order — a legacy legal item, fully excluded from the base-margin figures above, and one of the bear arguments our review killed (slide 07). The margin problem is in the live book, not the legal drawer.

Mix is compounding the squeeze: US Gas — the lowest-margin segment at a 5.95% base gross margin — grew from 46.5% to 50.9% of revenue year over year.

04

The structural finding: 77% of the book eats its own cost inflation, and "wait for H2" fails the history test

From the 10-Q's contract-type note: 77% of revenue is unit-price plus fixed-price work — the contractor, not the customer, bears cost increases. The escalators in those contracts reset annually and are capped, and the filing concedes it plainly: "our actual costs at times can exceed the contractual caps." This year's fuel and labor inflation is therefore genuinely unrecoverable until reset — the $6M fuel hit is a margin event, not a pass-through. Meanwhile competitively-bid work has grown from 17.6% of revenue in FY23 to 25.0% now, which is where fixed-price estimating risk lives.

The seasonality defense — tested against four years of quarters, and refuted

The bull case says H2 margins of ~9.0% vs H1's 6.3% is "normal seasonality." The quarterly record says otherwise: Q1 is the trough; Q2 through Q4 run in a tight band with no second-half step-up. Q2-26's 7.9% is a 100bps miss inside the normal band — and the guide needs the decline to stop and fully reverse in one quarter.

Two honesty notes on the history itself: FY24's H1-to-H2 spread is inflated by $91.1M of second-half storm revenue at ~36% margin, and FY23's H2 (9.83%) was actually below its own Q2 (11.17%) — there is no H2 magic anywhere in the record. The floor breaks at any H2 margin below ~8.55%. Prior-year H2 comparators were 9.10% and 9.31%; the most recent quarter printed 7.90%.

06

The AI-cycle check: the entry does not need the AI boom — and the "cheap vs peers" argument secretly does

House discipline: every thesis states its sensitivity to the AI investment cycle. Centuri's direct datacenter exposure is small and new — a $125M award in Q2's bookings, about 2% of backlog. Since 59.4% of revenue is mandate-driven gas work, a break in the AI cycle would mostly remove the bull case rather than create new downside. The entry does not require the cycle holding.

The ruling: every expensive name in this cohort is an electric/power/datacenter story; the only two cheap ones are the two without it. CTRI's discount to the median is the AI premium it does not carry. Buying it for "convergence to 18.3x" is a leveraged bet on the AI capex cycle holding — the opposite of what its gas-heavy mix makes it look like. Convergence is rejected; no scenario below assumes it. Our base multiple is 11.0x — above Primoris's deteriorating-name floor (CTRI grows 33%; Primoris shrinks), far below the AI cohort. The residual discount is earned: second-highest leverage in the set (~3.0x on the peer convention), second-lowest margin, margins compressing while the cohort's expand.

CTRI's ~12.9x on the peer convention is derived from filings, not company-reported — treat as approximate. Portfolio note: the household already carries substantial AI-complex exposure, so any bull-case payoff here is a concentration add, not a diversifier.

08

The Icahn position: real anchor, honest counterweight — optionality, not thesis

Carl Icahn's vehicles hold 14,336,044 shares — 14.4% — with a board designee seated since November 2025 and a verified average cost of $19.74. Our entire pre-earnings ladder sits at or below his basis.

  • What's verified: $19.74 average across the 11,849,549 shares with disclosed purchase prices. (The first 2,486,495 shares were bought below the 5% disclosure threshold with no price anywhere in the record — his true blended basis is unverifiable, but on the priced tranche the number is exact.)
  • The governance asset: the cooperation agreement gives his designee a seat and a committee-bypass right — any board consideration of CEO/CFO appointment or employment, mergers, or material asset transactions must include him. His filing calls the stock "undervalued" with "no plans or proposals" — friendly, no strategic-review demand, no amendments filed to date.
  • The honest counterweight: 11,465,749 of his shares (11.4% of the company) are registered for resale and fully unlocked — no lock-up, sellable at will. The board seat survives down to 5,423,836 shares, so he can sell 8.9M shares and keep the seat. The former parent's overhang is gone (slide 12); this one replaced it.
  • The story that wasn't: "Icahn added $78M in March 2026" made headlines — but the share count is unchanged at 14,336,044 in both the March proxy and the May registration statement. The headline was a mark-to-market artifact of quarterly holdings reports, not a purchase.
Why this shapes the ladder rather than the thesis: buying above an activist's basis in a name where he holds 11.5M unlocked, registered shares is paying for the wrong side of his option. At $19.75 and below, his position is support; at $21.88 it is supply risk. A filed reduction below ~10M shares kills the whole plan (slide 11).

The plan: two branches, deliberately alternatives — never an AND-gate

Price and evidence are negatively correlated here: if the November quarter proves the margins, the stock will not be at $19.75; if the stock reaches $19.75 first, the evidence hasn't arrived yet. A plan demanding both a cheap price and confirmation would be an unfillable "no" dressed up as a plan. So the full position — 0.75% of deployable capital, ~$25k (cut from the normal 1.2% cap for the contract-economics finding) — is split across two routes that each pay for what they get.

Branch B — post-evidence add: pay up for proof (~$12.5k plus whatever A left unfilled)

If the Q3 evidence fails (base margin below 8.5% or a guidance cut): both branches close, and any Branch A fills go back under review for exit. The structural reading wins, and $17 is fair — not cheap.
