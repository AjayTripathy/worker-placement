# PITCH — DOCS — VERDICT: PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION

**No entry today. Both benches land on no-position; the disagreement is about why, and both of them got the stock price wrong.**

---

## What we believe

Doximity is a profitable, debt-free advertising and hiring platform for doctors. It has $687.8M of cash and securities, no borrowings, and generated $39.6M of free cash in the June quarter. It is not in any danger.

The problem is what you pay for it and what you get. Revenue growth has slowed to roughly +7% year over year ($156.6M in the June quarter against $145.9M a year earlier), and the company guided the September quarter to $170–171M, which is only about +1% against last year. At the same time, the company now pays its employees about $147M a year in stock — roughly 22% of all revenue — and that cost is real even though it doesn't show up in the profit number the company highlights.

If you treat stock pay as the expense it is, you are paying about **25 times** the company's true annual operating profit for a business growing 7%. That is expensive.

The thing that makes expensive dangerous here: Doximity has two classes of stock, and the founders' shares carry extra votes. That means no outside investor can force change, and no acquirer can buy the company without the founders' consent. For most richly-priced, asset-light businesses, "someone could just buy it" is the floor under the price. Here there is no such floor.

So: a good business, priced for a better one, with the usual safety net cut away. We pass, and we say exactly what would change our minds.

## What the market believes

The market does not have one view — it has two incompatible ones, which is unusual and is the only reason this is still an open file.

- The stock is **$25.89** (broker feed, 2026-08-21). It is **down 66% from its 52-week high of $76.50** and **up 51% from its 52-week low of $17.16**. Something already broke; the argument is over whether it's fixed.
- On revenue, everyone agrees. Wall Street's average forecast for this fiscal year is about **$670.5M**; Truist just raised its number to **$673M**; the company itself guided **$676M**. Our own work landed at $676M. There is no revenue disagreement to trade.
- On what it's worth, the disagreement is enormous. **Wells Fargo downgraded the stock and put a $18 target on it. Needham raised its target to $41.** That is a 2.3-to-1 spread at a $25.89 stock. Translated into the measure we care about, the street band is **16 times to 42 times** post-stock-pay operating profit — the analysts are not arguing about the business, they are arguing about the multiple.

In plain terms: the market believes the revenue story is settled and the valuation story is not.

## Why we might have edge

Three narrow places, and we should not overstate them:

1. **We expense stock pay; most published models don't.** The company's own headline profit figure ($319M for the year) ignores $147M of stock compensation. Nobody has to agree with our adjustment, but it is the difference between "13 times profit" (cheap-ish) and "25 times profit" (expensive). Since the street's fight is entirely about the multiple, the adjustment sits exactly on the contested variable.
2. **The vote structure appears in nobody's target price.** Neither our original brief nor, as far as we can tell from the published research, the street's, prices the absence of a takeover floor. For a business at 6.3 times sales, that is the whole downside case.
3. **We have a cleaner forward alarm than a target price.** From the company's own annual guidance we can back out what the December quarter must deliver, and check it against a single line in the November press release. That is a date-stamped, falsifiable test, not an opinion.

## Why it might be priced in

Stated honestly, this is the stronger side of the ledger right now:

- **The bear case is already published.** Wells Fargo's $18 is roughly our own "this is what it's worth if you expense the stock pay" number. We are not early to a view; we are agreeing with a downgrade that already printed at a $25.89 tape.
- **Two of our three original complaints don't survive checking.** We said growth was structurally breaking; the weak September quarter is lapping the strongest quarter in the company's history, so the two-year comparison is flat, not collapsing. We said stock pay was ramping; it went $21.9M → $29.5M → $33.5M → $36.7M → $36.8M per quarter — the ramp ended a quarter ago. What's left is "the level is high," which is a static fact the market has had four quarters to absorb.
- **Margins are guided flat, not falling.** The September guide implies a 47.5% margin, the full year 47.2%, the back half 46.8%. The step down from 54.7% already happened. "Damage arriving" was wrong; "damage arrived and stayed" is right, and arrived damage is usually priced.
- **The share count is shrinking, not growing.** Diluted shares went from 201.2M to 191.2M, down 5.0%. Buybacks are more than absorbing the stock pay. The dilution complaint, as an ownership matter, is backwards.
