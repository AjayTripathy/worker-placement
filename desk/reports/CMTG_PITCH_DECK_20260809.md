# PITCH — CMTG — PROPOSED VERDICT (one line; label clearly "PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION")

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION: NO POSITION. Do not buy Claros Mortgage Trust at $1.94. Both benches independently landed on staying out — the discount is real but there is no mechanism that forces it to close. Re-examine at the Q3 print (~2026-11-04, date unconfirmed) behind the four evidence gates listed under Entry Bands.**

## What we believe

Claros Mortgage Trust is a commercial real estate lender trading at $1.94 against a stated book value of $8.58 per share — about 22.6 cents on the dollar. Our courts found that a genuine gap probably exists between what the market is pricing and what the company has reserved: after honestly netting out the costs of just existing (management fees, operating losses, dead time), the price still implies lifetime losses of roughly 32–36% on the loan book, versus the 16.9% the company has actually reserved. That is a real, testable disagreement.

But we also believe the gap has no engine to close it. The company is run by an outside manager who collected ~$36M in 2025 (about 3% of book value per year) on a fee base that is contractually defined and does not shrink with the stock price — which pays the manager to extend and wait, not to liquidate and return cash. A corporate term loan taken in January 2026 has first claim on resolution proceeds, so cash from loan payoffs goes to the lender before it can fund anything shareholder-friendly like a buyback. There is no dividend and no new lending. Cheap with no catalyst and a misaligned steward is a value trap until proven otherwise, so we propose standing flat.

## What the market believes

The market has cut the stock 51% from its 52-week high of $3.99 and holds it 17% above the low of $1.655, ten days after the Q2 earnings release. This is not a neglected or undiscovered stock — it is a covered, institutionally-owned name whose price was set by people who read the same release we did. The market's implicit view: losses on the loan book will keep growing (the company added $2.08 per share to loss reserves in just the first half of 2026, and book value fell from $10.69 to $8.58), the company will keep losing a little money each quarter while paying its external manager, and nothing forces the discount to book value to ever close. Two consecutive quarters of fresh reserve additions and four new loans downgraded to the company's worst internal rating in Q2 support reading management's loss estimates as chronically behind events, not ahead of them.

## Why we might have edge

Three findings survived both benches:

1. **The bearish "reserves keep rising" metric is built wrong.** Reserves as a percentage of the loan book rise mechanically even when nothing new goes bad, because healthy loans pay off at full value and exit the denominator, concentrating the troubled ones in what remains. July's resolutions actually recovered 88.8 cents on the dollar blended ($223M at full value, $75M at 94%, $112M at 63%). The correct scoreboard is realized losses on resolved loans versus what was reserved for them — and on that scoreboard the company has so far done better than the market's implied number.
2. **The true operating bleed is small.** The single most bull-favorable number in the print, missed by the original brief and by our own red bench: excluding realized credit losses, the company's distributable loss was only about $0.07 per share for the quarter — near breakeven — not the $0.36 the red bench initially claimed (see bench errors below).
3. **The market may be extrapolating reserve growth that is partly a one-time catch-up.** Of the $2.08/share first-half reserve build, $0.89 was a general portfolio overlay, not new named problem loans.

If Q3 shows resolutions still recovering well, near-breakeven operations, and the problem-loan bucket shrinking, the ~32–36% implied loss rate is too pessimistic and the stock is worth materially more than $1.94.

## Why it might be priced in

The bear case is strong and partly confirmed by our own checks. The "watchlist improving" story is substantially migration, not healing: the watchlist fell $477M in a quarter when roughly $372–447M of loans (the two company figures disagree — unresolved) were downgraded *out* of it into the worst rating bucket, and our reserve arithmetic implies that worst bucket holds ~$1.5–1.6B of loans — more than the entire $1.2B "watchlist." The company's $168M of liquidity is quoted before $161M of contractual funding obligations on already-troubled loans (per the [Q2 2026 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001666291/000119312526323733/cmtg-20260630.htm), though we could not independently re-open that filing this session). The fee drag, term-loan waterfall, absence of a dividend, and absence of new lending mean even a correct bullish view on credit could take years to pay, bleeding ~3% of book annually to the manager while it waits. And because the price was set post-earnings by informed holders, "cheap versus book" is the market's considered answer, not an oversight.
