## What we believe

Royal Hotel is a Japanese hotel operator that appears to be worth less than the cash sitting inside it. It has essentially no debt (debt ratio 0.01, verified). It holds roughly ¥11.66 billion of cash against a market value of somewhere between ¥13.8bn and ¥18.7bn — which means between 62% and 84% of what you pay for the whole company comes back to you as cash on the balance sheet. The hotels themselves are being handed to you for between ¥2.1bn and ¥7.0bn against roughly ¥2.5bn of trailing operating profit.

That is the entire case. It is a balance-sheet case, not a business case. The hotel operation earns a 3.5% operating margin, loses money in two quarters out of four, and is being asked to compete with Marriott, Four Seasons and Hyatt in Osaka.

We believe it *may* be cheap. We do not currently believe it enough to own it, because the two numbers that decide whether it is 62% cash or 84% cash — and whether the ¥11.66bn is free or already spoken for — are both unresolved.

## What the market believes

The market believes the cash is not really yours. That is the standard Japanese small-cap discount, and here it has a specific, named form: the company has a ¥13.5 billion renovation programme in front of it. ¥13.5bn is more than ¥11.66bn. If the company pays for that renovation out of its own cash, the "net cash" is not net cash — it is a construction budget that happens to be sitting in a bank account, and the stock is roughly fairly priced.

The market also does not believe the profits are durable. Trailing operating profit was earned during a peak in inbound tourism that included the Osaka World Expo. The Expo closed on 2025-10-13. With a 3.5% margin and a fixed cost base, a modest fall in occupancy or a modest rise in wages takes operating profit to zero.

And the market is paid almost nothing to wait: the dividend is about ¥6 per share, a yield of roughly 0.67%.

## Why we might have edge

One reason, and it is genuinely a good one if it holds: press reporting from 2024 around a sale of the Rihga Royal Osaka suggests the renovation may be funded by an outside partner — BGO, in an asset-light structure alongside an IHG Vignette affiliation. If a third party funds the ¥13.5bn, then the ¥11.66bn of cash is unencumbered, the company becomes a fee-earning operator instead of a capital-consuming owner, and the discount the market is applying is being applied to the wrong risk.

That is a fact about a specific transaction, disclosed in Japanese, in documents most non-Japanese investors will not read. It is exactly the kind of thing that stays mispriced.

The second possible edge is arithmetic, not insight: the share count is genuinely ambiguous in the data we have, and if the low count is right the stock is at 0.58x book with 84% of its value in cash, which is a materially better security than the 0.78x / 62% version.

## Why it might be priced in

The BGO funding claim is **unverified**. Not disputed — unverified. No primary document in our record says who writes the cheque for the ¥13.5bn. Everything above rests on that sentence.

Beyond that, the bear case is mostly confirmed:

- The Expo demand surge is over, and it was over ten months before our own court sat.
- Two of the last four quarters were operating **losses** (Jul–Sep −¥175m, Jan–Mar −¥706m). This is a violently seasonal business.
- A 3.5% margin gives no protection against Japanese hospitality wage inflation, which is currently the most reliable cost trend in the country.
- Governance is entrenched and shareholder returns are token. A company that will not pay out cash is a company whose cash may never become yours, no matter what the balance sheet says.
