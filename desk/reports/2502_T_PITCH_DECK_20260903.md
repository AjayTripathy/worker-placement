## What we believe

The idea we came in with was simple: Japanese drinkers are switching from beer to zero-alcohol beer, that switch is early and under-observed by Western investors, and Asahi — the biggest Japanese brewer, with the leading zero-alcohol beer brand (Dry Zero) — is the listed way to own it.

After the court, here is what we actually believe:

1. **The consumer observation is probably real. The investment vehicle is wrong.** The product that prompted this is made by **Suntory Holdings, which is private.** There is no listed way to own that specific business. Asahi was our substitute, and it does not collect the economics we were trying to buy.
2. **The switch, where we can measure it, is small and slow.** Asahi publishes a monthly volume series. In May 2026 the Dry Zero line printed **105% of the prior year** — i.e. +5%. The broader "beer-adjacent" category, which contains it, ran **+1% in the first half**. Both sit on a base worth **under 2% of a ¥2.4tn company**. Even a doubling does not move the earnings.
3. **The company itself is voting the other way.** Its own results release names **Super Dry (+31%)** and **Peroni (+7%)** — full-strength beer — as the growth drivers. Non-alcoholic is not named as a driver anywhere. And its marginal capital, **US$2.3bn for 65% of East African Breweries plus a spirits stake**, went into full-strength alcohol in emerging markets at **~17x earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation**, pushing borrowings to roughly **3.6x earnings** against a stated 2.5–3.0x policy.
4. **The reported growth is currency, not business.** First-half 2026: revenue **+7.7% as reported but −0.6% at constant exchange rates**; core operating profit **+1.5% reported but −8.5% at constant rates**, against a clean pre-cyberattack comparison. For a US-dollar investor that is a flat-to-down business wearing a weak yen as a costume.
5. **Asahi is not, however, a bad company or a short.** It trades at 15.0x trailing and ~12.7x this year's forecast earnings, yields ~3.75%, and sits mid-range in its 52-week band. We have one novel objection to *our thesis*; we have no argued case against *the stock*.

**So: flat, with two specific conditions under which we come back — one of which is a completely different thesis that would owe its own court.**

## What could go wrong

*(Pre-mortem. We hold no position, so this covers two distinct failures: being wrong about the conditions under which we would come back, and being wrong to stay out.)*

### Failure narrative 1 — The pass-through trap (monitorable)

We come back in November because the October volume data shows Japanese beer running +8% or better after the duty cut, and we call it premiumisation. In fact Asahi hands the entire ¥9.10 duty saving to the shelf to buy share from Kirin and Suntory. Volume is up, revenue per litre is down, and gross profit is flat or worse. We bought a price war and called it a mix shift. The stock re-rates on the volume headline in November and de-rates on the margin in the February full-year numbers.

*Tripwire:* revenue-per-litre and value-per-litre in the October, November and December monthly disclosures, read alongside volume — **volume alone is not a signal**. Confirm at the Q3 print. Kill the re-entry if unit revenue falls more than ~4% (the size of the duty cut at shelf).

### Failure narrative 2 — East African Breweries (monitorable)

Completion has already slipped from "second half 2026" toward December, with multiple Kenyan suits escalated to the Chief Justice as of July 2026. Three ways this hurts: the deal breaks and the growth stub disappears from the 2026 bridge; or it closes and borrowings sit at ~3.6x against a 2.5–3.0x policy into a rating review; or it closes and the Kenyan shilling, Ugandan shilling and Tanzanian shilling translate the earnings away — currencies nobody can hedge at reasonable cost. The 35% of East African Breweries that stays in outside hands (Kenya granted a mandatory-offer exemption, so the minorities remain) means we consolidate the revenue and share the profit.

*Tripwire:* the Kenyan court docket, the completion press release, and net-debt-to-earnings disclosed at the full-year results; plus the purchase-accounting note when it publishes.

### Failure narrative 3 — The one we cannot watch (NO TRIPWIRE)

**Asahi never discloses gross profit per litre by product, and never discloses how much of any duty change it retains versus passes through.** Neither figure exists at any reporting frequency, in any venue, ever. If the non-alcoholic line is structurally lower-margin than beer — plausible, since it carries no excise but also commands a lower shelf price and runs on shorter production batches — then the growth we were originally hunting was *value-destructive growth*, and we would never have found out. Worse: if we re-enter on a mix thesis and the mix arithmetic runs against us, the evidence would first surface in a full-year margin bridge twelve-plus months later, blended together with currency moves and the African acquisition, and would be **unattributable** — we would not be able to tell we were wrong, only that we lost money.

**Unknown-unknown class: no native disclosure plane for the driver entity.** The thing that actually determines whether we make money (product-level unit economics) is not reported at any level; the finest reporting grain available is the segment. This class has a sibling here that makes it worse: **the private-competitor blind spot** — the most important competitor in this specific category, Suntory Holdings, is not listed and discloses nothing, so we cannot even triangulate from the other side of the market.

This is the failure we could not build a sensor for, and we should say so out loud rather than pretend the monthly volume series covers it. It does not. It covers volume; it does not cover profit per unit.

### The reflexive and structural leg

- **Our own size is irrelevant to the market and that is a fact, not a comfort.** A 1% starter on ~$3.4m of deployable capital is ~$34k ≈ ¥5.1m, against ¥11.65bn of daily volume — **0.04% of a day**. We cannot move this stock, we will never be trapped in it, and our published-ladder limit of 1% of daily volume is not remotely binding. The reflexive risk here is therefore **entirely internal**: nothing external stops us from sizing on conviction alone.
- **We must not bid into flow we ourselves predict.** If our own mix thesis is right, the re-rating we expect lands the stock around ¥1,750–1,850. Any reopen level we set must sit **below** that, or we are buying our own forecast at our own forecast price. The reopen band below does.
- **Two of our own rules pull in opposite directions here, and one wins on a technicality worth stating.** Our rule for quality names at fair value says a fair price justifies a small starter rather than waiting. Our court rule says staying flat requires a *named* kill, not a shrug. We have a named kill — the non-alcoholic line is immaterial — so flat is **licensed, not defaulted into**. But note precisely what that kill covers: it kills *this thesis*. It is not a judgment that Asahi is a bad holding, and it must not be carried into a future court on different grounds.
- **The currency leg would be an undeclared second position.** Buying this in dollars is a long-yen bet stacked on the equity. Funding it in yen — which the original brief proposed — is the reverse: an outright **short-yen position sized to the whole equity stake**, taken without ever writing it down as a position. Either way it must be declared and sized on its own merits, not smuggled in as "funding."
- **Tax interaction:** 2026 is a loss-harvesting year against a large realised gain. Opening a new long in September whose loss branch only resolves in 2027 puts the loss in the wrong tax year. This does not create an edge for staying flat, but it removes one of the usual arguments for a starter position.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we re-entered on the October mix read, and the duty cut turned out to be passed straight through to the shelf — volumes up, profit per litre quietly down — so what we bought as premiumisation was a price war we had no way to see until the margin bridge arrived a year later, blended with currency and Africa.**
