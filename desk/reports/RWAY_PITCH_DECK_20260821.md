# PITCH — RWAY — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — small starter, but only on a limit at **$6.25 or better** (stock is $6.60); no buying at today's price, no averaging up.

## What we believe

Runway Growth Finance is a lender to venture-backed private companies. It borrows money, lends it out at floating rates, and pays out nearly all the interest it collects as a dividend. In April 2026 it bought SWK Holdings for $249M, adding about $240M of loans (mostly healthcare) and about 6.4M new shares.

Three things we think are true:

1. **The stock is cheap on cash earnings even after we strip out the flattering accounting.** Management markets $0.43 of quarterly net investment income and 1.30x dividend coverage. That number is not real (see below) — the honest figure is about **$0.34 per share per quarter, or $1.36 a year**. On a $6.60 share price that is still roughly a **20% earnings yield**, against a $1.32 annual dividend. Cheap, even after the haircut.

2. **The two loans that went bad were recognized, not hidden.** Net asset value is **$11.91/share** and was roughly flat once you back out the one-time deal costs. The two non-accruals were moved onto non-accrual at the end of Q1'26 — that is damage taken, not damage being deferred. Our own bear case tried to prove the remaining book is quietly deteriorating and its arithmetic did not survive checking.

3. **The stock is cheap for identifiable, dated reasons that decay.** A holder who has been selling (Carlson/Double Black Diamond, still ~3.885M shares, ~9.2% of the company) has been printing stock at $6.59–6.64. Roughly 6.4M shares were handed to SWK's owners at an $11.93 valuation and are down ~45%. That is a lot of motivated supply sitting right where we would bid. Supply exhausts; it is a reason to bid lower, not a reason to pass.

The trade is: buy a 20% cash-earnings yield at roughly **55 cents on the dollar of stated book value**, in small size, below the level where the known sellers have been hitting bids, and let the quarterly reports either confirm or destroy the thesis.

## What the market believes

The market believes the marks are wrong and the leverage makes that fatal.

- The portfolio is $1.19B of loans to private venture-backed companies. Nobody outside the company can verify what they are worth. They are valued by the company's own board using models, not market prices.
- Against that sits **$679M of debt** and **$503M of equity** — borrowings of about **1.36x equity**, above the top of management's own stated range.
- Because of that leverage, a 1% error in the loan marks moves book value **2.36%**. For the stock price to equal stated book value, the portfolio would have to be marked down **18.8%**. And a **13.7%** markdown would push the company through its **150% asset-coverage floor** — the legal test that governs whether a lender like this can borrow more or keep paying dividends.
- So the market's position is simple: the entire discount sits *past* the point where the balance sheet gets into trouble. The discount is not a bargain, it is a probability.

Underneath that, the market also knows the headline dividend yield near 20% has been supported by an accounting quirk (below), and that a shrinking legacy loan book is earning less interest than a year ago.

## Why we might have edge

**1. We priced the accounting quirk from the contract, not from last year's dollar figure.** The adviser gets a performance fee only after clearing a total-return hurdle. $45.3M of realized loan losses impaired that hurdle, so the fee went to **negative $199k this quarter versus positive $3,523k a year ago** — a $3.7M swing that flows straight into "earnings." We rebuilt the fee from the fee formula in the N-2 prospectus (2% quarterly hurdle, catch-up to 2.667%, 20% above that): pre-fee income of $17,992k is 3.58% of net assets, well past full catch-up, so the formula fee is **$3,598k**. Normalized income is **$0.341/share, covering the $0.33 dividend 1.03x — not 1.30x**. We validated the formula against the prior-year quarter (predicted $3,494k vs actual $3,523k). Most write-ups quote the 1.30x.

That is a *valuation* conclusion, not a *disqualifying* one: it tells us what to pay, not whether to own.

**2. We caught our own bear case making an arithmetic error that inverted a conclusion.** Our bear analyst argued that the reported improvement in average loan risk rating (2.67 → 2.34) must mean the old loans got worse, and computed an implied legacy rating of 3.01–3.26. Redoing the weighted average with the disclosed weight — SWK loans are **$216.2M of an ~$1.1B debt book, about 20%** — gives implied legacy of **2.42 to 2.67**, i.e. flat to better. Getting to 3.13 would require SWK to be ~70% of the book. It is 20%. This is the difference between "the remaining book is rotting" and "it isn't," and it turned on one weight.

**3. We can price the buyback both ways.** The bear framing is that buying stock and cutting leverage are mutually exclusive. Both are true at once and the trade-off is measurable: spending the authorized $15M at ~$6.70 retires 2.24M shares, lifts book value per share to **$12.20 (+2.4%)**, and costs about 2 points of asset coverage (174.0% → 171.8%). That is a priced trade-off, not a contradiction.

**4. The unverifiable thing resolves on a clock.** We cannot check borrower-level loan quality with any data source we own — we checked, and we own nothing that reaches private venture-credit obligors. But the marks get restated every 90 days in a filed document. "Cannot verify today" is a reason to size small and wait, not automatically a reason to skip.

## Why it might be priced in

Honestly, most of it probably is.

- **Leverage at 1.36x and the discount to book are in every published write-up.** There is no edge in noticing them.
- **The revenue line is not a source of edge.** Street consensus for FY26 revenue is $139.1M; our own build is $140.5M. For a lender, revenue is contractual coupon — matching it means nothing.
- **The sellers are visible to everyone.** A 9.2% holder distributing stock and a 15%-of-shares block sitting 45% underwater are public facts. Everyone can see the overhang; that is partly why it's $6.60.
- **The bear case on coverage may already be half-known.** Analysts who read the income statement can see the negative incentive fee line. Our edge is the formula reconstruction, which is a refinement, not a secret.
- **Our best argument is a valuation argument in a name whose central variable we admit we cannot audit.** A 20% earnings yield on marks we cannot verify is not obviously mispriced — it may be correctly priced for the mark risk.

## PROPOSED ENTRY BANDS

**Entry — limit $6.25 or better. Do not bid at $6.60.**
- Basis: the known seller printed stock at **$6.59–6.64**. Bidding at $6.60 means buying from a demonstrated supplier at his price. $6.25 is below his demonstrated zone.
- At $6.25, normalized earnings of $1.36/yr is a **21.8% earnings yield** and the price is **52% of the $11.91 book value**.

**Size — 0.35% to 0.50% of the book.** Half a normal starter. The reason for the halving is explicit: a 1% error in loan marks moves book value 2.36%, and we have no way to audit those marks. Fill in one clip; do not scale in above $6.25.

**No averaging up.** If it runs from here without new information, we missed it. That is acceptable.

**Adds — only after evidence, and only at or below the entry level.** Add to a full position (deck proposal: **1.0% of book**) if *any one* of these appears in a filed document:
- Q3 10-Q discloses the risk-rating split between the legacy book and the acquired SWK book, with the legacy average **≤ 2.67**; or
- Quarterly net investment income **≥ $0.35/share with a positive incentive fee actually accrued** (coverage that survives the fee coming back); or
- Borrowings back to **≤ 1.15x equity** without shrinking the earning-asset base.

**Kill — exit in full, no debate, if either occurs:**
- Q3 book value prints **below $11.91** excluding one-time items (the "damage was taken, not deferred" claim is then false); or
