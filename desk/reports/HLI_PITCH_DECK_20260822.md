# PITCH — HLI — VERDICT: no position today at $128.06; small starter authorized only on a limit at $113 or lower, second buy withheld until the 10/29 earnings report — **PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION**

## What we believe

Houlihan Lokey is a good business having a bad year, and the stock has already fallen most of the way to a price where that is properly paid for — but not all of it.

The specifics. The June quarter (their fiscal Q1 2027) brought in about $511M of fees, down 15.5% from a year earlier and roughly 20% below what analysts expected ($636M). The headline said profit per share fell 37%. That headline is misleading in the company's favour *and* against it: on plain accounting rules profit per share fell 19%, not 37%. The extra decline is an accounting add-back rolling off — shares and cash promised to the sellers of two firms Houlihan bought (Waller Helms, 7 Mile Advisors) that finally vested in January 2026, so the prior year's "adjusted" number was flattered, not this year's crushed. Pay as a share of revenue was flat-to-better year over year (61.5% both years on the adjusted basis; 64.3% vs 64.9% on the reported basis) on 15.5% less revenue, which is not what a business losing its franchise looks like.

Two things we think the market has half-marked:

1. **The year is probably worse than the sell side says.** June is Houlihan's weakest quarter every year — it was 21.5%, 21.2% and 23.1% of the full fiscal year in the last three years. Grossing $511M up by that range gives roughly **$2.21–2.41B of revenue this fiscal year**. Analysts, marked on 2026-08-05, carry **$2.63B**. For $2.63B to be right, June would have to be only 19.4% of the year — lower than any of the last three years. Their earnings number is $6.99 per share; ours, using the profit margin the company actually printed this quarter (17.9%), is about **$6.00**, and about $6.30–6.50 if margin recovers to last year's 19.8%.

2. **Even on the sell side's own optimistic numbers, the stock is not cheap against its closest comparable.** At $128.06 it is 18.3x this year's expected earnings while those earnings are forecast to *fall* 7.5%. Evercore is 14.9x on earnings forecast to *rise* 34%. Houlihan carries a ~23% premium for the shrinking one.

So: quality is intact, the price is not yet compelling, and there is a dated test — the next earnings report — that resolves the one thing we genuinely cannot resolve from filings.

## What the market believes

The market believes this is a cyclical trough in deal-making at a franchise that has historically been the most defensive one in the sector, and that you buy it into the trough.

Concretely: 11 analysts marked after the miss carry $2.63B of revenue, $6.99 of earnings, and an average price target of **$153.50** — about 20% above today's price. The stock is 38.9% below its 52-week high of $209.65 but has already bounced 13.5% off its 52-week low of $112.83, so a third of the drawdown has been retraced by people acting on exactly this view. The most direct evidence of who is winning that argument: on 2026-08-13, the day Houlihan actually filed the prospectus letting acquisition sellers resell their shares, the stock rose 7.12% to $132.21. Buyers absorbed a supply event without flinching.

The market also believes Houlihan's restructuring arm is a hedge — when mergers dry up, distressed work picks up. That is the historical pattern and it is why the stock trades at a premium to Evercore in the first place.

## Why we might have edge

- **We read the reported numbers, not the press release.** The "-37% earnings" figure that anchors the bear case comes from the company's own adjusted presentation; the audited filing says -19%, and the gap is a vesting earnout, not deterioration. Anyone screening on adjusted earnings decline is measuring the wrong thing — in both directions, because last year's base was inflated by the same item. This is a repeatable pattern we can look for in other serial acquirers.
- **We did the seasonal arithmetic.** Both the original note (which multiplied June by four) and the sell side (which implicitly assumes June was only 19.4% of the year) are outside the three-year historical range. The honest range is $2.21–2.41B.
- **We compared like for like.** The original note compared Houlihan's *forward* multiple to Evercore's *trailing* one, which manufactured a fake 24x-vs-16.5x gap. When both are put on marked forward numbers the gap is smaller but real and pointing the same way: 18.3x vs 14.9x.
- **We checked the balance sheet against what is owed.** Cash and investments of $797M looks like 8.9% of the company's market value. But $589M of that is accrued salaries and bonuses that get paid out. Genuinely unencumbered cash is closer to $208M, or **2.3%** of market value. There is no debt drawn on the credit line. The balance sheet is clean but it is not a cushion.

## Why it might be priced in

Symmetrically, here is why we may have no edge at all:

- **Fiscal-Q1 seasonality is not a secret.** Every sell-side model knows June is the weak quarter. Our "they're too high" claim reduces to a disagreement about the *recovery slope* in the back three quarters, which is a forecast, not a discovered fact.
- **The 8% earnings gap is small.** Street $6.99 vs our $6.00–6.50 is an ~8% difference. That is inside normal forecast error, not a mispricing.
- **Supply has already been tested and absorbed.** The +7.12% print on the day the resale registration filed is direct evidence that the overhang story is not operative.
- **The premium may be correct.** If restructuring genuinely picks up as mergers stay slow, the earnings we are extrapolating down are the trough, and paying 18.3x a trough versus 14.9x a peak is the right trade, not the wrong one.
- **We have no read on positioning.** We could not pull short interest or any crowding measure. If the stock is already heavily shorted, the "everyone still believes" premise is wrong.
