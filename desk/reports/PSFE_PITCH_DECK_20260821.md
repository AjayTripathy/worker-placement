# PITCH — PSFE — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — no position, no order, no standing price gate; the name reopens only on named Q3 evidence, and our own two benches each got material numbers wrong.

---

## What we believe

Paysafe is a payments company (the Skrill, NETELLER and paysafecard wallets, plus a merchant-processing arm) whose stock has fallen 54% from its 52-week high to $6.87. What makes it interesting is not the fall — it is the shape of the balance sheet. The company carries about $2.27 billion of net debt against roughly $359 million of stock market value. That is about 6.4 dollars of debt for every dollar of equity. In that structure the stock is a thin slice at the top: small changes in the business, or in the debt, move the stock several times as much.

The original case for owning it was simple. The company throws off real cash. If it pays that cash to lenders, the debt shrinks, and everything that comes off the debt lands on the thin equity slice. Management has publicly framed it that way — roughly $200 million of debt reduction is $3–4 per share on a $6.87 stock.

Having run this through both benches, here is what we actually believe now:

1. **The cash is real and it did reach shareholders in the first half.** Total debt fell from $2,605M to $2,499M, net debt fell about $82 million in six months (roughly 23% of the entire market value of the equity), and the company also bought back $13.0 million of its own stock. This is not a story where lenders take everything.
2. **The engine stalled in the most recent quarter.** In Q1 the company generated $42.3M of cash after capital spending. In Q2 it generated *negative* $6.1M (operating cash $25.3M against capital spending of $31.4M, which jumped 45% quarter-on-quarter). Leverage went the wrong way, from 5.2x to 5.3x.
3. **The wallet business is shrinking on the audited numbers.** Customer account balances — money customers hold with Paysafe — fell from $1,095.1M to $983.8M, down 10.2% in six months. That is the same segment whose profit margin fell 4.9 percentage points.
4. **The refinancing on 12 August 2026 bought time but cost money.** Maturities were pushed out to June 2030 (term loans) and August 2031 (revolver), clearing a 2028 wall. In exchange, interest went up, and the company cut its own earnings-per-share guidance to pay for it.

So: a genuinely cheap, genuinely cash-generative business, where the two most recent data points both point down, and where we have no view that differs from the market's on the number that decides the next six months. **We do not own it and we are not bidding for it.**

## What the market believes

The market is pricing roughly 3.5x this year's guided adjusted earnings per share and about 5.8x enterprise value to guided profit. That is a price that says one of two things: either the debt eventually eats the equity, or the wallet business is in structural decline and this year's profit is the high-water mark.

The market also believes management's guidance. Analysts anchor their full-year profit estimate to the company's own range of $449–464 million. So does our brief. There is no gap between our number and the street's number, because we never built our own number — more on that below, because it is our failure, not a fact about the stock.

Insiders have been selling into this. Rule 144 notices were filed on 26 June 2026 and 14 August 2026, and a Form 4 on 17 August 2026 shows sales at a $6.58 average price ([SEC Form 4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001833835/000119312526354375/ownership.xml)). On a $359 million company, programmatic insider selling is a meaningful share of daily volume.

## Why we might have edge

Three places where we could plausibly know something the average holder does not:

- **The quarterly split.** Most coverage reported the first-half cash flow as one number ($89.2M operating cash, $53.0M capex, $36.2M free cash). Split by quarter, the second quarter was cash-negative. Anyone reading the half-year aggregate would miss that the trend inverted. Our own red bench made exactly this mistake; the blue bench caught it.
- **The customer-balance line.** The 10.2% drop in customer account balances sits on the balance sheet, not in the press-release highlights. It is harder evidence about wallet health than any of the third-party proxies we ran.
- **The currency and hedging plumbing.** About €478.4 million (roughly $560M) of the $2.5 billion debt stack is euro-denominated. On a stub this thin, a 10% move in the euro is worth roughly $0.85–1.10 per share — 12 to 16% of the equity value — with no company news at all. Most holders of a $6.87 payments stock are not modelling that.

That is the honest list. None of it is a reason to buy today; all of it is a reason to be the fastest reader of the Q3 filing.

## Why it might be priced in

- **The cheapness is not a secret.** A 54% drawdown to 3.5x earnings is the market saying it has seen the leverage. "Cheap on a levered stub" is the most crowded trade in small-cap value.
- **We have no independent estimate.** The second-half profit bar our brief cited ($247–262M) is nothing more than the company's guidance minus the first half that already printed. It is arithmetic. Neither bench produced an independent forecast, so we cannot claim to see a gap the street doesn't.
- **The most recent quarter went the wrong way in public.** Negative free cash flow and rising leverage were both disclosed on 13 August. The stock has traded since. Whatever we "discovered" in the quarterly split has had eight trading days to be discovered by everyone else.
- **The insider selling is visible on EDGAR** and is a known overhang, not a hidden one.
- **The price gate problem.** With 6.4x leverage, the stock moves several percent on market-wide sentiment alone. A standing buy order at a lower price would fire on a bad day for the market, not on news about Paysafe. Both benches independently refused to set one, and we agree.

## PROPOSED ENTRY BANDS

**Both benches say no entry, and no price gate at any level.** Their reasoning, which we adopt: with 6.4x leverage the stock's day-to-day moves are dominated by the overall market, so a resting buy order below the market would be filled by a bad tape rather than by anything happening at Paysafe. Buying a levered stub because it got cheaper is buying beta and calling it research.

So what follows are **reopen conditions, not entry bands.**

**Reopen requires all three of these at the Q3 report (both benches converged on this list):**

## What remains unverified

- **Trading liquidity and short interest.** Not retrieved by either bench. This blocks sizing outright and is the main reason the blue bench held its own conviction at 7/10 rather than higher.
- **Any independent second-half estimate.** Neither bench built one. Everything we cite for the second half is the company's guidance minus the first half that already printed. We currently have zero analytical distance from the street on the number that decides the next six months.
- **Seasonality in customer account balances.** The 10.2% decline is audited fact; whether it reflects customers leaving or normal seasonal movement is not established. Two prior years of the same line would settle it and neither bench pulled them.
- **Currency of earnings.** How much of profit is earned in euros versus dollars, and therefore how much of the euro debt is naturally hedged. Red asserted the exposure is unhedged; blue showed the wallets earn in euros. Neither produced the split.
- **Whether the first-half cash flow is operating cash or settlement timing.** Paysafe holds customer money; movements in that float can flatter or depress operating cash without any economic change. The filings do not separate it clearly enough for us to tell.
- **Merchant concentration.** Would require reading the 20-F risk factors; not done.
- **The peer cohort definition.** Flagged as unread in the original brief and still unread after both benches. It is the reason the "de-rated less than peers" claim collapsed.
- **Whether the Q2 capital-spending jump (+45%) is a one-off or a new run rate.** This single line is the difference between the cash engine having stalled and having merely paused.
- **App-review evidence remains plausible-only** — under one review per day is too thin to carry weight, and paysafecard never resolved.

**Final honest summary:** our original brief reached the right conclusion (stay flat) through three wrong numbers — the share count, the guidance status, and the peer comparison. The red bench corrected those and then made two errors of its own in the same style: it claimed debt was flat when $82 million was actually repaid, and it annualized a single vesting-heavy quarter into a 7% dilution rate while the trailing year had retired 12% of the company. The blue bench caught both, and then found the thing neither of the others had: the cash engine went negative in Q2 and leverage rose. Three passes, and the case only became clear on the third. Court-worthiness stands at 5/10.
