## What we believe

Constellation Brands sells Modelo, Corona, Pacifico and Victoria in the United States, plus a much smaller wine and spirits business. The stock is $128.18, down 23% from its 52-week high and 2.7% above its 52-week low.

Our belief is narrow and unexciting, and we want to say so up front.

The thing the market is most afraid of — that the new CEO (four months in the job) will throw out this year's profit forecast and reset it lower — is less likely than it looks, and the reason is arithmetic. In the May quarter the company shipped 1.8% more beer to distributors than a year ago, but consumers pulled 0.3% *less* off the shelves. That gap means distributors are sitting on beer they have to work off. Normally you'd call that a borrowed quarter and expect a nasty surprise later. But the company's own full-year forecast — beer sales between −1% and +1% — already requires the rest of the year to run *negative* to land in the range. The destocking isn't hidden inside a rosy forecast. It is the forecast. A weak second quarter is the company hitting its number, not missing it.

So: the profit range of $11.20–$11.90 per share looks reachable, the business throws off $1.6–1.7 billion of free cash against a $21.9 billion market value (a 7.3–7.8% cash yield, after paying interest on the debt), and the dividend eats only 42% of that cash.

What we do **not** believe is that this is a bargain. Sales are guided to roughly flat. The two biggest brands are shrinking in volume — Modelo Especial down 2%, Corona Extra down more than 5%. At 11x earnings with no growth, this is a fairly-priced cash machine you can own, not a mispricing you can exploit. Our own scenario table (below) lands at $127.78 against a $128.18 quote — a 0.3% difference. We do not have an edge here. We have an absence of a fatal flaw, and those are not the same thing.

## What the market believes

Wall Street analysts model $11.84–$11.93 per share for this year — *above the top of the company's own range*. The market is paying 10.8x that number.

Decoded, the consensus view is: the destocking is a temporary air pocket, management is sandbagging the forecast, and by next year beer volumes normalize. The 23% decline from the high is the market pricing a slower-growing beer business, not a broken one.

The market also just made a loud, cheap statement. Berkshire Hathaway sold its entire stake — about 630,000 shares, roughly 0.37% of the company. On the day that became public (2026-08-17) the stock fell 6% and was the worst performer in the S&P 500. A trivial amount of stock, an enormous price reaction. Whatever the market thinks, it does not think this quietly.

## Why we might have edge

Three candidates, honestly graded:

**1. The guide-arithmetic point (weak-to-moderate edge).** If a soft second quarter is *confirmation* rather than a miss, then the most-feared event on the calendar is largely defused. We have not seen this drawn explicitly in published work. It is available to anyone with the earnings release and a calculator, which caps how much edge it can be — but the market's positioning (four consecutive new 52-week lows) suggests it is not being priced.

**2. A possible unpriced cost tailwind (unverified, genuinely open).** In April 2025 the U.S. government added beer and empty aluminum cans to the list of goods hit by aluminum tariffs ([Federal Register 2025-05884](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/04/2025-05884/implementation-of-duties-on-aluminum-derivatives-beer-and-empty-aluminum-cans-pursuant-to)). Secondary reporting suggests an April 2026 action reversed that for beer. Neither bench could confirm it — the relevant Federal Register document sits behind a bot wall — and the document's title reads "Strengthening Actions," which cuts the *other* way. If the reversal is real, it is cost relief inside a forecast that was set before it. If the title is what it says, it is a headwind. We do not know, and we are not pretending to.

**3. Reading the Berkshire event correctly (marginal).** A 0.37% stake did not mechanically push the stock down 6%. What moved it was the *interpretation* — a famous investor publicly grading the business. That is a sentiment event, now in the price, and it does not recur. This is worth something, but not much: it argues the last leg down was not fundamental, not that the stock is cheap.

## Why it might be priced in

Bluntly, most of it is.

Everything in our bear case — the destocking, the shrinking flagship brands, the debt, the Berkshire exit — is the published bear note. Everything in our bull case is arithmetic anyone can do from a public earnings release. The stock has set four new 52-week lows in twelve months ($149.42, $131.88, $126.45, and now $124.76). This is a heavily-examined, widely-owned large-cap that the market has been steadily marking down. It is not an overlooked security.

And our own scenario table settles at essentially the current price. When your independent estimate reproduces the quote, the honest conclusion is that the quote is right.

## What could go wrong

**Failure narrative 1 — The slow bleed (most likely).** No single bad event. Beer volumes simply keep drifting down 1–3% a year, the "destocking is temporary" story quietly becomes "demand is lower," and an 11x multiple on a shrinking business becomes a 9x multiple. We lose 15–20% over two years with no dramatic moment to react to. *Tripwire: monitorable* — quarterly depletions by brand, published every print.

**Failure narrative 2 — The consumer base itself contracts. NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE.** Constellation's beer business is disproportionately sold to Hispanic consumers in the U.S. If that consumer base withdraws from spending — from immigration enforcement, from a job-market shock concentrated in construction and services, from a change in where and how they shop — volumes fall for reasons that appear in no filing, no permit database, and no series we track. We would learn about it in a depletions number one quarter *after* it started, at which point the stock has already moved.

We have no sensor for this and we should not pretend we can build one from what we own. **Unknown-unknown class: demand-base composition risk** — a change in *who* the buyers are rather than *how many* units move. Our consumer-heat detector explicitly cannot see it: the court established that heat scores measure attention, not volume, and are non-diagnostic for this company (Victoria has the highest heat score and growing volume; Modelo has a high heat score and *shrinking* volume). We would be flying blind for one full quarter, minimum.

**Failure narrative 3 — The new CEO spends the balance sheet.** A four-month-old CEO at 2.9x debt-to-earnings, running a business with no organic growth, has an obvious temptation: buy growth. A large acquisition, or a leverage-funded defense of the dividend, re-rates the debt and crushes the equity. *Tripwire: monitorable* — 8-K filings, leverage disclosure in each 10-Q, any change in the stated ~3.0x target.

**The reflexive and structural leg.**

*Our size is irrelevant to the market and that is the point.* A 0.4–0.5% starter is a few hundred thousand dollars at most against roughly $300 million of daily trading volume. We are far under one-tenth of one percent of a day's volume. We cannot move this stock, we will not be trapped in it, and we are not bidding into our own predicted flow. (Our actual average-volume figure is unverified — the broker connection was denied this session — so that $300M is an estimate off the 2026-08-17 event, not a measured number.)

*The real reflexive risk is inside our own process.* Our doctrine says a court that produces no fatal objection defaults to a minimum starter. STZ produced no fatal objection. But it also produced no edge — our expected value is 0.3% *below* the current price. If we mechanically buy every name where the prosecution fails, we will assemble a book of thirty-two individually-defensible, collectively-indistinguishable-from-the-index positions, each one costing us attention and none of them earning it. STZ is the cleanest possible specimen of that failure mode: a good company, a fair price, a defensible write-up, and no reason to own it over the index. The minimum-starter default is doing the work here that conviction should be doing, and we should say that out loud rather than let a small position size disguise it.

*A second doctrine interaction the court missed entirely:* our Parametric separately-managed sleeve is a direct-index portfolio that holds individual S&P 500 names and harvests losses in them. It almost certainly holds a small amount of STZ. If Parametric sells STZ at a loss within thirty days of us buying it here, that harvested loss is disallowed under the wash-sale rule. The dollars are tiny — we estimate $5–6K of STZ inside the sleeve — but the rule does not care about size, and coordinating this is our job, not the manager's. This is listed as an open item below.

*Tax:* buying a new position in a year where we are deliberately harvesting losses is not itself a problem — there is no existing basis to protect. But if we buy in October and the stock falls, that loss is only harvestable in this tax year if we act before December 31, and we should not let "it might become tax-loss material" become a reason to buy. It is not one.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we bought a statistically cheap multiple on a business whose core consumer was quietly contracting, and mistook the absence of a fatal objection for the presence of an edge.**
