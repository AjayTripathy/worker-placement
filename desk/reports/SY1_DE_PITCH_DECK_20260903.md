# PITCH — SY1.DE — VERDICT

**PROPOSED — PENDING ADJUDICATION** — both benches land on *no purchase*: the specific idea we came to test (Symrise gets paid on the alcohol-free beverage boom) is dead on size, not on story; what is left is an ordinary good company at an ordinary full price, which deserves its own separate hearing rather than inheriting this one.

## What we believe

We came in believing Symrise is a "picks and shovels" way to own the alcohol-free drinks boom: when a brewer strips the alcohol out of a beer, it strips most of the taste out with it, so somebody has to sell the taste back — and Symrise is one of four companies in the world that does that at industrial scale.

After doing the chemistry and the arithmetic, here is what we actually believe:

1. **The physical problem is real and well documented.** Removing alcohol from a fermented drink destroys 81–92% of its aroma compounds by the gentle (membrane) method and 96–98% by the hot (vacuum) method ([peer-reviewed, wine matrix](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8708490/)). Alcohol is also the molecule that delivers warmth and body, and nothing recovered from the drink itself can replace it. That last piece genuinely is a chemistry purchase.
2. **But the brewer usually fixes it in its own plant, for free.** The equipment that removes the alcohol also captures the aroma and puts it back. A Technical University of Munich study measured this in beer: at the right settings, 67% of the key "banana" fruity ester is recovered and re-dosed from the plant's own condensate ([Foods, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8306714/)). A third route — special yeast that never makes alcohol in the first place — buys neither the equipment nor the flavour ([Foods, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12524115/)).
3. **The biggest customers have patented not needing us.** AB InBev holds a granted, live-to-2031 patent on making low-alcohol beer through the recipe and fermentation temperature ([EP2566944B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2566944B1/en)); Heineken has done the same through yeast genetics.
4. **So the size is small.** Our most aggressive, deliberately pro-thesis estimate puts alcohol-free-linked revenue at €19–50m, or 0.4–1.0% of a €4,929m company. Our neutral estimate is 0.05–0.78%. Either way it is an order of magnitude below the 2%-of-revenue bar we set before we started.
5. **Separately, and more interestingly:** the company itself looks like a decent, fairly-priced compounder that just accelerated — second-quarter organic growth of 4.5%, above the top of its own full-year guidance — while its accounting profit halved on write-downs. That is a different investment, at the same price, and we are explicitly *not* smuggling it in under this verdict.

## What the market believes

Seventeen analysts cover the stock. Consensus is positive, average price target €98.63 (+7.8% from €91.48), range €75–110. The two numbers everyone actually models are organic sales growth of 2–4% and an adjusted profit margin of 21.5–22.5% — which is the company's own guidance, restated.

Nobody publishes a number for alcohol-free exposure. There is no analyst note sizing it, because there is no disclosure to size it from. On the half-year call a JPMorgan analyst had to *ask* whether beverages had softened, and received no figure in reply ([call transcript](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-symrise-lifts-growth-momentum-in-h1-2026-93CH-4824649)).

So the market does not disagree with us about alcohol-free. It has simply never priced it either way — which is a very different thing from being wrong.

## Why we might have edge

Honestly stated, the edge we found is a *negative* one, and it is real:

- **We sized something nobody sized.** We built the exposure estimate from the ground up — end-use share of the flavour market, the beverage unit's plausible euro revenue, the alcohol-adjacent share within it, and the extra flavour dosing per litre. Two independent routes, both under 1%. No sell-side note contains this number. That is genuine original work, and its output is "don't buy."
- **We read the process literature instead of the marketing.** The finding that equipment and chemistry are *substitutes at the margin* — better membranes mean less purchased flavour — inverts the thesis. It also points somewhere useful: the equipment maker is the only layer paid on all three production routes.
- **We caught an accounting quality pattern.** Accounting net income ran €340m (2023) → €478m (2024) → €249m (2025), while the marketed "adjusted" figure was €513m. That is a serial pattern, not a one-off, and the company's stated way of monetising a hot adjacent category (pet health) was to buy it and then write €150m of it off.

What we do **not** have is an edge on the two variables that actually price this stock. We forecast the same growth and the same margin as everyone else.

## Why it might be priced in

Comprehensively:

- The stock is a DAX constituent with 17 analysts, 3.7% below its 52-week high and 41% above its 52-week low. There is no neglect here to exploit.
- Valuation, recomputed on the corrected share count (139.8m × €91.48 = €12.79bn market value, plus €2,088m of net debt including pensions and leases = **€14.88bn enterprise value ≈ 13.8× adjusted EBITDA, 24.4× accounting operating profit**, 22× forward earnings). That is a full price for 2–4% growth, but it is not an absurd one for a company of this quality. It is *fair*, and fair is not an opportunity.
- The alcohol-free trend itself is thoroughly public: no-alcohol beer volumes +8% in 2025 against −1% for beer overall, per the industry data vendor IWSR. Everyone knows. The question was never whether the trend is real; it was whether Symrise gets paid for it.

## What could go wrong

*This is the pre-mortem. We are proposing no position, so it runs in both directions: what kills us if we later enter at the reopen bands, and what the rejection itself costs us.*

**Failure narrative 1 — we reject the flavour thesis correctly and then buy the same stock two months later under a new name.** The most likely way this deck loses money is not the trade it declines; it is the trade it invites. "Symrise-the-compounder should be courted separately" is one sentence away from owning a €12.79bn stock at 13.8× because it accelerated for one quarter. Several of our own standing rules point the same way at once: quality-at-fair prices gets a small starter by default; a clean hearing with no named kill gets a minimum starter by default; and we allow ourselves to pay up modestly for things we already like. Each is individually reasonable and together they form a ratchet toward buying. *Tripwire: yes* — any SY1 order that appears without a fresh, separately-adjudicated compounder hearing is the failure, mechanically detectable.

**Failure narrative 2 — the input side eats the margin, exactly as it just did.** Symrise is backward-integrated into agricultural raw materials: vanilla from Madagascar, citrus terpenes, onion and garlic through Diana. It wrote down its own Terpene business by €148m in 2025. That is not a theoretical risk; it is the realised version of the risk, already on the tape once. *Tripwire: yes, but it is not armed* — a satellite crop-health read on the vanilla and citrus origins would front-run this by a season. Both benches flagged it as material. Neither ran it. That is our gap, not the company's.

**Failure narrative 3 — NO MONITORABLE TRIPWIRE.** A regulatory or safety re-evaluation removes or restricts a molecule family that carries real revenue — a flavour compound, a cooling agent, a masking flavanone. We cannot build a tripwire for this because we cannot enumerate the exposure: Symrise discloses nothing below the divisional line, so there is no mapping from any specific molecule to any specific euro of revenue. A European Commission or EFSA dossier would land on a chemical name we could not price. **Unknown-unknown class: undisclosed-input regulatory obsolescence** — the failure is invisible not because the event is rare but because the disclosure regime makes the exposure unenumerable in advance. A sibling class sits next to it, equally untrippable: **unfalsifiable-by-disclosure erosion** — if the largest customers quietly insource the flavour add-back, it shows up as slow weakness in a business-unit line that does not exist in any published document. We would learn about it years late, from a margin, not from a fact.

**The reflexive and structural leg.** Our own size is not the problem here and we should say so plainly rather than perform caution: a starter of 0.5–1.0% of a ~$3.4M deployable book is roughly $17–34k against a DAX constituent's daily volume — immaterial, no market impact, no flow to front-run, and no predicted-flow conflict. (We could not pull the actual average daily volume this session; the broker rail was closed. The conclusion is robust to any plausible value.) The real structural risks are three, and none of them is liquidity: (1) the doctrine ratchet in narrative 1; (2) potential double-ownership — the separately-managed Parametric sleeve is levered 159% and we have not checked whether it already holds Symrise or European specialty chemicals, which would both overstate a "new" position and create a wash-sale interaction at any future harvest; (3) the equipment substitution finding routes to GEA (G1A.DE) as a *separate* candidate, and there is a live risk we simply transfer the same enthusiasm one link up the chain without re-testing it from scratch.

**If this position loses money, the most likely reason will be that we rejected the alcohol-free thesis on its merits and then bought the identical stock at the identical price a quarter later as "the compounder," having let a one-quarter growth acceleration and three separate default-to-a-starter rules do the work that a fresh adversarial hearing was supposed to do.**
