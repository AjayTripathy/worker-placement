Before anything else — what this data actually is

Plain language, said straight.

This is a table. One row per carbon-offset project. Each row states, in tonnes, what the project claims to deliver each year, and what an independent satellite measurement observes at the project's own coordinates — and the gap between the two. Nothing here is an opinion or a letter grade. It is a measured number next to a claimed number.

The claim
The annual emission reduction the project registered with Verra, in tonnes of CO₂ per year. Pulled from the public Verra registry.
The observation
What a satellite actually recorded over the project's mapped boundary — forest lost, methane leaking, mangrove gained or lost — from three public, peer-reviewed data sources.
The gap
Observed divided by claimed. A project that says it avoids 1,000,000 tonnes but whose forest is being cut faster than a matched neighbour scores near zero, or negative.
The severity tier
A plain sort key — RED_FLAG (negative / near-zero delivery), SEVERE / MODERATE (partial), PASS (delivers most of the claim). The test is symmetric: projects can and do pass.
Reproducible
Same public inputs plus same published method version give the same number. No black box, no analyst discretion. You can re-run any row.

That is the whole product. Everything below is who buys the table, how it is built, what it does not yet cover, and what it costs to license.

How each number is measured

Three sensors, three peer-reviewed methods.

373

Forest — Hansen GFC

For each project's mapped boundary we compare its annual tree-cover loss to a buffer ring 5–30 km outside it — a matched-neighbour control, the approach of West et al. 2020 (PNAS). If the protected land is cut as fast as the unprotected ring, the avoidance claim has no basis. Hansen Global Forest Change v1.11, 30 m, 2001–2023.

13

Methane — Carbon Mapper

For landfill and gas projects we pull point-source methane plumes from the Carbon Mapper L4A catalog (Planet Tanager-1, NASA EMIT, aircraft) at the site and back out an implied capture rate. A project claiming 90% capture with a persistent multi-kilo-per-hour plume overhead is not capturing 90%.

17

Mangrove — GMW v3

The same buffer-ring logic on Global Mangrove Watch v3 extent tiles (25 m, Landsat + Sentinel-1), converted to tonnes at 350 tCO₂/ha (Donato et al. 2011). All 17 scored blue-carbon projects show effectively zero net mangrove gain against their coastal control. To our knowledge the first satellite-anchored screen of blue-carbon delivery.

Every method produces a measured number, not a pattern-recognition guess. The pipeline distinguishes real projects from failing ones: the landfill at ECOPARQUE Paulinia, Brazil, passes at an 87% implied capture rate even though three plumes were detected at the site. It is not a one-way flag generator.

The science this stands on

Peer-reviewed research proved the phenomenon. We industrialized the measurement.

The forest method is not ours to invent. Our scan_forest_v2.py explicitly implements the synthetic-control counterfactual of West et al. 2020, PNAS — "Overstated carbon emission reductions from voluntary REDD+ projects in the Brazilian Amazon" (West, Börner, Sills & Kontoleon), which matched 12 Brazilian Amazon REDD+ projects to controls and found the accepted crediting methodologies systematically overstate avoided deforestation. The follow-up, West et al. 2023, Science, extended the same approach to roughly 26 project sites across six countries in South America, Africa and Southeast Asia and found only a small fraction of the claimed reductions held up against the controls. That body of work underpinned the January-2023 Guardian / Die Zeit / SourceMaterial investigation of Verra rainforest credits — whose headline ">90% worthless" figure was the journalists' own derivation across the projects in the two studies, and which Verra and other researchers have contested on methodology.

The published studies established the effect at academic scale; this dataset turns the same test into an operational, ranked product. One row is deliberately marked as their advantage.

Why the trade is rational: covariate-matched controls do not scale to 400 projects without per-project analyst time — they are a research method, not a pipeline. The buffer ring is the scalable approximation, and it is weakest exactly where the finding is softest (marginal underdelivery). The RED_FLAG tier — a project deforesting faster than its own surrounding ring — is robust to the coarser control: no plausible covariate match rescues a project that is losing habitat faster than the unprotected land next to it.

Where this sits against the raters — honestly

The market has raters. It does not have a measured league table.

Sylvera, BeZero and Calyx Global publish analyst-driven letter grades (AAA–D), weighting governance, additionality, permanence and co-benefits differently, one project at a time, largely behind a subscription. Renoster leans most algorithmic. These are good products. Ours is a different kind of thing, and we will not pretend to replace their qualitative depth.

What the raters give you

A considered letter grade per project, blending physical, financial and governance risk, produced by analysts. Broad in what it weighs; qualitative; priced per report; not independently re-runnable by you.

What we give you

One measured number per project — observed vs. claimed — from public satellites, with the raw evidence attached and the method published. Narrow (delivery only), fully satellite-direct, numerical, reproducible, and it ranks every project, not a paywalled few.

Use us with a rater, not instead of one: we are the physical-delivery ground truth their carbon-delivery score can be checked against, at registry scale. The differentiator is not "better ratings" — it is measured, reproducible, complete-coverage evidence where the incumbents sell judgment on a subset.

What this data does NOT do — featured, not buried

The honest limits, on the page.

A buyer who trusts a dataset that oversells itself gets hurt later. Here is every material limitation we know of, stated before you ask.

Satellite coverage is opportunistic

Especially for methane: a plume appears only where a satellite happened to fly and a plume was present above the detection threshold. Absence of a plume is not proof of no emissions. 137 methane sites were surveyed with no detection — treat those as "not observed," not "clean." Multi-observation sites (e.g. La Yesca, 11 detections) are the robust signals; single-observation flags are preliminary.

Mangrove is a small sample

17 blue-carbon projects scored. The May write-up cited 7 of 17 after KML failures; parser hardening has since recovered all 17 — but 17 is still a small universe, and GMW's 25 m annual grids can miss fine-grained restoration. Read the mangrove finding as a strong first signal, not a settled census.

The buffer-ring counterfactual has critics

Matched-neighbour controls are the peer-reviewed standard (West 2020), but they have known failure modes: leakage displacement (deforestation pushed into the ring makes the project look better than it is) and matched-control validity (the ring may not be a true like-for-like). Where deforestation actually increased under a project, no counterfactual recovers the claim; where it merely fell short, the debate is legitimately open.

Data vintage is May 2026

The scan reflects Hansen v1.11 (through 2023), the Carbon Mapper catalog and GMW v3 as of May 2026. Forest loss, plumes and registry status move. A fresh scan to the current data is a scoped line item in any paid engagement — not an afterthought — and re-runs are cheap by design.

The probability-of-invalidation model — what it honestly is

calibrate_probability.py trains a logistic regression on Verra Withdrawn / Rejected vs. Registered outcomes to output P(invalidation) over 1–10 year horizons for 322 projects. Validation is candid: the model is dominated by demographic features — country, sub-category, age, claim size — not by the satellite divergence signal, because most invalidated projects also failed the satellite scan (parse errors, tile timeouts), so divergence and invalidation barely co-occur in the training set. It still ranks sensibly — all four publicly-known scandal projects score 78–82% P(5yr) — but for "this project resembles ones that got rejected" reasons, not satellite evidence. We sell the satellite findings as the unique signal and offer the model only as corroboration or a fallback where no satellite coverage exists. We do not lead with it.

What could make this wrong

The risks to the data itself — not in the footnotes.

  • A flag is a signal to investigate, not a verdict of fraud. Every flagged project deserves review against its PDD baseline, monitoring reports and developer response before any public conclusion. The dataset routes attention; it does not adjudicate.
  • Boundary geometry can be wrong at the source. Some Verra KMLs ship giant envelope rectangles or degenerate polygons; we filter polygons over 5M ha as artifacts and flag every case, but a mis-mapped boundary can move a score. Records carry their scan status so you can see which are clean.
  • Generic carbon stock (165 tCO₂/ha forest) is a mid-tropical estimate. Primary rainforest is higher — which would worsen, not soften, the gap on red-flag projects. The direction of that error favours the projects, not our finding.
  • Coverage is partial and grows with parser work. 403 projects are satellite-direct today; KML-parse failures, tile timeouts and Carbon Mapper's Asia gap (needs TROPOMI Sentinel-5P) leave the rest unscored. We report the exact failure reason per project rather than silently dropping it.
  • The vintage is May 2026. A live buyer gets a fresh scan; a stale table can re-flag a resolved project or miss a newly-degraded one. Freshness is a scoped deliverable, not an assumption.
