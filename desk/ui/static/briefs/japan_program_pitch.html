Before the thesis — what this program actually is

Said straight, in one breath.

This is a repeatable pipeline, not a stock tip. It reads Japan's audited corporate filings directly from the regulator's database, ranks every company trading below the cash-and-assets it owns, then runs each survivor through a verification gauntlet that kills value traps and a mock courtroom that argues both sides before a single share is bought. It is 36 hours old. Everything in this document is process and a first, deliberately tiny book — there is zero realized profit or loss yet. We are pitching the machine and the discipline, not a track record.

Our edge is not stock-picking genius. It is three narrower, real things: (a) primary-source data plumbing no retail investor runs — we pull the filings ourselves, from the source, for free; (b) a verification pipeline that kills traps at roughly 70–90%; and (c) being small enough to own ¥3–130 billion companies that no large fund can be bothered to cover. The capacity ceiling is small by design — that smallness is the edge.

The thesis — the forcing function

A whole market is being made to give the cash back.

For decades, Japanese companies hoarded cash and traded below the value of what they owned, and nothing changed it. That is now changing, on the record, driven by policy — and the change is a live mechanism, not a slogan.

The discount is closing, measurably

The Tokyo Stock Exchange now requires companies trading below book to publish an improvement plan — how they will fix their capital efficiency, in writing. The share of the Prime market below book has fallen from roughly half to about a quarter. That is a forcing function converting discounts into dividends, tender offers and buybacks — and it is happening now.

The parent-child untangling

Japan is dismantling its listed-subsidiary web: government takeover guidelines and pressure on parents have driven the count of listed subsidiaries from over 500 toward 179. Each unwind is a control event that must pay the minority holders — the exact event we position ahead of, on names where a large holder blocks a cheap exit.

The prize is real. The catch, stated up front and featured throughout this document: the forcing function only reaches part of the shelf directly — and for a US taxpayer, a tax rule quietly removes the cash-richest half. Both are the subject of the sections that follow. We lead with the constraints, not the pitch.

The build, in numbers

We pull the filings ourselves. From the source. For free.

Japan's regulator (the FSA) runs EDINET — its equivalent of the SEC's EDGAR — and every listed company files its audited annual report there as structured, machine-readable data. The catch: the direct programming interface needs a subscription key, and our sign-up stalled when an email verification code was unreachable mid-flow. So we did the thing that made the key unnecessary.

The gate that halved the shelf

For a US taxpayer, the cheapness and the tax poison are the same number.

This is the single most important constraint in the program, and we feature it rather than bury it. US tax law has a punitive regime — PFIC, the Passive Foreign Investment Company rules — for foreign companies that are more than half "passive assets." A company gets tested on the market value of its assets. And here is the trap: a cheap Japanese cash-box is passive by construction, because the discount itself shrinks the market-value denominator the test runs on. The cheaper it looks, the more likely it poisons your taxes. Averaging down mechanically makes it worse.

The worked example — Daishin Chemical (4629), the best name in the first batch

Daishin is a genuinely excellent business: Japan's #1 paint-thinner maker, zero debt, a pension in surplus, and cash of ¥9.34 billion against a market cap of ¥8.7 billion — you are paid to take the operating company. A serial accumulator (Hikari Tsushin) has been buying relentlessly, 5.0% → 7.48%. Everything about it says "buy." But on the market-value asset test, passive assets are roughly ¥17.4 billion against a total that puts the passive share at 58% — over the line. It fails PFIC. For our US-taxable book it is tax-gated: court deferred, not convened. Its batch-mate Eidai (7877), the other top name, fails the same way at 54%. The two statistically best names on the shelf are the two the tax code removes.

52% of our cheap top-120 fails this test. The ownable universe — the names that pass on a market-value basis and can be traded — is 57 non-PFIC names. That is the real menu. The names in the rest of this deck all clear the gate; the near-line passers (45–50%) carry an annual re-test and an explicit "do not average down through the line" rule, because a 15% price drop tips them over.

The verification law we derived

Cheap without a named actor is dead money — by design.

We ran three verification batches, verdicting 23 names one at a time against Japanese-language holder records, 5%-rule filings, and the primary financial statements. A single repeatable rule fell out, and it now governs the pipeline: on the second-tier "Standard" segment, cheapness with no named actor — a parent event, an activist on the register, a family-succession trigger — never closes. The TSE improvement-plan forcing function reaches the top-tier "Prime" segment directly; below it, something specific has to force the outcome, or the discount simply persists for another decade. The court record — reversals in both directions

We argue both sides before we buy. The court has overruled us both ways.

Four names that survived the gauntlet went to a Stage-2 deep dive, then to a mock court: an adversarial red bench built the strongest case against, a blue bench the case for, and a presiding view adjudicated. The point is credibility — a process that only ever confirms the analyst's favorite is theater. Ours killed its own favorite and promoted two names it had merely been watching.

Killed — the deep-dive's own favorite

Tachi-S (7239)  ·  struck down on arithmetic

The deep dive loved it at "4× earnings." The red bench found the 4× double-counted the cash — it credited the company's pre-deal net cash and the ¥21.8 billion Toyo Seat acquisition that consumed it. On a clean pro-forma basis it is ~6× current, above its closest peer, with no verified activist on the register. Verdict: killed the starter. It re-opens only below 5× on clean pro-forma math, or if a named actor appears. This is the single most important entry in the record: the machine overruled the person who built the case.

Promoted from watch — bounded carry

Meiji Electric (3388)  ·  a hard dividend floor on a Prime name

The only Prime name in the batches — so the forcing function applies directly, and no activist is needed. The blue bench's decisive fact, verbatim from the filing: an ¥88 dividend floor held through FY2027 — a hard, written commitment. At the entry price that is a 4.4% floored yield at 0.74× book, and the run-rate cash flow covers the floor about four times over. This is not a moonshot; it is paid, bounded, verified carry. Promoted to a starter buy.

Promoted from watch — special situation

Nissan Shatai (7222)  ·  a structural price floor under parent distress

Nissan owns exactly 50%; a long-term holder, Effissimo, owns 22.59% and has for ~15 years. To squeeze out minorities cheaply, the parent needs a two-thirds vote it cannot reach without paying Effissimo's price — a structural floor under the stock at any turnout up to 67.8%. Meanwhile the parent is distressed and needs either cash (sell its 50% into a change-of-control tender) or simplification (buy the minorities in). Precedent is on the minority's side: every forced resolution of this kind has paid them +13% / +21% / +26%. Promoted — but half-size, for the reason in the next block.

Affirmed a gate — the exit was a mirage

Endo Lighting (6932)  ·  buy only at the insider's own strike

The bull case was a "contracted buy-out exit." The court read the contract text and found it is really the founder vehicle's option to launch a tender at a trailing average price with zero premium — the private-equity holder's own conversion strike, not a windfall for us. So the gate holds: we enter only at ¥2,230, that strike, to the yen — currently ~11% below the market. At today's ¥2,526 you would be paying 12.6% over the insider's price for an exit that isn't contracted. No chase.

Concessions — featured, not buried

Everything that could make this wrong, on the page.

A program that oversells itself gets the reader hurt later. Here is every material limitation we know of, before you ask.

Thin liquidity — a permanent cap

Several names trade only ¥1–5 million a day. Positions in them are permanently size-capped; a $10–20k line can take days to build. This is the flip side of the small-cap edge, and it never goes away.

Zero carry on Nissan Shatai while we wait

The parent voted the dividend into suspension. We collect nothing on that position until the event resolves — pure event risk, no coupon to pay us for the wait. We accepted this consciously, and it is why the line is half-size.

The 15-year-stalemate precedent

Effissimo has held its Nissan Shatai block since roughly 2011 without forcing a resolution. Our 55% within-24-months call may be too optimistic — a large, patient holder is exactly what can make an event never arrive. The precedent cuts against us and we count it.

The tax gate removed the two best names

Daishin and Eidai — statistically the strongest names on the shelf — are unavailable to a US-taxable book. The ownable universe is genuinely worse than the raw screen, and any figure quoting the "best" of the shelf must carry that asterisk.

Yen unhedged

Every position is in yen and unhedged. A yen move can swamp a small value gain in dollar terms. We treat this as a deliberate, uncompensated exposure to be sized around, not an edge.

Screen prices go stale between crawl and dispatch

Two names in the batches had already re-rated before we finished verifying them (Yagi 2.7×; Nakano to ~1.0× book). We now tape-verify at dispatch — but it is a real, recurring latency cost, logged as such.

Filing vintage is FY-March-2026

The fundamentals are the audited year to 31 March 2026. Balance sheets, holder registers and guidance move. A live entry re-checks the tape and the latest disclosures; a stale figure can mis-state cheapness in either direction.

The courts are single benches, not ensembles

Each verdict is one red bench and one blue bench, not a distribution of them. It is a disciplined argument, not a statistical proof. We escalate the load-bearing ones to a stronger review, but the sample is one.

What this proves for the broader program

The reusable part is the machine, not any one trade.

Japan is the third shelf to run through the same three stages — screen, then trap catalog, then court — after the US and Korea, with Europe's filings next. The value compounds even when no trade results, because the knowledge accumulates in a reusable graph.

One pipeline, four shelves

Screen → trap catalog → court now runs on the US, Korea and Japan. Europe (ESEF filings) is next — one filing-taxonomy adapter serves several countries. The adapter pattern is the product.

The knowledge outlasts the trade

Every batch adds to a durable catalog: the trap species, the distribution of what forced resolutions pay minorities, and the PFIC-market-value law. A killed name still teaches the machine something reusable.

Replication at zero fee is the point

Doing an active manager's primary-source research on names too small for them to cover — at no fee — is itself the deliverable. Alpha is a hoped-for overlay; the diligence replication is the floor.
