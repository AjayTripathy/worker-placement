# California Municipal Bond Portfolio — Option B (Disciplined Higher-Yield)

Ladder 2035–2045 · Prepared 2026-06-09 · All pricing EMMA-verified

---

## 1. Executive summary

Option B is a 24-bond, $1,000,000-par portfolio of California **school-district general-obligation (GO) bonds** — bonds backed by a statutory first lien on an *unlimited* ad-valorem property-tax levy on every taxable parcel in the district (California SB-222). The district must raise the tax rate as high as debt service requires; there is no cap. Every bond was checked against the MSRB's EMMA system to confirm it is a true voter-approved GO and not a look-alike structure.

**The pitch in one paragraph.** This portfolio yields **about 9% on an after-tax-equivalent basis** (9.03% taxable-equivalent yield, net of the de-minimis tax drag) from bonds that are AA-grade safe and maximally insulated from an AI/tech-driven downturn. The yield is **not a reward for taking more credit risk** — it is a reward for accepting *less liquidity*. These are small, obscure school districts whose bonds trade by dealer quote, not on a screen, and 22 of the 24 are deep-discount bonds the market has left cheap. Versus the more liquid AA portfolio (Option A), Option B yields *more* after tax (9.03% vs 7.65%) while scoring *higher* on AI-insulation (99 vs 92) and *higher* on creditworthiness (90 vs 82). The extra yield came from going down in liquidity and obscurity — **not** down in quality. Mispriced-safe, not mispriced-risky.

## 2. Methodology — how the portfolio was built

Two screens were applied to a universe of California municipal bonds, then a verification gate, then the yield was sourced *on-thesis*.

**Screen 1 — AI-insulation.** We measured each issuer's credit sensitivity to a sharp AI/technology-sector downturn. The mechanism we care about runs through the state budget: California's general fund leans heavily on capital-gains income tax, which collapses when tech equity markets crash (the dot-com bust cut that revenue line by roughly 70%, and the state's own GO rating fell several notches). Bonds repaid from a *local property-tax* levy — like school-district GOs — are structurally walled off from that channel: property is assessed and taxed regardless of what the Nasdaq does. Every bond here scores **99.2** on insulation because property-tax-secured school GOs do not touch the capital-gains revenue line at all.

**Screen 2 — verified creditworthiness.** We required an independently strong credit profile (score **90**), driven by the unlimited-tax GO security itself: a statutory first lien on an uncapped property-tax levy, broad and diversified tax bases, and the essential-public-purpose nature of K-12 and community-college districts. This is AA-grade credit substance, sized to the security, not to the size or fame of the issuer.

**The verification gate — what EMMA *excluded*.** A high stated yield on a "California school" bond is often a trap: the name says school but the *security* is something weaker. We pulled the official security description for every candidate from EMMA and **rejected anything that was not a true unlimited-tax GO**:

- **Certificates of participation (COPs)** and **lease-revenue bonds** — repaid only if the district keeps appropriating lease payments; subject to abatement and non-appropriation, no first lien on taxes. Rejected.
- **Community Facilities District / Mello-Roos bonds** — secured by a special tax on a *narrow, often undeveloped* parcel set; far weaker and more volatile than a districtwide ad-valorem GO. Rejected.
- **Tax-increment / former-redevelopment bonds** — repaid from incremental assessed value, which is exactly the cyclical, growth-dependent revenue we are trying to avoid. Rejected.

Only bonds that came back from EMMA as genuine voter-approved, unlimited-ad-valorem GOs were kept. That filter is why the book is 100% school-GO and why the credit and insulation scores are uniform across all 24 names.

**How the extra yield was sourced — on-thesis.** Having locked the *quality* screens, we then deliberately reached for yield **only along the liquidity/obscurity axis**, never the credit axis:

- **Small, infrequently-traded districts** the large funds skip because the positions are too small to matter to them.
- **Deep-discount, low-coupon bonds** (3.00% coupons priced in the high-70s to low-80s) that the retail market shuns because of the de-minimis tax mechanics (Section 3) — even though the *credit* is identical to a par bond from the same issuer.

The result: the same AA-grade, fully-insulated credit, bought at a discount and a yield concession the market demands for illiquidity. That concession is the entire source of the pickup over Option A.

## 3. Yield

**Headline: ~9.03% after-tax taxable-equivalent yield.** Walk-through:

| Step | Rate | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Yield to worst | **4.94%** | The actual par-weighted yield earned on the bonds, tax-exempt. |
| Taxable-equivalent yield — gross | **9.93%** | What a *fully taxable* bond would have to yield to match, grossing up at California's top combined bracket (≈2.01×). This is the upper bound — it assumes every dollar of return is tax-exempt. |
| **Taxable-equivalent yield — after de-minimis** | **9.03%** | The honest figure. It nets out the federal ordinary-income tax owed on the discount accretion of the deep-discount bonds (see below). Use this number. |

The 0.90-percentage-point gap between 9.93% and 9.03% is **not a haircut for credit or fees — it is the de-minimis tax claw-back, and we are showing it explicitly rather than quoting the flattering 9.93%.**

**The de-minimis explanation (told straight).** When a municipal bond is bought at a discount larger than a small statutory threshold (roughly ¼ point per year to maturity), the IRS treats the *accretion of that discount back toward par* as **ordinary income**, not tax-free muni interest and not lower-taxed capital gain. **22 of the 24 bonds in this book breach the de-minimis threshold** — by design, because deep discounts are exactly where the illiquidity yield concession lives. So a portion of each of those bonds' return is taxed at ordinary rates. The 9.03% after-tax figure already reflects that drag bond-by-bond; the 9.93% gross figure does not. The two bonds that do *not* breach (the 5.105% of 2035 and the 5.32% of 2036, both priced near par) carry no claw-back, which is why their after-tax TEY equals their gross.

## 5. Option A vs Option B

Same two quality screens (AI-insulation and verified creditworthiness) were run on both. The only difference is *where on the liquidity spectrum* each portfolio sits.

| | **Option A** (AA-liquid) | **Option B** (school-GO higher-yield) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Liquid, large-issuer, AA-rated CA munis | Small-district CA school GOs, deep discounts |
| After-tax TEY | 7.65% | **9.03%** |
| AI-insulation | 92 | **99** |
| Creditworthiness | 82 | **90** |
| Liquidity / tradeability | **High** — screen-tradable, tight markets | Lower — dealer/RFQ, wider spreads |
| Best for | Investors who may need to sell early | Buy-and-hold to the ladder |

**The key point:** Option B is not a "step down in quality for more yield." On both quality screens it scores *higher* than Option A. The roughly 1.4-point after-tax yield pickup is paid for entirely by **giving up liquidity and tradeability** — accepting bonds that are harder to buy and sell and quoted by dealers rather than on a screen. If you can hold to the ladder, you are paid ~1.4 points a year, after tax, to do so. If you might need to exit early, Option A's tighter markets are worth the lower yield.

## 6. What could make this wrong

Stated prominently and honestly — these are the real ways Option B disappoints.

- **Liquidity is the central risk.** This is 24 small school districts in deep-discount, low-coupon bonds. They do **not** trade on a screen; buying and selling is by dealer quote / RFQ, spreads are wide, and a forced sale in a stressed market could cost real money versus the marks shown. **4 of the 24 bonds have stale marks** (last reported trade more than ~6 months ago: 345102LT2, 932889YC6, 219764SF5, 275282QD6) — those prices may not reflect where the bond would actually execute today. Treat this as a **buy-and-hold-to-maturity** book, not a tradeable one. The entire yield advantage *is* this illiquidity premium; you are being paid for it, but you are genuinely bearing it.

- **De-minimis tax covers most of the book.** 22 of 24 bonds breach the de-minimis threshold, so a portion of return is taxed as ordinary income. We have already netted this into the 9.03% after-tax figure, but if an investor's marginal federal rate is higher than assumed, the after-tax yield is lower than shown. Do not quote the 9.93% gross figure to a taxable investor as if it were achievable after tax.

- **Single-sector concentration.** 100% California school-district GO. There is no sector diversification: a statewide shock to California K-12 finance, Proposition-13 assessment mechanics, or the SB-222 GO lien framework would hit every holding at once. The uniform insulation/credit scores are a feature of the screen but also mean the names are highly correlated.

- **Duration and deep-discount price sensitivity.** A ladder out to 2045 of low-coupon, deep-discount bonds is *more* rate-sensitive than a par bond of the same maturity — deep discounts have longer effective duration. If rates rise, these marks fall harder than a comparable current-coupon book. Held to maturity you still collect par; sold early in a higher-rate environment you could realize a meaningful loss.

- **Rate-regime risk — read the insulation score correctly.** The "99 insulation" is a statement about **credit** — these bonds' ability to *pay* is walled off from an AI/tech crash because they are repaid by local property taxes, not state capital-gains revenue. It is **not** a statement that the *price* is insulated. In a general interest-rate selloff, or a muni-market liquidity event, these bonds can and will mark down regardless of how safe the credit is. Insulation protects the coupon and principal you collect at maturity; it does not protect the interim mark.
